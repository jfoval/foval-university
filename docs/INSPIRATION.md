# Inspiration: Peterson Academy

Research notes on Peterson Academy (petersonacademy.com), compiled September 2026 as a reference point for Foval University. Foval is free, aims for strict political and ideological neutrality, and wants a general-education progression that also covers practical life skills. Peterson Academy is a paid, for-profit platform with a stated ideological purpose, so it is a useful comparison rather than a template.

## 1. Mission and pedagogical approach

Peterson Academy launched in September 2024, founded by psychologist Jordan Peterson and his daughter Mikhaila Fuller (CEO), under the parent company Luminate Enterprises Ltd. The founders say they invested about $3 million of seed money.

In their own words:

- Tagline: "How to Think, Not What to Think."
- Homepage: "a global academy dedicated to intellectual freedom led by distinguished professors from Harvard, Oxford, MIT, Cambridge, and other great institutions."
- Social bio: "We're on a mission to stop indoctrination. Higher education at 1% of the price. Let's take education back."
- Fuller (True North interview, Oct 2024): the goal is to "make top-tier education available internationally to everyone regardless of their background and financial situation," and to reduce the cost of a degree by roughly 95%. On accreditation: "degrees are becoming less and less relevant so we're not focused on accreditation," and the academy will not "lower the quality of our education to fit into an archaic mold."
- Instructors are told they have "absolute intellectual autonomy" (homepage quote).

The pedagogical model is lecture-first and self-paced: "Learn anywhere. Speed up or slow down. Watch on your laptop, or listen like a podcast on your phone." There are no live classes, no office hours, no deadlines, and no required readings. Peterson has proposed a direct-to-employer model in which certificates would signal conscientiousness and cognitive ability rather than accredited credit. Fuller has said about half of enrolled students use the platform for continuing education rather than any degree-like goal.

## 2. Courses offered

Observations on the mix: philosophy, psychology and neuroscience dominate; there is no computer science, programming, AI, foreign language, chemistry, or engineering; practical courses are limited to writing, personal planning, and finance/economics. Several instructors teach many courses (Hicks 9, Orr 9, Peterson 6, Jalal 5, Andreyev 5, Brook 5, Kaufmann 4, Posner 4).

## 3. Course structure

- **Length.** The standard unit is an "eight-hour course." Catalogue data shows total durations from 4.6 to 11.8 hours (mean 8.05 hours), split into 6 to 12 lectures (mean 7.7, most commonly 8). Individual lectures run roughly 45 to 75 minutes.
- **Lecture format.** Pre-recorded, high-production studio lectures, filmed on a minimalist set (Slate's Luke Winkie described it as a "white, CGI-rendered void"). Talking-head with occasional graphics; no slides-heavy or screen-cast style. Playback supports speed control, chapters, picture-in-picture, and audio-only mode.
- **Assessments.** Every lecture carries an optional multiple-choice quiz (840 of 842 lectures in the catalogue data have a quiz flag; reviewers describe 10 questions with immediate feedback). Quizzes were initially AI-generated. Course final exams with answer explanations were added to the app in 2025-26. There are no essays, graded assignments, or instructor feedback; Fuller has mentioned optional essays as a future feature.
- **Readings.** No required readings. Some instructors publish their own syllabi with primary texts (for example Stephen Hicks' eight-lecture Philosophy of Ethics syllabus, which lists Kant, Mill, Nietzsche and others), and the platform lists recommended books that students buy separately.
- **Progression.** Course pages list "prerequisite" and "follow-up" courses, forming informal chains (for example Intro to Neuroscience leads to the Neuroscience of Dreams, Love, and Human Nature). In 2026 the app added "the official Peterson Academy curriculum, organized by semester with required coursework and electives," where students "view your standing by term, and receive semester credentials on your profile as you complete each stage." Earlier reporting (DeSmog, citing Peterson) described a planned unaccredited "general education degree" requiring 90 courses.
- **Certificates.** No accredited degree or credit. Semester credentials appear on the in-app profile; certificates of completion tied to passing tests have been announced but reviewers through 2026 describe them as still rolling out.
- **AI features.** "Lecture Bot" ("Ask"): students ask questions about any lecture and get answers grounded in the lecture with tappable timestamps that jump to the relevant moment.
- **Community.** A social feed called "The Quad" with profiles, posts, reposts, lecture clips, and timestamp-linked comments; a global "student map"; and, before January 2025, a downvote button (since removed). Students organised their own book clubs, essay clubs, "Poetry Sundays", a student podcast and a nightly gazette. Direct messaging, study groups and tagging were announced as planned features.

## 4. Pricing and platform

- **Price history.** $499/year at launch (Sept 2024); increased to $599/year on 23 January 2025 (justified as funding four course releases a month instead of three); reduced to $399/year by spring 2025, which is the current list price, with a 7-day money-back guarantee, annual auto-renewal, and periodic sales (a Labor Day 2026 promotion advertised savings "up to $1776"). Veteran discounts are mentioned by third parties. Early plans quoted $40/month.
- **Access.** Open enrolment, no application, no prerequisites.
- **Platforms.** Web app, iOS (iOS 16+, about 150 MB, rated 4.8/5 from 137 ratings on the US App Store) and Android. The apps offer offline downloads, audio-only mode when the phone locks, picture-in-picture, video chapters, "coming soon" course trailers, and progress tracking. Users have noted that background audio playback was missing in early versions and that downloaded lectures sometimes stay in "continue watching."
- **Status.** The platform has described itself as in public beta since launch and still did as of March 2026.

## 5. What critics and fans say

**Positive (fans and reviewers).**
- Production quality and lecture clarity are widely praised; the Technology for Learners review called the courses "thought-provoking and well-structured" with "high-quality production."
- Price relative to university tuition is the most repeated selling point (the Matt Walsh Blog review and Modern Adult Substack both call it a bargain).
- The audio/offline mobile experience and the ability to "listen like a podcast" are cited as a differentiator.
- Even critical writers note community enthusiasm: Winkie (Slate) praised students' "life-giving understanding that one can always become sharper and better," and expelled students described a lively, mutually encouraging peer culture.

**Criticisms of pedagogy and delivery.**
- Reviewers (Technology for Learners, Learn.org, Matt Walsh Blog) note the absence of accreditation, transferable credit, graded work, professor feedback, and live discussion, and warn it cannot replace training for licensed professions.
- Modern Adult Substack: "Right now there isn't a curriculum to follow," recommended readings lack syllabi, and the social feed is "very one-dimensional." (The semester curriculum has since been added.)
- Wikipedia's summary of press coverage: lacks syllabi, required readings, office hours and live discussion.
- Rude Vulture (Oswin Vex) characterised it as "a basic video streaming service masquerading as a university" and a case of "overpromising and underdelivering" on exams, certificates and group features.
- OpenSource Temple argued the lectures contain rhetoric that "sound[s] like [it] might be saying something" without specific content, criticised individual instructors' credentials and past statements, and recommended Khan Academy instead.
- The Better Business Bureau lists a C rating with unresolved complaints about accreditation claims and lack of recourse.

**Criticisms of political slant (attributed).**
- Slate (Luke Winkie, Oct 2024) described the launch curriculum as narrow, focused on culture-war topics, with instructors sharing Peterson's viewpoint and few STEM courses.
- Wikipedia's reception summary: some observers question the claim of ideological neutrality, pointing to course selection that reflects Peterson's worldview (for example "Deconstructing Decolonization," "The Dark Side of Marxism," and multiple courses from Objectivist and conservative institutions).
- DeSmog flags the involvement of climate-sceptic Bjorn Lomborg and a social-media post citing Harvard's vaccination policy as "Reason #2 to go to Peterson Academy."
- Supporters frame the same facts differently: Fuller and the marketing position the academy as a corrective to "indoctrination" in universities, and reviewer-fans value curricula "free from progressive ideology" (Meandering Minstrel).

**Governance controversy.** On 8 January 2025 the academy removed several paying students, without warning and before a code of conduct existed, after they criticised the price increase on the social feed; Slate's Winkie had earlier been removed for contacting students for an article, and the Toronto Star (Alex Boyd, March 2025) reported its journalist was also banned. Expelled students (Dusty May Taylor, "Frankie" at Meandering Minstrel) said they were refunded but lost coursework and contacts, and described the CEO telling complainants to get a better job or work harder. The Toronto Star and others called this ironic given Peterson's free-speech advocacy; the academy subsequently published a code of conduct and removed the downvote feature.

## 6. What Foval could borrow, and what to do differently

**Worth borrowing**
- The fixed "eight-hour course" unit of 6 to 10 lectures: substantive, finishable, and legible in a catalogue.
- Per-lecture 10-question quizzes with instant explanations, plus an optional course final.
- A lecture-grounded "Ask" assistant with timestamp jumps; Foval can also use it to teach critical use of AI.
- Prerequisite/follow-up chains on every course page, and a semester curriculum with core, electives and visible "standing by term."
- Audio-only, offline and speed-controlled playback; the podcast use case is the most praised platform feature.
- Course families (Intro plus three or four follow-ups by the same instructor), coming-soon trailers, and a public monthly release cadence.
- Instructor syllabi with primary readings (the Hicks model), integrated into the course page.

**Do differently**
- Neutrality by design. Peterson Academy's course list is the main evidence critics cite for slant. For contested subjects, recruit instructors across viewpoints or present opposing positions within one course, and publish an editorial-review standard.
- Publish a code of conduct, moderation process and appeal route before opening any community feature; the January 2025 expulsions happened in their absence.
- Fill the gaps critics name: computer science, statistics beyond Part 1, languages, chemistry, and the practical strand (business, sales, personal finance, survival, health, using AI). Peterson Academy has finance and economics but no sales, first aid or applied technology.
- Add assessment beyond multiple choice; every reviewer names the lack of feedback as the biggest weakness.
- Use free readings so the "$50-95 of books per course" complaint does not apply.
- Avoid single-instructor and founder dependence if neutrality is the goal.

## Recommendations for Foval

- Adopt the 8-hour, 8-lecture course as the standard unit, with a 45-75 minute lecture length and total-hours shown on every card.
- Ship per-lecture quizzes, a course final, and a lecture-grounded AI "Ask" with timestamps in the first release.
- Structure the catalogue as a semester curriculum (core plus electives) with prerequisite and follow-up links on every course page and a visible "standing by term."
- Build audio-only, offline download and playback-speed support into the web and mobile apps from launch.
- Commission course sequences (Intro plus three or four follow-ups) rather than one-off courses, but cap any single instructor at a small share of the core.
- Write and publish a viewpoint-balance policy for contested subjects, and have a small editorial board review every course against it before release.
- Publish community rules, moderation and appeal procedures before turning on any social feature; log moderation decisions.
- Prioritise the gaps: statistics, computer science, languages, chemistry, and the practical strand (personal finance, sales, business basics, first aid and survival, health literacy, using AI).
- Add written and project-based assessment with rubric grading and peer review, plus a per-semester capstone.
- Issue free, verifiable completion certificates and state plainly that they are not accredited credit.
- Use only free, open-licensed or public-domain readings, embedded in the course page.
- Keep a public roadmap and changelog instead of an indefinite "beta" label.
