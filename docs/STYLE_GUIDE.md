# Foval Style Guide: How We Write

Read this before you draft or edit any lesson. It is short on purpose. If a lesson breaks it, the lesson is not done.

## The one idea

Write like a good professor talking to one student across a table. Not lecturing to a hall. Not writing a textbook. Not writing a blog post. Talking. You know the subject cold, you like this person, and you want them to get it.

That professor does a few things naturally. They say the main point plainly. They give you an example before the rule. They tell you what people usually get wrong. They pause and ask "does that make sense?" and actually wait. They admit when something is unsettled. They never try to sound impressive, because they don't need to.

Everything below is just that idea, spelled out.

## What it sounds like

**Plain words.** Use the word a person would say out loud. "Use" not "utilise". "Start" not "commence". "About" not "approximately". If a technical term is needed, use it, then say what it means in the next sentence.

**Short sentences, mostly.** Fifteen to twenty words on average. A long sentence now and then is fine when the idea needs it. A long sentence every time is a wall. (This is about sentences, not lessons. A lesson is as long as the teaching needs; see the standards, 1.3.)

**You and I.** Second person throughout. "You'll notice", "try this", "here's the part people miss". The lesson can say "I" when it is speaking as the teacher: "I'd start with the second method."

**Examples first.** Show the thing, then name it. A learner who has seen two concrete cases understands the abstract rule on first reading. A learner who gets the rule first has to hold it in their head with nothing attached.

**Say what people get wrong.** Every real teacher knows the mistakes their students make. Name them: "Most people assume X here. It's actually Y, and here's why the assumption feels right."

**Admit the limits.** "We don't fully know why." "The evidence here is weaker than the confident advice online." "Researchers still argue about this." Those sentences build trust. A lesson that is certain about everything is lying about something.

**Contractions.** Don't, it's, you'll, here's. Their absence is the fastest way to sound like a manual.

**Warmth without gush.** The reader is an adult who chose to be here. Respect that. No "Great job!", no "You've got this!", no exclamation marks doing the work a sentence should.

## What it never sounds like

These are the fingerprints of machine-written text. Any one of them makes a careful reader stop trusting the page.

**Em dashes.** None. Not one. Use a comma, a full stop, a colon, or parentheses.

**The AI vocabulary.** Never: delve, dive in, dive into, unlock, unleash, harness, leverage, empower, elevate, transform, transformative, game-changer, journey, landscape, realm, tapestry, testament, navigate (unless about maps), robust, seamless, cutting-edge, in today's fast-paced world, in the ever-evolving, at the end of the day, it's worth noting, it's important to note, importantly, crucially, notably, essentially, ultimately, a testament to, plays a crucial role, serves as, stands as, a myriad of, a plethora of, foster, facilitate, underscore, highlight (as a verb for "show"), showcase, embark, elevate, resonate, nuanced, multifaceted, holistic, synergy, paradigm.

**The AI sentence shapes.**
- "It's not just X, it's Y." / "Not only X but also Y." Say Y.
- Triads for rhythm: "faster, cheaper, and better" when two would do or one is the real point. Lists of three because three sounds nice are padding.
- "Here's the thing:" / "Here's the kicker:" / "Let's break this down." / "Let's unpack that."
- Opening with a rhetorical question the lesson then answers. Once per lesson at most, and only if a real student would ask it.
- "In this lesson, we will..." / "By the end of this lesson, you will..." Just start.
- "In conclusion" / "To sum up" / "In summary". If a summary is needed, write it without announcing it.
- Ending a paragraph with a punchy one-line moral. "And that changes everything." "That's the whole game." Once in a while it lands. Every paragraph, it's a tic.
- Sentences that restate the previous sentence with more drama.
- "Whether you're a student, a professional, or just curious..." Nobody is addressed by a list of everyone.

**Salesy talk.** No promising outcomes ("you'll never forget a name again"). No "secret", "hack", "trick", "the one thing". No urgency. The material is good; it doesn't need selling.

**Fact-stating without teaching.** A paragraph that is a list of true sentences is not a lesson. Each fact needs a reason it's there: what it explains, what it lets you do, what it rules out. If you can't say why the reader needs this sentence, cut it.

**Hedging as a habit.** "Generally speaking", "in many cases", "it could be argued", "some might say". Say what is true. When something is genuinely uncertain, say exactly how and why, once, clearly.

**Formatting as a substitute for writing.** Bold on half the words. Bullet lists where a paragraph would carry the reasoning. Headings every hundred words. Use structure to help the reader find things, not to avoid explaining them.

## Two examples

**Not this:**

> In today's fast-paced world, effective learning is more crucial than ever. It's not just about memorisation — it's about truly understanding. Let's dive into the science of retrieval practice, a game-changing technique that can transform your studying. Research has shown that testing yourself is one of the most powerful tools in your learning arsenal.

**This:**

> Most people study by reading their notes again. It feels productive, and it isn't. In 2006 two psychologists at Washington University ran a simple test. Students read a passage. Then half of them read it again, and half closed the book and wrote down what they could remember. A week later, the group that had closed the book remembered a third more. The rereaders had felt more confident the whole time. That gap, between what feels like learning and what is learning, is what this lesson is about.

**Not this:**

> Compound interest is a powerful financial concept that plays a crucial role in wealth building. Understanding it is essential for anyone looking to secure their financial future. Essentially, it means earning interest on your interest, creating a snowball effect over time.

**This:**

> Say you put $1,000 in an account paying 7% a year. After a year you have $1,070. The next year you earn 7% on $1,070, not on $1,000, so you get $75 instead of $70. That extra $5 doesn't sound like much. Keep going for forty years and the account holds about $15,000, and most of that growth came in the last decade. This is compound interest, and the reason it matters is that the same thing happens to debt.

## Making the reader think, not just read

A lesson is not paragraphs followed by a quiz. Reading is the weakest form of learning there is, and a lesson that only asks you to read has failed before the quiz starts. Build in the work:

- **Predict before you're told.** Before revealing a result, ask the reader to guess. Use a `:::predict` block: the question, then the answer hidden behind a button. Two or three per lesson.
- **Checkpoint questions mid-lesson.** After a hard idea, one quick question with the answer hidden, so the reader finds out right then whether it landed. Don't save every question for the end.
- **Worked examples with a gap.** Show the first steps, then ask the reader to do the next one before showing it.
- **Do it now.** Every lesson has at least one exercise the reader actually does, on paper or in their own subject, before the quiz.
- **Free recall before the quiz.** The site asks the reader to write everything they remember before the quiz opens. That is the single most useful thing a lesson can make someone do.

## The check

Before a lesson is done, read it aloud. Where you would be embarrassed to say a sentence to a real person sitting across from you, rewrite it. Then search the file for "—" and for every word in the banned list. Then ask: does this sound like someone who knows the subject and likes the reader? If not, it's not done.
