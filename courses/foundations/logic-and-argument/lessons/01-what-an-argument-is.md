  - q: You mention to a neighbour that the bakery on Hill Road has shut. She says, "It closed because the rent went up and the owner couldn't cover it." Which description of what she said is right?
    options:
      - An argument; the word "because" marks the rent rise as a premise offered in support
      - An explanation; the closing is already agreed and she is saying why it happened
      - A report; she repeats what the owner said without taking any position of her own
      - An argument with a missing premise, namely that rising rent forces bakeries to close
    answer: 1
    explain: Ask what she is trying to get you to accept. Not that the bakery closed; you told her that. She takes the closing as given and tells you why. That is an explanation, so B is right. A and D both read "because" as a premise marker, which it often is, but not here; the test is direction, not the word. C would fit if she'd said "the owner says it was the rent", but she doesn't attribute the claim to anyone; she just states it.
  - q: A landlord emails, "The boiler is eleven years old. We'll replace it before winter rather than repair it again. It has needed three call-outs this year. A new one carries a ten-year guarantee." Which sentence is the conclusion?
    options:
      - The boiler is eleven years old
      - We'll replace it before winter rather than repair it again
      - It has needed three call-outs this year
      - A new one carries a ten-year guarantee
    answer: 1
    explain: No indicator words, so ask what the email wants you to accept. Three sentences are facts about the boiler that nobody would dispute; the second is a decision the other three are there to justify. That is B. A learner who assumes the conclusion comes last picks D, which is a premise (it supports replacing rather than repairing). A and C are premises too. The conclusion is the second sentence, which is where real writers often put it.
  - q: Your aunt says, "She's been driving for thirty years, so she'll pass the test." Which bracketed premise is the one you should add before you judge the argument?
    options:
      - "[People with many years of driving experience usually pass a driving test]"
      - "[Anyone who has driven for thirty years is certain to pass a driving test]"
      - "[The driving test is easy enough that nearly everyone who takes it passes]"
      - "[She has already passed several driving tests during those thirty years]"
    answer: 0
    explain: The rule is to add the premise the arguer would accept, in the form they'd accept it, and then see what follows. Your aunt is relying on experience being a good sign of passing, which is A. B makes the argument airtight, but your aunt would not sign it; nobody thinks thirty years makes passing certain, and adding it would hand you a straw man to knock down. C changes the subject from her experience to the test's difficulty, which is not what your aunt said. D is a new factual claim, not the link between experience and passing.
  - q: Four members of a gym comment on its opening hours. Which one is giving you an argument?
    options:
      - "If the gym opens at six, the early classes will fill up."
      - "The notice on the door says the gym will open at six from May."
      - "The gym should open at six, and that's the end of it."
      - "Every treadmill is taken by seven as it is, so it should open at six."
    answer: 3
    explain: D offers a reason (the machines are full by seven) for a conclusion (open at six), which is what an argument is. A is a conditional on its own; it says what would follow if the gym opened at six, but doesn't claim it should. B reports what a notice says and takes no position. C asserts the conclusion and refuses to give a reason, which is the opposite of arguing. The missing premise in D, roughly "[if the machines are full by seven, opening earlier would spread the demand]", is one the speaker would accept, and once it's written down you can see it's the step to check.
  - q: A head teacher says, "Attendance fell in the term after we moved the start time, so the new start time must be to blame." You notice a step is missing. What should you do next?
    options:
      - Reject the argument on the spot, since an argument that leaves a premise unstated is defective
      - Write down the premise she relies on, that nothing else she knows of changed that term, then check it
      - Add whatever premise makes the conclusion follow with certainty, then judge the argument on that
      - Treat the passage as an explanation of why attendance fell, which means there is nothing to check
    answer: 1
    explain: Most real arguments leave a step unstated, so A would reject nearly everything. The unstated step here is roughly "nothing else I know of that affects attendance changed that term", and once it's written down you can ask whether it holds; maybe a flu went round, maybe the bus route changed. That is B. C is the mistake of making the argument work rather than making it faithful; the head would not sign an airtight premise like "nothing else could possibly have changed". D misreads the direction; she is trying to establish that the start time is to blame, which is not agreed, so it's an argument, not an explanation.

      - "She's been off work since Tuesday."
    answer: 1
    explain: In B the "since" clause is offered as a reason to accept the other clause, and the swap test confirms it; "Because you've never driven on ice, take the train" reads naturally. A and D are dates; swapping in "because" produces nonsense. C is the one to think about. "Since" there gives the time the change began, and even if you hear a cause in it, nobody is being persuaded that traffic doubled; that's reported as known, and a cause offered for something already accepted is an explanation, not an argument. The swap test alone won't settle C, because "because the road reopened" reads fine; the direction test does.

A friend texts you on Sunday night: "Take the train tomorrow. The M6 is shut at junction 10." Two sentences, and you're already deciding whether to agree. That's an argument, and you just did something with it without noticing: you supplied a step your friend never wrote, something like "if the M6 is shut, driving will take much longer". If that step is wrong (the closure is northbound and you're going south), the advice is wrong, and your friend never said the thing that was wrong.

Almost every argument you meet works like this. Your manager, the advert, the voice in your own head: reasons offered for a claim, with one reason left out because it seemed too obvious to say. This lesson teaches you to see the shape of an argument, tell it apart from the things that only look like one, and write down the step that's missing. That last skill is the one that pays. When an argument has a weak step, the unstated one is a good place to look, because it's the step nobody checked.

## The check you'll use for the whole course

Here is the method. You'll add to it lesson by lesson, and by lesson 3 you should be able to say it from memory.

1. Find the conclusion.
2. Find the premises.
3. Supply what's missing.
4. Test the link: does the conclusion follow from the premises?
5. Test the premises: are they acceptable?
6. Only now consult your opinion of the conclusion.

This lesson does steps 1 to 3. Step 6 is written last on purpose. Most people start there, decide whether they like the conclusion, and then go looking for reasons to have been right. The check makes you earn your opinion. It's also the order in which logic can help you: logic tests the link, and only the link. Whether the premises are true is a question for evidence, and that is a different course. I'll say that again in lesson 2, because it's the sentence people forget first.

## What an argument is

An argument is a set of statements in which some of them, the premises, are offered as reasons for another, the conclusion. Not a quarrel. Just reasons offered for a claim, however calmly.

The definition is old. Aristotle, in the fourth century BC, wrote that a syllogism, his word for a deduction, is "discourse in which, certain things being stated, something other than what is stated follows of necessity from their being so" ([*Prior Analytics* I.1](http://classics.mit.edu/Aristotle/prior.1.i.html)).[7] Read it slowly: certain things stated (premises), something other (the conclusion), follows from their being so (the link). Nearly twenty-four centuries later that's still the shape. Aristotle's "of necessity" describes one kind of link, the deductive kind; there are also arguments whose premises are meant only to make the conclusion likely, and lesson 5 covers those.[4] The three parts are the same in both.

Your friend's text, laid out:

```
1. The M6 is shut at junction 10.
2. [If the M6 is shut, driving to work tomorrow will take much longer.]
------------------------------------------------------------------------
C: You should take the train tomorrow.
```

That's standard form, and every argument in this course gets written the same way: numbered premises, a line, then "C:" for the conclusion. Anything the arguer left unsaid goes in square brackets. No symbols, just the sentences in the order that shows what supports what.

One small thing you'll have noticed: your friend wrote "take the train", a command, and the standard form says "you should take the train", a claim. That's deliberate. A command can't be true or false, and a claim can, so a command gets rewritten as the claim behind it before it goes on the page. Every line in standard form is something you can ask "is that true?" of, including the conclusion.[1]

Standard form is a tool, not a ritual. Its point is that once the argument is laid out, you can see each premise on its own and ask two separate questions of it: is it true, and does it help? Buried in a paragraph, the premises lean on each other and on the tone of voice, and neither question gets asked.

## Step 1: find the conclusion

The conclusion is whatever the passage is trying to get you to accept. That's the whole test, and it's more reliable than any word list.

Word lists exist, and they're useful as long as you don't trust them. Words that often mark a conclusion: so, therefore, hence, thus, consequently, it follows that, implies that. Words that often mark a premise: because, since, for, given that, as, seeing that. Matthew Van Cleave, whose free textbook is the best treatment of this lesson's material, gives both lists in [section 1.2](https://human.libretexts.org/Bookshelves/Philosophy/Introduction_to_Logic_and_Critical_Thinking_2e_%28van_Cleave%29/01%3A_Reconstructing_and_Analyzing_Arguments/1.02%3A_Identifying_Arguments) and then warns that they are "not failsafe".[2]

Two problems. First, most real arguments use none of these words. "Nobody's used the new system. The rollout should wait. Training takes two weeks." No indicator anywhere, and the conclusion is in the middle. Second, the words have other jobs. Van Cleave's examples: "I have been running competitively since 1999" has "since" as a date, and "I am so happy to have finally finished that class" has "so" as an intensifier. His quick test is to swap the word for a different indicator from the same list. "I have been running competitively because 1999" is nonsense, so that "since" wasn't marking a premise.[2] The swap has a limit, though. When a sentence has a causal reading, "because" slots in fine and the test tells you nothing: "since the road reopened, traffic has doubled" survives the swap and still isn't offering you a reason to believe anything. So the swap is a hint, and the question below is the test.

For any passage: what is this trying to get me to accept? Often the answer is the sentence you'd push back on if you disagreed. Premises tend to be things the writer expects you to grant; the conclusion is the thing they're working to earn.

One more thing people assume: the conclusion comes last. It comes last in textbook examples. In real writing it's often first: people state the claim and then defend it. A careful reader gets used to finding the conclusion first, in the middle, or split across two sentences.

:::checkpoint "Book the earlier flight. The later one lands after the last train has gone. A taxi from that airport is over a hundred pounds." Which sentence is the conclusion?
The first. It's the thing the passage wants from you; the other two are facts you'd grant without a fight. If you picked the last sentence because it came last, that's the habit this section is trying to break. Notice there isn't an indicator word anywhere, and notice the command has to become a claim before it goes in standard form: "C: You should book the earlier flight."
:::

## Step 2: find the premises, and notice what isn't an argument

Once you have the conclusion, the premises are the statements offered to support it. Everything else in the passage (background, colour, a joke, a repetition) can be left out of the standard form.

But before you list premises, check that you have an argument at all. Several kinds of passage look like arguments and aren't. The one that fools nearly everyone is the explanation.

:::predict Here are three passages that each contain the word "because". Sort them: which are arguments and which are explanations?
(a) "The train was late because a tree came down on the line near Crewe."
(b) "You should go to bed early because the exam is at nine."
(c) "Our sales fell in March because the new website is harder to use."

Most people sort (a) as an explanation and (b) as an argument, and get stuck on (c). Here is the answer. (a) is an explanation: nobody is trying to convince you the train was late; you were on it. (b) is an argument: it's trying to establish that you should go to bed early, and you might well disagree. (c) can be either, and that's the lesson. If everyone agrees sales fell and the speaker is telling you why, it's an explanation. If the speaker is trying to convince you that the website is at fault, then "sales fell" is a premise and the website claim is the conclusion, and the sentence is an argument with the conclusion tucked into the "because" clause. Same words; the job depends on what's taken as given.
:::

The test is direction. An argument runs from what's offered to what's claimed: here are reasons, therefore accept this. An explanation runs the other way: this is accepted, here is why it happened. Van Cleave puts it as the difference between establishing *that* something is true and explaining *why* it's true; in an explanation, "we assume that what we are trying to explain (i.e., the conclusion) is true" ([section 1.3](https://human.libretexts.org/Bookshelves/Philosophy/Introduction_to_Logic_and_Critical_Thinking_2e_%28van_Cleave%29/01%3A_Reconstructing_and_Analyzing_Arguments/1.03%3A_Arguments_vs._Explanations)).[2] Trudy Govier draws the same line in the first chapter of her textbook: an argument tries to justify a claim, an explanation tries to account for a fact already granted.[1]

So the question to ask is: is the thing after "because" being used to convince me of the thing before it, or is the thing before it already agreed and the "because" telling me how it came about? Go back to (a): "the train was late because a tree came down." If you'd never believe the train was late unless told about the tree, it would be an argument. Nobody's in that position. It's an explanation.

Three more look-alikes, quicker:

**A report.** "The city council's report says the bridge needs repairs within two years." That tells you what a document claims. It gives no reason to believe the claim and takes no position on it. A report can *contain* an argument ("the council argues that, because the piers are corroding, the bridge needs repairs"), in which case you can lay out the council's argument. But the reporting sentence itself argues for nothing.

**A bare assertion.** "The bridge needs repairs, and that's all there is to it." A conclusion with no premises. People assert things all day, and the confident tone can make an assertion feel argued for. It isn't. You can't check a link that isn't there; you can only ask for one.

**A conditional on its own.** "If the bridge isn't repaired by June, it'll have to close." This says what follows from a condition. It doesn't claim the condition holds, so it doesn't conclude anything. Add "and it won't be repaired by June" and you have an argument. Lesson 3 is about conditionals, and a great deal of confused arguing comes from treating "if P then Q" as though it had asserted P.

:::checkpoint Four passages. Which one is not an argument, and what is it instead?
(1) "The lights won't come on and the starter's silent, so the battery's dead."
(2) "Take the 7:40 tomorrow. Every 8:10 this month has been cancelled."
(3) "The pipe burst because the water in it froze overnight."
(4) "Anyone who's lived here ten years knows the river floods. Don't build on the flat."

(3) is not an argument. It's an explanation: the pipe bursting is taken as known and the freezing is offered as the cause. (1) is an argument with "so" marking the conclusion. (2) is an argument with no indicator words and the conclusion first; the cancellations are the reason offered for taking the earlier train. (4) is an argument whose conclusion comes last and whose premise is dressed up as what everyone knows. Its missing step is something like "[land the river floods isn't safe to build on]", and once that's written you can ask whether it's true of this flat.
:::

## Step 3: supply what's missing

Now the part that matters most: nearly every argument you'll meet outside a logic textbook leaves something out. The old name for an argument with an unstated premise (or, sometimes, an unstated conclusion) is an enthymeme, and the standard textbooks treat them as routine.[1][2][3] The name doesn't matter. Why the premise is missing does.

It's missing because the arguer thought it too obvious to say. Most of the time they were right: the unstated step is shared background, and writing it down changes nothing. But "too obvious to say" is also exactly the description of a claim nobody checked. When someone writes a premise down, they look at it, and a reader looks at it. When they leave it out, neither does. So some unstated steps are harmless and some are the weak point of the whole argument, and from the outside the two look identical. The only way to tell which you have is to write it down. That's why you always do.

That gives you the rule for what to write in the brackets. Not the premise that makes the argument work. The premise the arguer is actually relying on, in the form they'd accept if you read it back to them. Sometimes that premise makes the argument work. Sometimes, once it's written down, it plainly doesn't, and then you've found the problem, and you've found it in the arguer's own words rather than in a version you invented.

You should know that most textbooks state the rule from the other side. Van Cleave, in [section 1.9](https://human.libretexts.org/Bookshelves/Philosophy/Introduction_to_Logic_and_Critical_Thinking_2e_%28van_Cleave%29/01%3A_Reconstructing_and_Analyzing_Arguments/1.09%3A_Arguments_with_Missing_Premises), tells you to supply the most plausible premise that makes the argument valid or strong, and calls that charity.[2] Govier says much the same.[1] In practice the two rules agree, because "most plausible" and "the one they'd accept" almost always pick out the same sentence, and where they differ the arguer's own version wins: a premise they'd reject isn't charity, it's a substitution. Lesson 7 comes back to charity properly. For now, hold both halves: the premise must be one the arguer would sign, and among those, take the strongest.

### Worked example 1: the icy roads

"The roads are icy. Leave ten minutes early."

Step 1, the conclusion: what's this trying to get me to do? Leave ten minutes early, which as a claim is "you should leave ten minutes early". Step 2, the premise: the roads are icy. Now step 3. Something links icy roads to leaving early, and it isn't stated.

:::predict Write the missing premise yourself, the way the speaker would say it if you asked "why does ice mean leaving early?" Then open this.
Here's mine:

```
1. The roads are icy.
2. [Icy roads make the drive slower.]
------------------------------------------
C: You should leave ten minutes early.
```

Notice what I didn't write: "[Icy roads always add exactly ten minutes]". That would tie the premise to the conclusion more tightly, but nobody believes it, and the speaker wouldn't sign it. "Icy roads make the drive slower" is what they'd say, and it's true, and it gets you most of the way. Whether it gets you exactly to "ten minutes" is a fair question, and now you can see it's a question.
:::

Now the same words doing a different job: "We left early because the roads were icy." Is that an argument? Run the test. Is anyone trying to convince you that we left early? No, that's granted. The sentence says why. It's an explanation, and there's nothing to put in standard form.

### Worked example 2: Harry, and the exception that appears when you write the rule down

This example is from Stephen Toulmin's *The Uses of Argument*, published in 1958, and nearly every textbook on argument since has borrowed it.[5][6]

"Harry was born in Bermuda, so Harry is a British subject."

:::predict Conclusion and premise are easy here. What is the missing step, in the form the speaker would accept?
The speaker is relying on a general rule about people born in Bermuda. Toulmin's wording: "A man born in Bermuda will generally be a British subject".[5] Notice the word "generally". The speaker would not accept "everyone born in Bermuda is a British subject, without exception"; they'd say "well, as a rule". Write it the way they'd say it.
:::

```
1. Harry was born in Bermuda.
2. [A man born in Bermuda will generally be a British subject.]
----------------------------------------------------------------
C: Harry is a British subject.
```

That was the law as Toulmin described it in 1958. British nationality law has been rewritten since, so the rule itself has gone out of date, which is worth a moment: a bracketed premise like this is a claim about the world, and claims about the world can stop being true.[8]

Here's the wrinkle. Look at premise 2. The moment it's written down, that "generally" is sitting there in plain view, and a reader immediately asks: generally, except when? Toulmin supplies two exceptions and a trailing ellipsis for the rest: unless both his parents were aliens (the legal term, then and now, for citizens of another state), or unless he has since become a naturalised American.[5] Those exceptions were invisible in the one-line version. Nobody reading "born in Bermuda, so British" pauses to think about Harry's parents. Reading "a man born in Bermuda will *generally* be a British subject", everyone does.

That is what step 3 is for. Writing the missing premise down shows you how far the argument reaches. A general rule has exceptions, the exceptions are the counter-cases to the argument, and you only find them by looking at the rule. Toulmin built a whole layout around this. He calls the rule the warrant and the exceptions the rebuttal, and he draws the argument like this:

Look at where the warrant sits: under the arrow, not in the line of premises. It's the rule that licenses the step from data to claim, and it's the thing nobody said. The rebuttal hangs off the claim, and it only exists because the warrant says "generally". Lesson 7 gives you the full layout, with the two slots this drawing leaves out. For now, the habit is enough: write the rule the arguer relies on, then ask what it doesn't cover.

Notice too that the argument for Harry is a perfectly good one. The exceptions don't sink it; they qualify it. "Harry is presumably a British subject, unless one of those things applies" is what the argument actually earns, and an honest arguer would say so. Most of the time, finding the missing premise doesn't destroy an argument. It tells you how much weight it can bear.

### Worked example 3: no indicator words, conclusion first

Here's a passage of the kind you might get in an email from a friend who's house-hunting. (It's made up; the point is the shape.)

"Don't offer on the Elm Street house. The survey found damp in two rooms. Damp like that costs thousands to put right. And the seller has already dropped the price once, which tells you something."

No "so", no "therefore", no "because". Do steps 1 and 2 yourself before opening the answer. What is this trying to get you to accept, and what's offered in support? Then try step 3: there are two things this argument leans on and never states. What are they?

:::predict Write the standard form, with the missing steps in brackets, then open this.
The conclusion is the first sentence: don't offer on the house, which as a claim is "you shouldn't offer on the Elm Street house". Everything after it is a reason. Two steps are missing. One is what the friend means by "which tells you something". The other is the rule that gets from damp and cost to "don't offer".

```
1. The survey found damp in two rooms.
2. Damp like that costs thousands to put right.
3. The seller has already dropped the price once.
4. [A price drop means other buyers have looked and walked away.]
5. [You shouldn't offer on a house with faults that cost thousands, unless the price already reflects them.]
------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
C: You shouldn't offer on the Elm Street house.
```

I could have written one bracketed line that said "a house with damp and a price drop is one you shouldn't offer on", and it would have made the argument work. It would also have told you nothing, because it's just the premises and the conclusion stapled together. Split into the two things the friend actually believes, each can be looked at on its own.
:::

Now look at premises 4 and 5, the ones the friend never said. Would they accept them? Roughly, yes. Are they true? Premise 5 carries its own exception in its last four words: if the price has already dropped by more than the damp costs to fix, the argument points the other way. And premise 4 is one reading of "which tells you something"; the other reading is that a seller who has dropped the price once is a seller in a hurry, which makes the house a bargain, not a warning. The friend's argument was persuasive in the email and looks much thinner in standard form, and nothing was added or removed; it was only written down.

:::checkpoint You reconstruct an argument and find that the only premise that makes it work is one the arguer would reject if you read it to them. What have you found?
You've found the argument's weak step, and you've found it fairly. Either the arguer is relying on a claim they don't believe, which they need to know, or there's a different, weaker premise they do believe, in which case the conclusion doesn't follow as strongly as they said. What you have not found is a reason to reject the conclusion. A bad argument for a claim tells you nothing about the claim; there may be a better one. Lesson 8 gives that mistake its name.
:::

## What people get wrong

**"An argument is a quarrel."** In everyday English it can be. In this course it never is. An argument is reasons offered for a claim, and two people can argue, in this sense, while agreeing about nearly everything and liking each other throughout. If you find yourself thinking "I don't want to argue", check whether you mean "I don't want to fight" or "I don't want to give reasons". The second is the one to worry about.

**"If there's a 'because', it's an argument."** "Because" introduces explanations as readily as premises. The cafe shut because the lease ran out. The pipe burst because the water froze. Neither of those is trying to convince you of anything. The test is never the word. The test is whether the thing before "because" is being established or taken for granted.

**"The conclusion is the last sentence."** It's the last sentence in textbook examples, which is where the habit comes from. In real writing it's often first, sometimes in the middle, and sometimes unstated (a passage can list reasons and let you draw the conclusion yourself, which is a rhetorical move you should notice). Find it by asking what the passage wants from you.

**"The missing premise is whatever makes the argument work, whether or not they'd accept it."** This one feels like generosity, and it isn't. If you add a premise the arguer wouldn't accept, you're no longer evaluating their argument; you're evaluating one you built. Add the strongest premise they'd sign. If that premise makes the argument work, good. If it doesn't, you've found the gap, and you can show it to them in their own terms, which is the version they can't wave away.

**"Finding a missing premise means the argument is bad."** Almost every real argument has one. Finding it is the beginning of evaluation, not the end. Harry's argument has a missing premise and is a good argument; it just reaches a little less far than its one-line version claimed.

:::callout One thing to keep separate
Steps 1 to 3 tell you what the argument is. They don't tell you whether it's any good. That takes steps 4 and 5, and you'll spend the next five lessons on them. It is tempting, having laid an argument out, to leap to a verdict. Don't yet. A clean reconstruction of an argument you disagree with is worth more than a hasty rejection of it, and the reconstruction is what the rest of the course works on.
:::

## Practice

Five passages. For each, decide whether it's an argument, an explanation, a report, or something else that isn't an argument. For the arguments, write the standard form with any missing premise in brackets. Do all five on paper before you open the answers.

(A) "The forecast says thunderstorms from three. Move the barbecue to Sunday."

(B) "The match was abandoned because lightning was seen within five miles of the stadium."

(C) "According to the club's statement, the pitch will be relaid over the summer."

(D) "He's never once returned a book on time. I wouldn't lend him that one."

(E) "If the river rises another foot, the footpath will be under water."

:::checkpoint Open this when your five answers are written.
(A) Argument. Conclusion: you should move the barbecue to Sunday. Premise: the forecast says thunderstorms from three. Missing: [you can't hold a barbecue in a thunderstorm] and, more quietly, [the forecast is reliable enough to plan by]. That second one is the interesting gap, because it's the premise you'd check with evidence rather than logic: how often is this forecast right a day out? That's step 5's question, and you couldn't have asked it until the premise was on the page.

(B) Explanation. The abandonment is taken as known; the lightning is the reason it happened. Nobody is being persuaded that the match was abandoned.

(C) Report. It tells you what the club said. It offers no reason to believe the pitch will be relaid beyond the club having said so, and takes no position.

(D) Argument, no indicator words, conclusion last. Premise: he's never once returned a book on time. Conclusion, as a claim: I shouldn't lend him that one. Missing: [someone who never returns books on time is likely to keep or lose this one]. Written down, the premise is plausible but has an obvious exception: the books he was slow with may have been ones he didn't care about, and this one might be different. Whether that matters is a question for step 5, and again you couldn't have asked it until the premise was on the page.

(E) Not an argument. A conditional on its own: it says what would follow if the river rose, and claims neither that it will rise nor that the path will flood. Add "and it's rising fast" and you'd have one.
:::

:::exercise Do it now: start your folder
This course ends with a project in which you take a real argument apart and answer it. You'll choose that argument from a folder you build as you go, starting now. Give this fifteen minutes.

1. Find one argument you actually met this week. A news article, a message from someone, an advert, something a colleague said, a post you disagreed with. It must be real and it must be trying to get you to accept something. Write down where it came from and the date.
2. Copy or paraphrase it in two to four sentences, as faithfully as you can.
3. Write its standard form: numbered premises, a line, "C:" and the conclusion. Put any missing premise in square brackets, in the form the arguer would accept if you read it back to them.
4. Under the standard form, write one sentence: which premise, stated or bracketed, would you want to check first, and why?

Keep the page. In lesson 2 you'll come back to this argument and ask one question of it: could every premise be true and the conclusion still false? You'll add to the folder every lesson, and by lesson 10 you'll have a set of checked arguments to choose the project from.
:::
