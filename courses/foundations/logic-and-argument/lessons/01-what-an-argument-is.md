quiz:
  - q: A neighbour tells you, "The bakery on Hill Road closed last month because the rent went up and the owner couldn't cover it." Which description of this passage is right?
    options:
      - An argument; the word "because" marks the rent rise as a premise offered in support
      - An explanation; the closing is treated as known and the passage says why it happened
      - A report; it repeats what the owner said without taking any position of its own
      - An argument with a missing premise, namely that rising rent forces bakeries to close
    answer: 1
    explain: Ask what the passage is trying to get you to accept. Not that the bakery closed; you both already know that. It takes the closing as given and tells you why. That is an explanation, so B is right. A and D both read "because" as a premise marker, which it often is, but not here; the test is direction, not the word. C would fit if the passage said "the owner says it was the rent", but it doesn't attribute the claim to anyone; it just states it.
  - q: A colleague writes, "Nobody on the team has used it. The rollout should wait a month. Training takes roughly two weeks. The busy season starts next week." Which sentence is the conclusion?
    options:
      - Nobody on the team has used it
      - Training takes roughly two weeks
      - The busy season starts next week
      - The rollout should wait a month
    answer: 3
    explain: There are no indicator words, so you find the conclusion by asking what the passage wants you to accept. Three of the sentences are facts about the situation that nobody would argue about; the fourth is a recommendation that the other three are there to support. That is D. A learner who assumes the conclusion is the last sentence picks C, which is a premise. A and B are premises too. The conclusion sits second, which is common in real writing.
  - q: Your aunt says, "She's been driving for thirty years, so she'll pass the test." Which bracketed premise is the one you should add before you judge the argument?
    options:
      - "[People with many years of driving experience usually pass a driving test]"
      - "[Anyone who has driven for thirty years is certain to pass a driving test]"
      - "[The driving test is easy enough that nearly everyone who takes it passes]"
      - "[She has already passed several driving tests during those thirty years]"
    answer: 0
    explain: The rule is to add the premise the arguer would accept, in the form they'd accept it, and then see what follows. Your aunt is relying on experience being a good sign of passing, which is A. B makes the argument airtight, but your aunt would not sign it; nobody thinks thirty years makes passing certain, and adding it would hand you a straw man to knock down. C changes the subject from her experience to the test's difficulty, which is not what your aunt said. D is a new factual claim, not the link between experience and passing.
  - q: Four people comment on the same footbridge. Which one is giving you an argument?
    options:
      - "If the bridge isn't fixed by June, it will have to be closed."
      - "The council's report says the bridge needs fixing by June."
      - "It failed two inspections, so it needs to be fixed by June."
      - "The bridge needs fixing by June, and that is all there is to it."
    answer: 2
    explain: C offers a reason (two failed inspections) for a conclusion (fix it by June), which is what an argument is. A is a single conditional; it tells you what follows from a condition but does not claim the condition holds, so it argues for nothing. B reports what a document says without endorsing it. D asserts the conclusion and refuses to give a reason, which is the opposite of arguing. The missing premise in C, roughly "[a bridge that fails two inspections needs prompt repair]", is one the speaker would accept.
  - q: A shop owner says, "Our sales dropped in March, so the new website must be to blame." You notice a step is missing. What should you do next?
    options:
      - Reject the argument on the spot, since an argument that leaves a premise unstated is defective
      - Write down the premise the owner relies on, that nothing else changed in March, then check it
      - Add whatever premise makes the conclusion follow with certainty, then judge the argument on that
      - Treat the passage as an explanation of why sales fell, which means there is nothing to check
    answer: 1
    explain: Most real arguments leave a step unstated, so A would reject nearly everything. The unstated step here is roughly "nothing else that affects sales changed in March", and once it's written down you can ask whether it holds; maybe a competitor opened, maybe the weather was bad. That is B. C is the mistake of making the argument work rather than making it faithful; the owner would not sign an airtight premise. D misreads the direction; the passage is trying to establish that the website is to blame, which is not agreed, so it's an argument, not an explanation.
  - q: A friend says, "Since the road reopened in April, traffic on our street has doubled." What is the word "since" doing here?
    options:
      - Marking a premise; the reopening is offered as evidence for the doubling of traffic
      - Marking a conclusion; the doubling of traffic is what the reopening is meant to establish
      - Marking a premise; try swapping in "because" and the sentence still reads naturally
      - Marking a time; the sentence reports when the change began, not a reason to believe it
    answer: 3
    explain: The word "since" can introduce a reason or a date, and here it is a date. The sentence reports a change and when it started; it does not offer the reopening as grounds for believing the traffic doubled. That is D. A and C both treat "since" as a premise indicator; the swap test in C is the right test but gives the wrong result here, because "because the road reopened, traffic has doubled" changes the meaning from a timing claim to a causal claim your friend may not have made. B has the direction backwards and treats a report as an argument.

A friend texts you on Sunday night: "Take the train tomorrow. The M6 is shut at junction 10." Two sentences, and you're already deciding whether to agree. That's an argument, and you just did something with it without noticing: you supplied a step your friend never wrote, something like "if the M6 is shut, driving will take much longer". If that step is wrong (the closure is northbound and you're going south), the advice is wrong, and your friend never said the thing that was wrong.

Almost every argument you meet works like this. The news, your manager, the advert, the voice in your own head: reasons offered for a claim, with the most important reason left out because it seemed too obvious to say. This lesson teaches you to see the shape of an argument, tell it apart from the things that only look like one, and write down the step that's missing. That last skill is the one that pays. The missing step is where bad arguments usually hide, because it's the step nobody checked.

## The check you'll use for the whole course

Here is the method. You'll add to it lesson by lesson, and by lesson 3 you should be able to say it from memory.

1. Find the conclusion.
2. Find the premises.
3. Supply what's missing.
4. Test the link: does the conclusion follow from the premises?
5. Test the premises: are they acceptable?
6. Only now consult your opinion of the conclusion.

This lesson does steps 1 to 3. Step 6 is written last on purpose. Most people start there, decide whether they like the conclusion, and then go looking for reasons to have been right. The check makes you earn your opinion. It's also the order in which logic can help you: logic tests the link, and only the link. Whether the premises are true is a question for evidence, and that is a different course. You'll hear this refrain again.

## What an argument is

An argument is a set of statements in which some of them, the premises, are offered as reasons for another, the conclusion. Not a quarrel. Not raised voices. Just reasons offered for a claim, however calmly.

The definition is old. Aristotle, in the fourth century BC, wrote that a deduction is "discourse in which, certain things being stated, something other than what is stated follows of necessity from their being so" (Aristotle, *Prior Analytics* I.1).[6] Read it slowly: certain things stated (premises), something other (the conclusion), follows from their being so (the link). Twenty-three centuries later that's still the shape. Aristotle's "of necessity" describes one kind of link, the deductive kind; lesson 5 covers links that only make the conclusion likely. The three parts are the same in both.

Your friend's text, laid out:

```
1. The M6 is shut at junction 10.
2. [If the M6 is shut, driving to work tomorrow will take much longer.]
------------------------------------------------------------------------
C: You should take the train tomorrow.
```

That's standard form, and every argument in this course gets written the same way: numbered premises, a line, then "C:" for the conclusion. Anything the arguer left unsaid goes in square brackets. No symbols, no jargon, just the sentences in the order that shows what supports what.

Standard form is a tool, not a ritual. Its point is that once the argument is laid out, you can see each premise on its own and ask two separate questions of it: is it true, and does it help? Buried in a paragraph, the premises lean on each other and on the tone of voice, and neither question gets asked.

## Step 1: find the conclusion

The conclusion is whatever the passage is trying to get you to accept. That's the whole test, and it's more reliable than any word list.

Word lists exist, and they're useful as long as you don't trust them. Words that often mark a conclusion: so, therefore, hence, thus, consequently, it follows that, which means. Words that often mark a premise: because, since, for, given that, as, seeing that (Van Cleave, 2016, ch. 1).[2]

Two problems. First, most real arguments use none of these words. "Nobody's used the new system. The rollout should wait. Training takes two weeks." No indicator anywhere, and the conclusion is in the middle. Second, the words have other jobs. "I've been running since 2019" has "since" as a date. "I'm so tired" has "so" as an intensifier. Van Cleave suggests a quick test: swap the word for a different indicator from the same list. "I've been running because 2019" is nonsense, so that "since" wasn't marking a premise.[2]

So use the words as hints and use the question as the test. For any passage: what is this trying to get me to accept? Often the answer is the sentence you'd push back on if you disagreed. Premises tend to be things the writer expects you to grant; the conclusion is the thing they're working to earn.

One more thing people assume: the conclusion comes last. It comes last in textbook examples and about half the time in real life. Writers often lead with the claim and follow with reasons, and a careful reader gets used to finding the conclusion first, in the middle, or split across two sentences.

## Step 2: find the premises, and notice what isn't an argument

Once you have the conclusion, the premises are the statements offered to support it. Everything else in the passage (background, colour, a joke, a repetition) can be left out of the standard form.

But before you list premises, check that you have an argument at all. Several kinds of passage look like arguments and aren't. The one that fools nearly everyone is the explanation.

:::predict Here are three passages that each contain the word "because". Sort them: which are arguments and which are explanations?
(a) "The train was late because a tree came down on the line near Crewe."
(b) "You should go to bed early because the exam is at nine."
(c) "Our sales fell in March because the new website is harder to use."

Most people sort (a) as an explanation and (b) as an argument, and get stuck on (c). Here is the answer. (a) is an explanation: nobody is trying to convince you the train was late; you were on it. (b) is an argument: it's trying to establish that you should go to bed early, and you might well disagree. (c) can be either, and that's the lesson. If everyone agrees sales fell and the speaker is telling you why, it's an explanation. If the speaker is trying to convince you that the website is at fault, then "sales fell" is a premise and the website claim is the conclusion, and the sentence is an argument with the conclusion tucked into the "because" clause. Same words; the job depends on what's taken as given.
:::

The test is direction. An argument runs from what's offered to what's claimed: here are reasons, therefore accept this. An explanation runs the other way: this is accepted, here is why it happened. Van Cleave puts it as the difference between establishing *that* something is true and explaining *why* it's true; in an explanation, "we assume that what we are trying to explain (i.e., the conclusion) is true" (Van Cleave, 2016, ch. 1).[2] Govier draws the same line in the first chapter of her textbook: an argument tries to justify a claim, an explanation tries to account for a fact already granted (Govier, 2013, ch. 1).[1]

So the question to ask is: is the thing after "because" being used to convince me of the thing before it, or is the thing before it already agreed and the "because" telling me how it came about? Go back to (a): "the train was late because a tree came down." If you'd never believe the train was late unless told about the tree, it would be an argument. Nobody's in that position. It's an explanation.

Three more look-alikes, quicker:

**A report.** "The council's report says the bridge needs repairs within two years." That tells you what a document claims. It gives no reason to believe the claim and takes no position on it. A report can *contain* an argument ("the council argues that, because the piers are corroding, the bridge needs repairs"), in which case you can lay out the council's argument. But the reporting sentence itself argues for nothing.

**A bare assertion.** "The bridge needs repairs, and that's all there is to it." A conclusion with no premises. People assert things all day, and the confident tone can make an assertion feel argued for. It isn't. You can't check a link that isn't there; you can only ask for one.

**A conditional on its own.** "If the bridge isn't repaired by June, it'll have to close." This says what follows from a condition. It doesn't claim the condition holds, so it doesn't conclude anything. Add "and it won't be repaired by June" and you have an argument. Lesson 3 is about conditionals, and a great deal of confused arguing comes from treating "if P then Q" as though it had asserted P.

:::checkpoint Four passages. Which one is not an argument, and what is it instead?
(1) "The lights won't come on and the starter's silent, so the battery's dead."
(2) "She's late every Monday. There's roadworks on her route."
(3) "The pipe burst because the water in it froze overnight."
(4) "Anyone who's lived here ten years knows the river floods. Don't build on the flat."

(3) is not an argument. It's an explanation: the pipe bursting is taken as known and the freezing is offered as the cause. (1) is an argument with "so" marking the conclusion. (2) is an argument with no indicator words at all; the roadworks are offered as a reason to accept that she's late every Monday, or, on another reading, as an explanation of a lateness everyone already knows about. If you found yourself unsure about (2), good: the honest answer is that you'd need to know what's taken as given. (4) is an argument whose conclusion comes last and whose premise is dressed up as what everyone knows.
:::

## Step 3: supply what's missing

Now the part that matters most.

Nearly every argument you'll meet outside a logic textbook leaves something out. The old name for an argument with an unstated premise is an enthymeme, and the standard textbooks treat them as a routine part of analysing arguments (Copi, Cohen & McMahon, 2011; Govier, 2013, ch. 2).[3][1] The name isn't important. What's important is why the premise is missing.

It's missing because the arguer thought it too obvious to say. And "too obvious to say" is exactly the description of a claim nobody checked. When someone writes down a premise, they look at it, and a reader looks at it. When they leave it out, neither does. So the unstated step is not usually the strongest part of an argument. It's usually the part that has never been examined, by anyone, including the person making the argument.

That gives you the rule for what to write in the brackets. Not the premise that makes the argument work. The premise the arguer is actually relying on, in the form they'd accept if you read it back to them. Sometimes that premise makes the argument work. Sometimes, once it's written down, it plainly doesn't, and then you've found the problem, and you've found it in the arguer's own words rather than in a version you invented.

### Worked example 1: the icy roads

"The roads are icy. Leave ten minutes early."

Step 1, the conclusion: what's this trying to get me to do? Leave ten minutes early. Step 2, the premise: the roads are icy. Now step 3. Something links icy roads to leaving early, and it isn't stated.

Before you read on, write the missing premise yourself. Say it the way the speaker would say it if you asked "why does ice mean leaving early?"

Here's mine:

```
1. The roads are icy.
2. [Icy roads make the drive slower.]
------------------------------------------
C: Leave ten minutes early.
```

Notice what I didn't write: "[Icy roads always add exactly ten minutes]". That would tie the premise to the conclusion more tightly, but nobody believes it, and the speaker wouldn't sign it. "Icy roads make the drive slower" is what they'd say, and it's true, and it gets you most of the way. Whether it gets you exactly to "ten minutes" is a fair question, and now you can see it's a question.

Now the same words doing a different job: "We left early because the roads were icy." Is that an argument? Run the test. Is anyone trying to convince you that we left early? No, that's granted. The sentence says why. It's an explanation, and there's nothing to put in standard form.

### Worked example 2: Harry, and the exception that appears when you write the rule down

This example is from Stephen Toulmin's *The Uses of Argument*, published in 1958, and it has been used by everyone who teaches this subject since (Toulmin, 1958, pp. 97 to 102, via Hitchcock, 2002).[5]

"Harry was born in Bermuda, so Harry is a British subject."

:::predict Conclusion and premise are easy here. What is the missing step, in the form the speaker would accept?
The speaker is relying on a general rule about people born in Bermuda. Toulmin's wording: "a man born in Bermuda is generally a British subject". Notice the word "generally". The speaker would not accept "everyone born in Bermuda is a British subject, without exception"; they'd say "well, as a rule". Write it the way they'd say it.
:::

```
1. Harry was born in Bermuda.
2. [A man born in Bermuda is generally a British subject.]
-----------------------------------------------------------
C: Harry is a British subject.
```

Here's the wrinkle. Look at premise 2. The moment it's written down, that "generally" is sitting there in plain view, and a reader immediately asks: generally, except when? Toulmin supplies the answer: unless both his parents were aliens, and he goes on to list others.[5] Those exceptions were invisible in the one-line version. Nobody reading "born in Bermuda, so British" pauses to think about Harry's parents. Reading "a man born in Bermuda is *generally* a British subject", everyone does.

That is the mechanism of step 3, and it's worth stating plainly. Writing the missing premise down doesn't just complete the argument. It shows the argument's reach. A general rule has exceptions, and the exceptions are the counter-cases to the argument, and you only find them by looking at the rule. Toulmin built a whole layout around this (he calls the rule the warrant and the exceptions the rebuttal), and lesson 7 will give you the full version. For now, the habit is enough: write the rule the arguer relies on, then ask what it doesn't cover.

Notice too that the argument for Harry is a perfectly good one. The exceptions don't sink it; they qualify it. "Harry is presumably a British subject, unless one of those things applies" is what the argument actually earns, and an honest arguer would say so. Most of the time, finding the missing premise doesn't destroy an argument. It tells you how much weight it can bear.

### Worked example 3: no indicator words, conclusion first

Here's a passage of the kind you might get in an email from a friend who's house-hunting. (It's made up; the point is the shape.)

"Don't offer on the Elm Street house. The survey found damp in two rooms. Damp like that costs thousands to put right. And the seller has already dropped the price once, which tells you something."

No "so", no "therefore", no "because". Do steps 1 and 2 yourself before reading on. What is this trying to get you to accept, and what's offered in support?

The conclusion is the first sentence: don't offer on the house. Everything after it is a reason. Now write the standard form, with the missing step in brackets. There is one general rule this argument leans on and never states. What is it?

```
1. The survey found damp in two rooms.
2. Damp like that costs thousands to put right.
3. The seller has already dropped the price once.
4. [A house with expensive faults and a seller who has dropped the price is one you should not offer on.]
------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
C: Don't offer on the Elm Street house.
```

Now look at premise 4, which is the one the friend never said. Is it something they'd accept? Roughly, yes. Is it true? Now that it's written down, you can see it's doing a lot of work, and you can see the exception at once: if the price has dropped by more than the damp costs to fix, the argument points the other way. A seller who has dropped the price once is, on one reading, a warning; on another, a bargain. The friend's argument was persuasive in the email and looks much thinner in standard form, and nothing was added or removed. It was just written down.

:::checkpoint You reconstruct an argument and find that the only premise that makes it work is one the arguer would reject if you read it to them. What have you found?
You've found the argument's weak step, and you've found it fairly. Either the arguer is relying on a claim they don't believe, which they need to know, or there's a different, weaker premise they do believe, in which case the conclusion doesn't follow as strongly as they said. What you have not found is a reason to reject the conclusion. A bad argument for a claim tells you nothing about the claim; there may be a better one. Lesson 8 gives that mistake its name.
:::

## What people get wrong

**"An argument is a quarrel."** In everyday English it can be. In this course it never is. An argument is reasons offered for a claim, and two people can argue, in this sense, while agreeing about nearly everything and liking each other throughout. If you find yourself thinking "I don't want to argue", check whether you mean "I don't want to fight" or "I don't want to give reasons". The second is the one to worry about.

**"If there's a 'because', it's an argument."** "Because" introduces an explanation at least as often as a premise. The bakery closed because the rent went up. The pipe burst because the water froze. Neither of those is trying to convince you of anything. The test is never the word. The test is whether the thing before "because" is being established or taken for granted.

**"The conclusion is the last sentence."** It's the last sentence in textbook examples, which is where the habit comes from. In real writing it's often first, sometimes in the middle, and sometimes unstated (a passage can list reasons and let you draw the conclusion yourself, which is a rhetorical move you should notice). Find it by asking what the passage wants from you.

**"The missing premise is whatever makes the argument work."** This is the one that experienced people get wrong, because it feels like generosity. It isn't. If you add a premise the arguer wouldn't accept, you're no longer evaluating their argument; you're evaluating one you built. Add the premise they'd sign. If that premise makes the argument work, good. If it doesn't, you've found the gap, and you can show it to them in their own terms, which is the only way anyone has ever been persuaded of anything.

**"Finding a missing premise means the argument is bad."** Almost every real argument has one. Finding it is the beginning of evaluation, not the end. Harry's argument has a missing premise and is a good argument; it just reaches a little less far than its one-line version claimed.

:::callout One thing to keep separate
Steps 1 to 3 tell you what the argument is. They don't tell you whether it's any good. That takes steps 4 and 5, and you'll spend the next five lessons on them. It is tempting, having laid an argument out, to leap to a verdict. Don't yet. A clean reconstruction of an argument you disagree with is worth more than a hasty rejection of it, and the reconstruction is what the rest of the course works on.
:::

## Practice

Four passages. For each, decide whether it's an argument, an explanation, or a report. For the arguments, write the standard form with any missing premise in brackets. Do all four on paper before you open the answers.

(A) "The forecast says thunderstorms from three. Move the barbecue to Sunday."

(B) "The match was abandoned because lightning was seen within five miles of the ground."

(C) "According to the club's statement, the pitch will be relaid over the summer."

(D) "He's never once returned a book on time. I wouldn't lend him that one."

:::checkpoint Open this when your four answers are written.
(A) Argument. Conclusion: move the barbecue to Sunday. Premise: the forecast says thunderstorms from three. Missing: [you can't hold a barbecue in a thunderstorm] and, more quietly, [the forecast is reliable enough to plan by]. That second one is the interesting gap.

(B) Explanation. The abandonment is taken as known; the lightning is the reason it happened. Nobody is being persuaded that the match was abandoned.

(C) Report. It tells you what the club said. It offers no reason to believe the pitch will be relaid beyond the club having said so, and takes no position.

(D) Argument, no indicator words, conclusion last. Premise: he's never once returned a book on time. Missing: [someone who never returns books on time is likely to keep or lose this one]. Written down, the premise is plausible but has an obvious exception: the books he was slow with may have been ones he didn't care about, and this one might be different. Whether that matters is a question for step 5, and you couldn't have asked it until the premise was on the page.
:::

1. Find one argument you actually met this week. A news article, a message from someone, an advert, something a colleague said, a post you disagreed with. It must be real and it must be trying to get you to accept something. Write down where it came from and the date.

Robin Smith's SEP entry "Aristotle's Logic" (rev. 2022) gives the alternative rendering "a deduction is speech in which, certain things having been supposed, something different from those supposed results of necessity because of their being so".
