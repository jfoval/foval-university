# Sources: Logic and Argument

*Stage 1 research. Written before any lesson is drafted. This is a working document: for each source, say what it is good for and what it establishes.*

Research date: September 2026. Where a figure below is quoted, it was read from the paper or book itself (full text, extracted from the PDF) unless marked "via secondary source". Those marked items must be checked against the primary source in the fact-check pass before a lesson quotes them. The How to Learn Anything research file shows how many Stage 1 figures drifted from the papers; this file tries to say, for every number, exactly where it was read.

## Canonical textbooks

- **Hurley, *A Concise Introduction to Logic* (13th ed. 2018; 14th ed. 2024, Cengage; Lori Watson co-author on recent editions).** The most widely assigned US intro-logic text. Best for: the clean definitions (valid/invalid, sound/unsound for deduction; strong/weak, cogent/uncogent for induction, all in ch. 1) and the fallacy taxonomy in ch. 3, which sorts twenty-two informal fallacies into five groups: relevance (appeal to force, appeal to pity, appeal to the people, argument against the person in abusive, circumstantial and tu quoque forms, accident, straw man, missing the point, red herring); weak induction (appeal to unqualified authority, appeal to ignorance, hasty generalization, false cause, slippery slope, weak analogy); presumption (begging the question, complex question, false dichotomy, suppressed evidence); ambiguity (equivocation, amphiboly); illicit transference (composition, division). Table of contents: Part I Informal Logic (1 Basic Concepts; 2 Language: Meaning and Definition; 3 Informal Fallacies); Part II Formal Logic (4 Categorical Propositions; 5 Categorical Syllogisms; 6 Propositional Logic; 7 Natural Deduction; 8 Predicate Logic); Part III Inductive Logic (9 Analogy and Legal and Moral Reasoning; 10 Causality and Mill's Methods; 11 Probability; 12 Statistical Reasoning; 13 Hypothetical/Scientific Reasoning; 14 Science and Superstition). We draw on 1, 3, 4 to 5 (lightly), 6 (lightly), 9 to 11. Hurley is also the source for the correction of the "deduction goes general to specific, induction specific to general" myth: his distinction is about whether the premises are *claimed* to make the conclusion certain or only probable (ch. 1.3). The fallacy list above was confirmed against several course summaries of the book rather than the 14th edition itself; treat section numbers as "verify".
- **Copi, Cohen & McMahon, *Introduction to Logic* (14th ed., Pearson 2011; Routledge reprint; 15th ed. with Rodych, 2019).** The other standard text, and the one Hamblin was reacting to in its early editions (Copi 1961). Structure: Part I Logic and Language (1 Basic Logical Concepts; 2 Analyzing Arguments; 3 Language and Definitions; 4 Fallacies); Part II Deduction (5 Categorical Propositions; 6 Categorical Syllogisms; 7 Syllogisms in Ordinary Language; 8 Symbolic Logic; 9 Methods of Deduction; 10 Quantification Theory); Part III Induction (11 Analogical Reasoning; 12 Causal Reasoning; 13 Science and Hypothesis; 14 Probability). Ch. 4 groups fallacies as Relevance (R), Defective Induction (D), Presumption (P), Ambiguity (A); from memory of the 14th ed. the codes run R1 appeal to the populace, R2 appeal to emotion, R3 red herring, R4 straw man, R5 attack on the person, R6 appeal to force, R7 missing the point; D1 argument from ignorance, D2 appeal to inappropriate authority, D3 false cause, D4 hasty generalization; P1 accident, P2 complex question, P3 begging the question; A1 equivocation, A2 amphiboly, A3 accent, A4 composition, A5 division. **Codes not re-verified this session; check ch. 4 before a lesson cites them.** Best for: ch. 2's argument diagramming and "analyzing arguments" material, and ch. 11 to 12 on analogy and cause, which are fuller than Hurley's. Lander University's Phil 103 (below) uses Copi & Cohen and its syllabus appendix gives a compact schema for each fallacy.
- **Weston, *A Rulebook for Arguments* (5th ed., Hackett, Feb 2018, 118 pp).** Short, cheap, and the book most often handed to students who need to *write* arguments rather than classify them. Chapters: I Short Arguments: Some General Rules; II Arguments by Example; III Arguments by Analogy; IV Arguments from Authority; V Arguments about Causes; VI Deductive Arguments; VII Extended Arguments; VIII Argumentative Essays; IX Oral Arguments; X Public Debates (new in 5th ed.); Appendix I Some Common Fallacies; Appendix II Definitions. Best for: the constructive side of the course (rules for sources: cite them, seek informed and impartial sources, cross-check; rules for causal claims; how to build an extended argument), and for the closing lessons where the learner writes an argument. The numbered rules (Rule 1 "Identify premises and conclusion" onward) could not be fetched this session; take them from the book when drafting.
- **Govier, *A Practical Study of Argument* (7th ed., enhanced, Cengage 2013).** The informal-logic movement's own textbook, Canadian, and the source of the **ARG conditions**: an argument is cogent if its premises are (A) acceptable, (R) relevant to the conclusion, and (G) provide adequate grounds for it (ch. 4). Chapters: 1 What Is an Argument? (And What Is Not?); 2 Pinning Down Argument Structure; 3 Looking at Language; 4 When Is an Argument a Good One?; 5 Premises: What to Accept and Why; 6 Working on Relevance; 7 Deductive Arguments: Categorical Logic; 8 Propositional Logic; 9 An Introduction to Inductive Arguments; 10 Causal Inductive Arguments; 11 Analogies; 12 Conductive Arguments and Counterconsiderations; Appendix A Summary of Fallacies. Best for: ch. 2 (linked vs convergent support, diagramming, missing premises), ch. 5 (when to accept a premise: common knowledge, testimony, authority), and ch. 12, which names a kind of argument the other books skip: **conductive** arguments, where several independent reasons are weighed against counter-considerations, which is what most real-life practical arguments are.
- **Sinnott-Armstrong & Fogelin, *Understanding Arguments: An Introduction to Informal Logic* (9th ed., Cengage 2015).** Widely assigned; the book behind Duke's free "Think Again" courses (below). Best for: "close analysis" of argumentative language (assuring, guarding, discounting terms) and "deep analysis" (reconstructing arguments in standard form, supplying suppressed premises). Not fetched directly; use through the Think Again videos and Van Cleave, which follows the same method.
- **Magnus, Button, Trueman, Zach et al., *forall x: Calgary* (Fall 2025, revision 2026-07-18, CC BY 4.0, forallx.openlogicproject.org).** The open formal-logic text, descended from Magnus's *forall x* (Albany) via Button's *forall x: Cambridge*. Part I Key notions of logic (1 Arguments; 2 The scope of logic; 3 Other logical notions) is exactly the formal core a general learner needs and is written plainly. Read from the PDF, ch. 2: "An argument is valid if and only if the conclusion is a consequence of the premises. An argument is invalid if and only if it is not valid, i.e., it has a counterexample." And: "validity is not about the actual truth or falsity of the sentences in the argument ... It is often said that logic doesn't care about feelings. Actually, it doesn't care about facts, either." Soundness: "an argument is sound if and only if it is both valid and all of its premises are true." And the line every lesson should echo: to rebut an argument "you can show that (one or more of) the premises are not true, or you can show that the argument is not valid. Logic, however, will only help you with the latter!" Parts II to III (truth-functional logic and truth tables) are the "go deeper" for learners who want the symbolic version; we do not teach natural deduction or predicate logic.

## Primary sources

- **Aristotle, *Prior Analytics* I (24b18 to 20) and *Sophistical Refutations* 4 to 5.** The Organon (Categories, On Interpretation, Prior Analytics, Posterior Analytics, Topics, Sophistical Refutations) is where the subject begins. Jenkinson's translation (MIT Classics): "A syllogism is discourse in which, certain things being stated, something other than what is stated follows of necessity from their being so." Robin Smith's SEP entry (rev. Nov 2022) renders it "a deduction is speech in which, certain things having been supposed, something different from those supposed results of necessity because of their being so", and notes that Aristotle "in effect supposes that all terms in syllogisms are non-empty" (existential import). Aristotle recognises three figures by the position of the middle term (SEP: fourteen valid moods in the *Prior Analytics*; the medieval names Barbara, Celarent and so on are mnemonics for his proofs). The standard count taught today is 256 possible forms (64 moods times 4 figures), of which 15 are valid without existential import and 9 more with it, 24 in all (via secondary source; confirm against Hurley ch. 5 before quoting). *Sophistical Refutations* 4 (Pickard-Cambridge): "Those ways of producing the false appearance of an argument which depend on language are six in number: they are ambiguity, amphiboly, combination, division of words, accent, form of expression." The seven "independent of language": accident; absolute vs qualified expression (secundum quid); ignorance of what refutation is (ignoratio elenchi); consequent; "assuming the original conclusion" (begging the question); "stating as cause what is not the cause"; "making of more than one question into one." Use: the opening of the fallacies lesson, to show that the list learners meet online is 2,300 years old and was built for a specific game (dialectical refutation), which is part of why it fits real life badly.
- **Mill, *A System of Logic* (1843), Book III ch. 8 "Of the Four Methods of Experimental Inquiry."** Read in Bennett's modernised text (earlymoderntexts.com, 2017). Five canons: Agreement ("If two or more instances of the phenomenon under investigation have only one circumstance in common, that one circumstance is the cause (or effect) of the given phenomenon"); Difference (an instance where the phenomenon occurs and one where it does not "have every circumstance in common except for one x that occurs only in the former"; then x "is the effect or the cause or an indispensable part of the cause"); the Joint Method of Agreement and Difference (third canon); Residues (fourth); Concomitant Variation (fifth: "If any phenomenon x varies in some specific way whenever another phenomenon y varies in some specific way, x is either a cause or an effect of y, or is causally connected with it in some other manner"). Mill's own example for Difference: a man shot through the heart. Use: the causal-reasoning lesson, as the ancestor of the controlled experiment and of "correlation is not causation" (the fifth canon's hedge is the point).
- **Toulmin, *The Uses of Argument* (Cambridge UP, 1958; updated ed. 2003).** Page references from Hitchcock's "Toulmin's Warrants" (ISSA 2002 / 2003, PDF read): data p. 97, warrant p. 98, the Harry example pp. 99 to 102. The layout: claim ("Harry is a British subject"), data or grounds ("Harry was born in Bermuda"), warrant ("A man born in Bermuda is generally a British subject"), backing (the statutes on nationality), modal qualifier ("presumably"), rebuttal ("unless both his parents were aliens..."). Toulmin: "Such a general, step-authorizing statement is called a warrant" (1958, 98 to 100, via Hitchcock). Hitchcock's clarification: a warrant is an inference-licence, not a premise, and warrants are field-dependent. Use: the reconstruction lesson, as a second layout alongside premise-conclusion standard form, and the best tool for finding the *unstated* general step in a real argument.
- **Hamblin, *Fallacies* (Methuen, 1970).** The book that started modern fallacy theory by attacking the "standard treatment". Quoted via the SEP "Fallacies" entry (Hansen, rev. Aug 2024, sec. 3.2): the typical textbook account "is as debased, worn-out and dogmatic a treatment as could be imagined", "incredibly tradition bound, yet lacking in logic and in historical sense alike" (Hamblin p. 12 via SEP). Hamblin also argued that the standard definition ("an argument that seems valid but is not") fits few items on the standard list. Use: the fallacies lesson must teach the list *and* this critique, or it fails the expert test.
- **Wason, "Reasoning about a rule", *Quarterly Journal of Experimental Psychology* 20(3), 1968, 273 to 281 (PDF read).** The four-card task: "if there is a vowel on one side of the card, then there is an even number on the other side", cards showing a vowel, a consonant, an even number, an odd number. Wason's summary of prior results: "Nearly all subjects select P, from 60 to 75 per cent select Q, only a minority select not-Q." Experiment 1 used 36 first-year psychology and statistics students at UCL. Ragni, Kola & Johnson-Laird's meta-analysis (Proc. Cognitive Science Society 2017, PDF read; 228 experiments: 104 abstract, 44 everyday, 80 deontic) gives the pooled percentages for the four canonical selections on abstract rules: p alone 36%, p and q 39%, p, q and not-q 5%, and the correct p and not-q 19%. On deontic rules ("If a person is drinking beer, then the person must be over 19"), p and not-q rises to 64%. Use: the conditionals lesson, to show that affirming the consequent is the natural error, and that content and framing change performance a great deal.
- **Evans, Barston & Pollard, "On the conflict between logic and belief in syllogistic reasoning", *Memory & Cognition* 11(3), 1983, 295 to 306.** Abstract (read from the Leeds Trinity portal): three experiments; "belief bias was more marked on invalid than on valid syllogisms"; verbal protocols were "in some cases ... rationalizations for prejudiced decisions" and in others genuine premise-to-conclusion reasoning. Materials contrasted believable conclusions ("some addictive things are not cigarettes") with unbelievable ones ("some cigarettes are not addictive") (via secondary source). The widely reproduced Experiment 1 acceptance rates, valid-believable about 89%, valid-unbelievable about 56%, invalid-believable about 71%, invalid-unbelievable about 10%, are **via secondary source**; the full text was paywalled this session. Verify against the paper's table before any lesson quotes them. Use: the validity lesson, as the evidence that "valid" and "believable" come apart in people's heads and that the fix is to ask "could the premises be true and the conclusion false?" before asking whether you like the conclusion.
- **Tversky & Kahneman, "Extensional versus intuitive reasoning: The conjunction fallacy in probability judgment", *Psychological Review* 90(4), 1983, 293 to 315 (PDF read).** Linda: "31 years old, single, outspoken and very bright. She majored in philosophy. As a student, she was deeply concerned with issues of discrimination and social justice, and also participated in anti-nuclear demonstrations." In the direct transparent test, 142 UBC undergraduates chose between "Linda is a bank teller" and "Linda is a bank teller and is active in the feminist movement"; "Overall, 85% of respondents indicated that T&F was more probable than T." The main-study incidence of violations in direct tests was 88% with "no effect of statistical sophistication in either indirect or direct tests." A betting version cut violations to 56% (n = 60); only the doctoral-level "sophisticated" group in one rating task got down to 36%. Gigerenzer and Hertwig's critique (that "probable" is read as "plausible" and that frequency wording reduces the error) is real and should be stated. Use: the probability lesson.
- **Gigerenzer & Hoffrage, "How to improve Bayesian reasoning without instruction: Frequency formats", *Psychological Review* 102(4), 1995, 684 to 704 (PDF read).** The mammography problem (adapted from Eddy 1982): base rate 1%, sensitivity 80%, false-positive rate 9.6% (9.69% in one version); correct posterior 7.8%. "Eddy (1982) reported that 95 out of 100 physicians estimated the posterior probability p(cancer|positive) to be between 70% and 80%, rather than 7.8%." Study 1 (15 problems): Bayesian answers were 16% in the standard probability format and 28% in the short probability format, versus 46% and 50% in the two frequency formats. Frequency wording used in the paper: "10 out of every 1,000 women ... 8 of every 10 women with breast cancer will get a positive mammography. 95 out of every 990 women without breast cancer will also get a positive mammography." Use: teach Bayes' rule through natural frequencies, never through the formula first.
- **Royal Statistical Society, statement on R v. Sally Clark, 23 October 2001 (PDF of the archived release read).** The expert witness "went on to square this figure to obtain a value of 1 in 73 million for the frequency of two cases of SIDS in such a family. This approach is, in general, statistically invalid. It would only be valid if SIDS cases arose independently within families." And: "The jury needs to weigh up two competing explanations for the babies' deaths: SIDS or murder ... What matters is the relative likelihood of the deaths under each explanation, not just how unlikely they are under one explanation." The RSS names this the Prosecutor's Fallacy. Use: the single best real-world case for the probability lesson, because it is documented, non-partisan, and cost someone years of liberty.
- **Mangel & Samaniego, "Abraham Wald's work on aircraft survivability", *JASA* 79(386), 1984, 259 to 267.** The primary account of Wald's Statistical Research Group work estimating aircraft vulnerability from the damage on planes that returned. Use: survivorship bias, sourced properly rather than from the internet retelling (the "armour the undamaged parts" anecdote in its popular form goes beyond what Wald's memoranda say; the lesson should stick to what Mangel & Samaniego report).
- **Harman, "The Inference to the Best Explanation", *Philosophical Review* 74(1), 1965, 88 to 95.** Names and defends the third kind of non-deductive inference: infer the hypothesis that would best explain the evidence. Use: the inductive lesson's section on explanation.
- **Dennett, *Intuition Pumps and Other Tools for Thinking* (2013), "Rapoport's Rules".** Via several secondary summaries, all agreeing: (1) re-express your target's position so clearly and fairly that they say "Thanks, I wish I'd thought of putting it that way"; (2) list any points of agreement, especially uncommon ones; (3) mention anything you learned from them; (4) only then criticise. The wording and page must be checked against the book. This is the best-known codification of the steelman and the natural spine of the reconstruction lesson. Note that "steelman" as a word is internet usage from the early 2010s and has no canonical source; the lesson should use the older academic term, the principle of charity, alongside it.

## Expert bodies and official guidance

- **SEP, "Fallacies" (Hans Hansen, first pub. 2015, rev. Aug 2024).** Working definition (sec. 1): a fallacy is "an argument that seems to be better than it really is." Sec. 2 gives the history (Aristotle's thirteen, Locke's ad arguments, Mill, Whately). Sec. 3 lays out the modern positions and coherence objections: Finocchiaro's doubt that textbook fallacies occur much in real discourse; Massey's asymmetry (there is a method for proving validity but none for proving invalidity, so "no argument can be convicted of being a fallacy on logical grounds"); Johnson & Blair's cogency criteria; Walton's dialogue types; pragma-dialectics' rule violations; Hahn & Oaksford's Bayesian account. This entry is the map for the contested-questions section below.
- **SEP, "Informal Logic" (Leo Groarke).** Definition: the study of "reasoning and inference as they occur in public discussion and debate"; history (Toulmin and Hamblin as touchstones; Kahane's *Logic and Contemporary Rhetoric* 1971; Johnson & Blair at Windsor, the *Informal Logic Newsletter* 1978 and first symposium 1980); argument diagramming with linked vs convergent premises (sec. 3.2); the acceptability/relevance/sufficiency criteria after Johnson & Blair 1977 (sec. 4.1); the four argument kinds: deductive, inductive, conductive, abductive.
- **SEP, "Aristotle's Logic" (Robin Smith, rev. 2022)** and **SEP, "Inductive Logic" (rev. Feb 2025).** The latter defines inductive logic by degree of support (premises "support the truth of its conclusion to some appropriate degree"), explains why Carnap's programme of a purely logical confirmation function is now generally regarded as unsuccessful, and presents the Bayesian framework (likelihoods, priors, likelihood ratios) with worked examples including a COVID-19 test, which is directly reusable.
- **IEP, "Fallacies" (Bradley Dowden).** Lists about 230 named fallacies, so it is the reference to link for any name a learner meets. Two lines to quote: "Most reasoning of this kind [appeal to authority] is not fallacious, and much of our knowledge properly comes from listening to authorities", and "simply applying the fallacy name to a passage cannot substitute for a detailed examination of the passage and its context." On ad hominem it gives the right test: a person's sexual conduct is irrelevant to their reasoning about Kepler but relevant to an argument that they should lead a church or city council.
- **Walton's argumentation schemes and critical questions.** Walton, *Appeal to Expert Opinion* (Penn State, 1997); Walton, *Ad Hominem Arguments* (Alabama, 1998); Walton, Reed & Macagno, *Argumentation Schemes* (Cambridge UP, 2008; 96 schemes). The scheme for expert opinion: E is an expert in domain S containing A; E asserts A; therefore A. Six critical questions: expertise (how credible is E?), field (is E an expert in the field A is in?), opinion (what did E actually assert?), trustworthiness (is E personally reliable?), consistency (is A consistent with other experts?), backup evidence (is E's assertion based on evidence?). Walton's thesis on ad hominem: no ad hominem argument is inherently fallacious; attacking a witness's honesty in cross-examination is legitimate because the claim rests on their credibility. Use: this "scheme plus critical questions" method is what we teach in place of bare labels.
- **Abrami, Bernard, Borokhovski, Waddington, Wade & Persson, "Strategies for teaching students to think critically: A meta-analysis", *Review of Educational Research* 85(2), 2015, 275 to 314.** 341 effect sizes; weighted mean g+ = 0.30. Instruction in critical thinking measurably works; the abstract reports that dialogue, exposure to authentic problems, and mentoring were the strategies associated with larger effects (via abstract; read the moderator tables before quoting specifics).

## What the field considers essential

The order below is the order the ideas depend on each other; it becomes the outline backbone. The four-unit shape (prerequisites, deduction, induction, fallacies) is shared by UNC, Duke's Think Again, Hurley and Copi; the placement of reconstruction before fallacies follows Sinnott-Armstrong and Govier.

1. **What an argument is, and what it isn't.** Premises, conclusion, indicator words; argument vs explanation vs report vs assertion (Govier ch. 1; Van Cleave ch. 1). Standard form. Enthymemes: most real arguments have a missing premise, and finding it is the first skill (Copi ch. 2; Toulmin's warrant is the same idea from another angle).
2. **Deductive validity and soundness.** Validity as "no possible case where the premises are true and the conclusion false" (forall x ch. 2); counterexample method; the four combinations of true/false premises with valid/invalid form, and the one that cannot occur. Soundness. Belief bias as the reason this needs practice (Evans et al. 1983). Logic evaluates the link, not the premises.
3. **Conditionals and the four basic forms.** Modus ponens, modus tollens, hypothetical syllogism, disjunctive syllogism, and the two famous invalid look-alikes, affirming the consequent and denying the antecedent. Necessary vs sufficient conditions; "only if"; contrapositive. Wason's task as the demonstration (Wason 1968; Ragni et al. 2017).
4. **Categorical reasoning, lightly.** All/no/some statements, the square of opposition, Venn checks for two-premise syllogisms; where "some" misleads; existential import as a footnote (Hurley chs. 4 to 5; Aristotle). One lesson, not three; a general learner needs to be able to check "All A are B, all C are B, so all A are C" and no more.
5. **Inductive strength and cogency.** Strong/weak, cogent/uncogent (Hurley 1.4); defeasibility (new evidence can weaken a strong argument, never a valid one). The three main inductive families: generalisation from samples (size, representativeness, Weston ch. II), analogy (relevant similarity, Govier ch. 11; Weston ch. III), and causal inference (Mill's methods; correlation vs causation; post hoc; confounders; Weston ch. V; Hurley ch. 10). Inference to the best explanation as the fourth (Harman 1965). Conductive arguments and counter-considerations (Govier ch. 12).
6. **Probability as it bears on argument.** The conjunction rule (Tversky & Kahneman 1983); base rates and Bayes' rule taught by natural frequencies (Gigerenzer & Hoffrage 1995; SEP Inductive Logic); the prosecutor's fallacy (RSS 2001); likelihood ratio as a measure of how much a piece of evidence should move you (Hahn & Oaksford 2007); absence of evidence as weak evidence of absence when evidence would have been expected (Oaksford & Hahn 2004). This is where "how strong is this argument?" gets a number.
7. **Reconstruction and charity.** Paraphrase to standard form; supply missing premises in the most plausible form; distinguish linked from convergent support; diagram (SEP Informal Logic 3.2; Govier ch. 2). Toulmin's layout for finding the warrant. Rapoport's rules and the principle of charity; steelman before criticising (Dennett 2013). Close analysis of guarding and assuring language (Sinnott-Armstrong & Fogelin). Then evaluate with Govier's ARG conditions.
8. **Fallacies: the names, the patterns, and the honest caveat.** The standard list by group (Hurley's five groups; Copi's four), with real names and real examples for each. Aristotle's origin of the list. Then the critique: Hamblin, Finocchiaro, Massey, Boudry et al.'s Fallacy Fork; Walton's answer (schemes plus critical questions; context decides). The practical rule that follows: a fallacy name is a hypothesis to test, not a verdict. Ad hominem vs legitimate credibility challenges; appeal to authority vs the six critical questions; slippery slope and argument from ignorance as sometimes-strong inductive arguments. The fallacy fallacy.
9. **Bad reasoning in the news and everyday life.** Evaluating sources (Weston ch. IV; CSUN objective 2); expert disagreement; statistics in headlines (relative vs absolute risk, base rates, survivorship: Mangel & Samaniego 1984); motivated reasoning as belief bias at scale; a repeatable checklist. Examples must be drawn from across the political spectrum and be documented; see scope concerns.
10. **Building your own argument.** Weston's rules for short and extended arguments; anticipating objections; conceding what should be conceded; writing a one-page argument as the course project, assessed with the ARG conditions and a fallacy check.

## Common misconceptions

- **"Valid" means "true" or "good".** Validity is a property of the link between premises and conclusion, not of the premises. A valid argument can have false premises and a false conclusion ("All fish fly; all whales are fish; so all whales fly"). The only combination ruled out is true premises with a false conclusion. Soundness is validity plus true premises (forall x ch. 2; Hurley 1.4).
- **"Logic tells you what's true."** It tells you what follows from what. forall x: logic helps you show an argument is invalid, "but not that a premise is false." The premises need evidence, which is another course (Statistics for Citizens, The Scientific Method).
- **"Deduction goes from general to particular, induction from particular to general."** Hurley corrects this explicitly: the distinction is whether the premises are claimed to guarantee the conclusion or only to make it probable. "All humans are mortal, Socrates is human, so Socrates is mortal" and "This coin came up heads 1,000 times, so the next toss is probably heads" differ in the strength claimed, not the direction of travel.
- **"If the argument is fallacious, the conclusion is false."** The fallacy fallacy. A bad argument for a claim tells you nothing about the claim; there may be a good one. Massey's asymmetry sharpens this: you cannot even show an argument invalid just by matching it to an invalid form (SEP Fallacies 3.2).
- **"Any appeal to authority is a fallacy."** IEP: "much of our knowledge properly comes from listening to authorities." The question is whether the six critical questions are satisfied (Walton). Likewise ad hominem: attacking a witness's honesty is relevant when the claim rests on the witness's word (Walton 1998; IEP).
- **"Slippery slope" and "argument from ignorance" are always fallacies.** Both are inductive patterns that can be strong or weak (Oaksford & Hahn 2004; Hahn & Oaksford 2007). "There is no evidence the drug is harmful" is weak if nobody looked and strong after large trials.
- **"Begs the question" means "raises the question".** In logic it names circular reasoning: the conclusion is assumed in a premise (Aristotle's "assuming the original conclusion"; Hurley 3.4). The lesson should acknowledge the everyday usage and keep the two apart.
- **Spotting a fallacy name settles the matter.** IEP: applying the name "cannot substitute for a detailed examination of the passage and its context." Boudry et al.: nearly every named fallacy has a cogent cousin with the same surface form.
- **A believable conclusion means a good argument.** Belief bias (Evans et al. 1983). The correction is procedural: test the link before you consult your opinion of the conclusion.
- **More detail makes a story more probable.** The conjunction fallacy: adding conditions can only lower probability, yet 85% of a sample judged "bank teller and feminist" more probable than "bank teller" (Tversky & Kahneman 1983).
- **A positive test on a rare condition means you probably have it.** Base-rate neglect; Eddy's physicians said 70 to 80% when the answer was 7.8% (via Gigerenzer & Hoffrage 1995). The fix is counting in natural frequencies.
- **"Correlation isn't causation" as a conversation-ender.** Mill's fifth canon says concomitant variation is evidence of *some* causal connection; the work is finding which (direct, reverse, common cause, chance). The slogan is a starting question, not an answer.
- **"Some" implies "not all".** In logic "some A are B" is compatible with "all A are B"; readers import the conversational implicature. Matters for syllogism checking.

## Contested questions and the positions on each

Classified per standards 3.1. None of these is a political or religious question; they are live disagreements inside the discipline, and the course should teach them as such rather than pick a side silently.

1. **Is the traditional fallacy taxonomy coherent, and should we teach it?** (Contested empirical and theoretical question within the field.) *Sceptics:* Hamblin (1970) showed the standard treatment is neither historically nor logically grounded; Finocchiaro (1981, *American Philosophical Quarterly* 18: 13 to 22) argued that textbook fallacies are rare in real discourse and preferred to speak of "fallacious arguments"; Massey (1981, *Midwest Studies in Philosophy* 6: 489 to 500) argued no argument can be convicted of fallacy on purely logical grounds; Boudry, Paglieri & Pigliucci (2015, *Argumentation* 29(4): 431 to 456, "The Fake, the Flimsy, and the Fallacious"; popularised as "The Fallacy Fork", *Skeptical Inquirer* 2017) argue that either a fallacy is defined strictly enough to be always bad, in which case it almost never occurs, or loosely enough to occur, in which case it is often cogent, so fallacy theory should be dropped in favour of case-by-case evaluation. *Defenders and reformers:* Walton (1995 *A Pragmatic Theory of Fallacy*; 2008 schemes) keeps the names but redefines a fallacy as a misuse of an argumentation scheme in a given dialogue type, evaluated by critical questions; pragma-dialectics (van Eemeren & Grootendorst, 1992 *Argumentation, Communication, and Fallacies*; 2004 *A Systematic Theory of Argumentation*) redefines fallacies as violations of ten rules for a critical discussion (e.g. the freedom rule, the burden-of-proof rule), which covers the traditional list and adds moves it missed; Johnson & Blair (1977 *Logical Self-Defense*) sort fallacies as failures of acceptability, relevance, or sufficiency. *What would settle it:* corpus studies of how often each pattern occurs in real argument and how often instances are actually defective; studies of whether fallacy instruction improves reasoning (Abrami 2015 shows critical-thinking instruction helps but does not isolate fallacy labels). *Our line:* teach the names because learners will meet them and because they are a good index of patterns; teach each with its critical questions and its cogent twin; teach the critique openly. This is close to Walton's position and to Boudry's practical advice, and both camps would recognise it as fair.
2. **How much formal logic does a general learner need?** (Contested empirical question.) *Formal-first view:* logic departments and texts like forall x and Copi Part II hold that symbolic logic gives the only precise account of validity and trains rigour. *Informal-logic view:* Johnson & Blair, Govier, Scriven, and Kahane argued from the 1970s that formal logic transfers poorly to real arguments, whose problems are usually acceptability and relevance, not form. *Evidence:* Cheng, Holyoak, Nisbett & Oliver (1986, *Cognitive Psychology* 18(3): 293 to 328) found that training in abstract logic did little for selection-task performance while training in pragmatic schemas (permissions, obligations) helped; the specific classroom result (a semester of logic producing only a small improvement) is via secondary source and must be read from the paper. Abrami et al. (2015) found critical-thinking instruction works overall (g+ = 0.30), with dialogue and authentic problems mattering most. *What would settle it:* transfer studies comparing formal and informal instruction on real-argument evaluation at delay. *Our line:* teach validity, the four forms and their look-alikes, and Venn checks; skip natural deduction and predicate logic; link forall x for those who want more. Say why.
3. **Which theory of argument: logic, dialectic, or rhetoric?** (Theoretical value question within the field; describe, do not resolve.) Informal logic (Johnson & Blair) evaluates arguments as products by ARS/ARG criteria; pragma-dialectics (Amsterdam school) evaluates argumentation as a procedure for resolving a difference of opinion; rhetoric (Perelman & Olbrechts-Tyteca 1958; Tindale 2007 *Fallacies and Argument Appraisal*) evaluates by audience and persuasion; Walton's dialogue-type theory sits between. The SEP Informal Logic entry (sec. 5) describes attempts to combine the three. For a general learner this is a "go deeper" note, but the course should not present the logical view as the only one.
4. **Bayesian vs classical accounts of inductive strength.** (Contested empirical and theoretical question.) *Classical / Carnapian:* inductive logic should be a logic of confirmation with degrees of support fixed by the language; the SEP entry reports that this programme is now widely regarded as unsuccessful. *Popperian:* there is no inductive logic; science proceeds by conjecture and refutation (Popper 1959). *Bayesian:* argument strength is change in probability given evidence, with likelihood ratios doing the work (SEP Inductive Logic; Hahn & Oaksford 2007), and the "fallacies" of ignorance, circularity and slippery slope come out as strong or weak by the same measure. *Objection to the Bayesian view:* the priors problem (where do the starting probabilities come from?), and the subjective/objective split the SEP entry sets aside. *A related empirical dispute:* are people irrational (Kahneman & Tversky's heuristics-and-biases reading of Linda and base-rate neglect) or well adapted to natural formats (Gigerenzer's ecological rationality, with frequency formats raising Bayesian answers from 16% to 46 to 50%)? Both are true of the data; they disagree about the interpretation. *Our line:* teach Bayes by frequencies as a tool, present likelihood ratio as one good measure of argument strength, and say plainly that the philosophical foundations are argued over.
5. **Existential import.** (Settled as a difference of convention, not a live dispute.) Aristotle's logic assumes non-empty terms and gets 24 valid syllogisms; modern logic does not and gets 15. State it in a footnote; do not present it as a controversy.

## Open concerns about scope

- **The news-and-everyday-life lesson touches sensitive domains** (politics, recent events) even though the course as a whole does not. Examples must be documented and balanced: for each pattern, either a non-partisan case (Sally Clark, Wald, Eddy's physicians, Linda) or a matched pair from opposite political sides. Recommend running the neutrality audit on that lesson and on the fallacies lesson regardless of the course-level `sensitive_domain: false` flag.
- **Real examples for the fallacies lesson are not yet sourced.** This file provides documented cases for the probabilistic fallacies and survivorship, but not for straw man, ad hominem, slippery slope, false dilemma, equivocation, and so on. The drafting session must find named, dated, citable instances (court opinions, Hansard or Congressional Record, retracted papers, advertising rulings) rather than invented ones. Budget time for this.
- **Length.** Ten essential topics at one lesson each is about ten lessons; categorical logic and probability could each run long. Estimate 12 hours for the learner. If lessons 3 and 4 (conditionals; categorical) prove heavy, merge them into one "forms that fool people" lesson rather than adding a third.
