# Outline: Logic and Argument

*Stage 2. Built from SOURCES.md. Ten lessons, a final test, and a project in which the learner takes apart a real argument and answers it. The sequence follows the dependency order in "What the field considers essential": first what an argument is and how to find its missing step, then the deductive test of the link (validity, the conditional forms, categorical forms), then the inductive test (strength, cause, explanation), then probability, which is where strength gets a number. Only then reconstruction and charity, because you cannot steelman what you cannot yet evaluate, and only after that the fallacy names, because a learner who meets the names before the tests uses them as verdicts. The last two lessons turn outward (the news) and inward (the learner's own argument). Conditionals and categorical logic stay separate: each needs its own worked examples and its own practice, and each reaches a natural stopping point on its own. Categorical is the shortest lesson in the course by design.*

**Conventions for every lesson:**

- **Open with a real argument**, quoted or closely paraphrased, that the learner could plausibly have met that week, then show what checking it looks like. Never open with a definition.
- **One method, built up across the course.** Lesson 1 introduces a six-step check and every later lesson fills in one step: (1) find the conclusion; (2) find the premises; (3) supply what's missing; (4) test the link (is it valid, or is it strong?); (5) test the premises (are they acceptable?); (6) only now consult your opinion of the conclusion. The learner should be able to recite it from lesson 3 onward, and lesson 7 completes it with the ARG conditions. The refrain from *forall x* runs through the whole course: logic tests the link; the premises need evidence, and that is another course.
- **Standard form is written the same way everywhere:** numbered premises, a line, then "C:" for the conclusion; unstated premises in square brackets. No symbols beyond the English words "if", "then", "not", "or", "all", "no", "some". Learners who want the symbolic version are pointed to *forall x* in "Go deeper".
- **Example arguments are of two kinds only:** documented cases named and dated in SOURCES.md (Wason, Linda, Eddy's physicians, Sally Clark, Wald, Toulmin's Harry, Mill's examples, Aristotle's list), or plainly hypothetical everyday cases (cars, cooking, weather, sport, house-buying, a landlord, a school timetable). No invented political, religious, or culture-war examples anywhere in the course. Lessons 8 and 9 have a stricter rule; see the neutrality notes.
- **No religious claims as specimens.** Standard logic textbooks use "God exists" or "the Bible is true" as stock examples of circularity or of value disagreement. We do not. Per standards 3.7, neutral courses neither assert nor mock religious claims, and a fallacy specimen is a kind of mockery.
- **Every lesson has a "Do it now"** in which the learner works on a real argument of their own choosing (from something they read or heard that week), so by lesson 10 they have a folder of checked arguments to pick the project from. Lesson 1 tells them to start the folder.
- **Predict blocks go before every empirical result** (Wason's percentages, Linda's 85%, Eddy's 7.8%, the Evans belief-bias pattern) and before the reveal in every worked example with a gap. **Checkpoint blocks go after every definition** that a learner is likely to misread (valid, sound, strong, cogent, necessary, sufficient, "only if", "some", warrant, acceptable). Two or three predicts and two or three checkpoints per lesson; placements are named below.
- **Sources are cited by author and year in the text** and listed in `## Sources`. Figures are quoted exactly as SOURCES.md records them. The items still marked "via secondary source" (the Evans et al. 1983 cell percentages; the 15/9/24 syllogism count; Cheng et al. 1986's classroom result; Dennett's exact wording of Rapoport's rules; the Copi fallacy codes; Hurley's section numbers; Weston's numbered rules) must either be read from the source at draft time or left out. They must not appear in a lesson marked only "via secondary source"; unlike How to Learn Anything, where the flagged items were effect sizes, here they are the kind of thing a learner will repeat, so the bar is read-it-or-omit-it.
- **Any new documented example found at draft time** (a court opinion, an advertising ruling, a retracted paper, a headline) is added to SOURCES.md before the lesson cites it, with the same "where was this read" note as the rest of that file.
- **The one disclaimer** (education, not personal medical or legal advice) appears once, in lesson 6, where the screening and courtroom cases live.

## Lesson sequence

### 1. What an argument is, and how to find the missing step (~35 min)
- **Objectives:** identify the conclusion and premises of a passage and rewrite it in standard form; distinguish an argument from an explanation, a report, and a bare assertion; supply the most plausible missing premise of an enthymeme.
- **Core ideas:** an argument is reasons offered for a conclusion; everything else that looks like one (an explanation of why something happened, a report of what someone said, a conditional on its own, a flat assertion) isn't, and the test is whether the passage is trying to *establish* the claim or take it as given (Govier ch. 1; Van Cleave ch. 1). Indicator words ("so", "therefore", "because", "since") help and are unreliable: "because" introduces an explanation as often as a premise. Standard form. Most real arguments leave a premise unstated (Copi ch. 2 on enthymemes), and the first skill is to write the missing one down in the form the arguer would accept. Aristotle's definition of a deduction ("certain things being stated, something other than what is stated follows of necessity") introduced as the oldest statement of the idea, so the learner knows the subject has a history.
- **Mechanism:** an argument has a direction (from what's offered to what's claimed) and an explanation has the opposite direction (from what's accepted to why). Same sentence, different job. Missing premises are missing because the arguer thought them too obvious to say; that's exactly where the weak step usually hides, because obvious-to-the-arguer is where nobody checked.
- **Worked examples:** (a) simple: "The roads are icy. Leave ten minutes early." Find the conclusion, find the premise, write the unstated one ("Icy roads make the journey slower") in brackets. Then the same words as an explanation: "We left early because the roads were icy", and show the test that tells them apart. (b) With a wrinkle: Toulmin's Harry (SOURCES: Hitchcock 2002 on Toulmin 1958 pp. 97 to 102): "Harry was born in Bermuda, so Harry is a British subject." The missing step is a general rule ("a man born in Bermuda is generally a British subject"), and once written down it visibly has exceptions ("unless both his parents were aliens"). The point: writing the missing premise down is what makes the exception visible. Toulmin's full layout is held back for lesson 7. (c) A passage with the conclusion stated first and no indicator words at all, so the learner has to find it by asking "what is this trying to get me to accept?"
- **Predict:** before revealing whether "because" passages are arguments or explanations, ask the learner to sort three. **Checkpoint:** after standard form, "which of these four passages is not an argument, and why?"
- **Misconceptions:** "an argument is a quarrel"; "if there's a 'because' it's an argument"; "the conclusion is the last sentence"; "the missing premise is whatever makes the argument work" (no: the most plausible thing the arguer would accept, which sometimes doesn't make it work, and then you've found the problem).
- **Practice:** four short passages (two arguments, one explanation, one report), sorted and, where arguments, put in standard form with missing premises bracketed. Then "Do it now": the learner takes one argument they met this week, writes it in standard form, and starts the folder they'll use for the project.
- **Sources:** Govier ch. 1 to 2; Van Cleave ch. 1; Copi, Cohen & McMahon ch. 2; Hurley ch. 1; Toulmin 1958 via Hitchcock 2002; Aristotle, *Prior Analytics* I 24b18 to 20 (Jenkinson / Smith 2022).
- **Connects:** introduces the six-step check with steps 1 to 3 filled in; lesson 2 fills step 4 for deductive arguments.

### 2. Valid, sound, and why your opinion of the conclusion gets in the way (~40 min)
- **Objectives:** explain validity as the absence of any case where the premises are true and the conclusion false; construct a counterexample to show an argument invalid; compare valid/invalid with true/false premises and identify the one combination that cannot occur; predict the effect of belief bias on a judgement of validity.
- **Core ideas:** validity is a property of the link, not the premises: "logic doesn't care about feelings. Actually, it doesn't care about facts, either" (*forall x* ch. 2). Invalid means there is a counterexample, a possible case with true premises and a false conclusion. Soundness is validity plus true premises, and only sound arguments guarantee their conclusions. The four-cell table (true/false premises against valid/invalid): every cell can occur except true premises, valid form, false conclusion. The correction of the "general to particular" myth: deductive and inductive differ in what the premises are *claimed* to do (guarantee vs make probable), not in the direction of travel (Hurley ch. 1). Belief bias: people accept invalid arguments with believable conclusions and reject valid arguments with unbelievable ones; the effect was larger on invalid syllogisms, and some verbal protocols were rationalisations after the fact (Evans, Barston & Pollard 1983, abstract). The procedural fix is the six-step check's step 6: test the link before you consult your opinion.
- **Mechanism:** a valid form is a shape; the counterexample method works by keeping the shape and swapping in content that makes the premises obviously true and the conclusion obviously false. If that can be done, the shape doesn't guarantee anything, and neither did the original. Belief bias exists because the mind checks the conclusion against memory first, which is fast, and the link second, which is slow, and it often stops after the first check.
- **Worked examples:** (a) simple: "All fish fly; all whales are fish; so all whales fly." Valid, two false premises, false conclusion. Then the same shape with true premises to show the shape is what's valid. (b) With a wrinkle: an Evans-style pair. One valid argument whose conclusion the learner will dislike (unbelievable but validly derived from its premises) and one invalid argument whose conclusion the learner will like. The learner rates both before the pattern is named. Use Evans's own material type ("some addictive things are not cigarettes" vs "some cigarettes are not addictive") only if the cell percentages have been read from the paper; otherwise use the pattern with everyday content and cite only the abstract's findings. (c) The counterexample method as a worked example with a gap: show the shape of "All A are B; some B are C; so some A are C", give the learner the first substitution, ask them to find the second before revealing one.
- **Predict:** before revealing Evans's finding, "which of these two arguments will most people accept?" **Checkpoint:** after the four-cell table, "a valid argument has a false conclusion; what do you know about its premises?" And after soundness: "sound but the conclusion feels wrong: which step of the check do you go back to?"
- **Misconceptions:** "valid means true"; "valid means good"; "a false conclusion means the argument is invalid"; "logic tells you what's true" (it tells you what follows; *forall x*: it helps show an argument invalid but not that a premise is false); the general-to-particular myth.
- **Practice:** six short arguments to sort into the four cells, with a counterexample written for each invalid one. Then "Do it now": the learner returns to their folder argument from lesson 1 and asks the one question: could the premises all be true and the conclusion still false? Write the case if so.
- **Sources:** *forall x: Calgary* ch. 1 to 3; Hurley ch. 1 (1.3 to 1.4, section numbers to verify); Evans, Barston & Pollard 1983 (abstract only unless the table has been read); Copi ch. 1.
- **Connects:** fills step 4 for deduction; lesson 3 gives the four shapes worth memorising and the two that fool people.
- **Spaced review of:** argument vs explanation (lesson 1), applied to a passage where the "because" turns out to be a premise.

### 3. Conditionals: the four forms and the two that fool almost everyone (~45 min)
- **Objectives:** identify modus ponens, modus tollens, hypothetical syllogism, and disjunctive syllogism in everyday arguments; identify affirming the consequent and denying the antecedent and explain why each is invalid; translate "only if", "unless", and necessary/sufficient statements into "if, then" form correctly; explain what the Wason task shows about how people test conditionals.
- **Core ideas:** "If P then Q" says P is sufficient for Q and Q is necessary for P, and nothing about what happens when P is false. The four valid forms and the two invalid look-alikes, each shown as a shape and each with a counterexample for the invalid ones. The contrapositive ("if not Q then not P") is the same claim; the converse is not. "Only if" reverses the direction; "unless" is "if not". Wason's selection task (1968): four cards, rule "if a vowel on one side then an even number on the other"; the correct turn is the vowel card and the odd-number card. Pooled results on abstract rules (Ragni, Kola & Johnson-Laird 2017, 228 experiments): P alone 36%, P and Q 39%, the correct P and not-Q 19%. On a deontic rule ("if drinking beer, then over 19") the correct choice rises to 64%. The lesson's reading: the natural error is to look for confirming cases (affirming the consequent) rather than the one case that could break the rule (modus tollens), and content that gives the rule a purpose fixes much of it.
- **Mechanism:** a conditional is a promise about one situation (P true) and is silent about the rest. Affirming the consequent treats the promise as running both ways. Modus tollens is the search for the breaking case, which is the same move as a counterexample in lesson 2 and, in lesson 5, the same move as a controlled experiment. Wason shows the mind's default is to seek matches, not breaks; the deontic result shows that when the rule is a permission with a cheater to catch, the mind finds the break easily.
- **Worked examples:** (a) simple: a car that won't start. "If the battery is dead, the lights won't come on. The lights come on. So the battery isn't dead" (modus tollens, valid). Then: "The lights don't come on. So the battery is dead" (affirming the consequent, invalid: a blown fuse is the counterexample). (b) With a wrinkle: Wason's four cards laid out for the learner to choose, as a predict block, before the percentages are shown; then the beer-and-age version, chosen again, then the 19% vs 64% contrast and what it means. (c) "Only if" and necessary/sufficient: "You'll pass only if you sit the exam" translated correctly (sitting is necessary, not sufficient), against the common misreading; one "unless" sentence translated.
- **Predict:** the four cards; then, before the deontic result, "will people do better or worse on the beer rule?" **Checkpoint:** after necessary/sufficient, "'You can vote only if you are 18.' Is being 18 necessary or sufficient for voting?" And after the two invalid forms: a five-second identification of a form by shape.
- **Misconceptions:** "if P then Q" implies "if Q then P"; "if P then Q" implies "if not P then not Q"; "only if" means "if"; "the Wason task shows people can't reason" (it shows what they reason about by default, and that framing moves it from 19% to 64%).
- **Practice:** ten short conditional arguments to label by form and mark valid or invalid, mixed so the invalid ones are not obvious by position. Three "only if"/"unless" sentences to translate. Then "Do it now": find one conditional claim in something you read this week, write its contrapositive and its converse, and say which one the writer seems to want you to accept.
- **Sources:** Wason 1968; Ragni, Kola & Johnson-Laird 2017; Hurley ch. 6 (lightly); *forall x* ch. 1 to 3; Copi ch. 8 (lightly); Cheng et al. 1986 only if read from the paper.
- **Connects:** the shapes are the deductive step 4 made fast; lesson 4 does the same for all/no/some. Modus tollens returns as the shape of a controlled experiment (lesson 5) and of a strong argument from ignorance (lesson 8).
- **Spaced review of:** counterexample method (lesson 2), now used on the two invalid forms; argument vs explanation (lesson 1) with a conditional that is an explanation, not an argument.

### 4. All, no, some: checking a syllogism in two minutes (~30 min)
- **Objectives:** translate everyday statements into the four categorical forms (all A are B; no A are B; some A are B; some A are not B); use a three-circle Venn diagram to test whether a two-premise syllogism is valid; explain why "some A are B" does not mean "some A are not B".
- **Core ideas:** the four standard forms and what each says about the circles. Contradictories on the square of opposition: "all A are B" is denied by "some A are not B", not by "no A are B" (which is the mistake that starts most bad rebuttals). The Venn method: shade for "all" and "no", place an X for "some", then read off whether the conclusion is already drawn. The example that catches everyone: "All A are B; all C are B; so all A are C" (all dogs are mammals, all cats are mammals, so all dogs are cats), the undistributed middle. Logic's "some" means "at least one" and is compatible with "all"; the "not all" reading is a conversational implicature (the misconception list in SOURCES). Existential import as a footnote: Aristotle assumed non-empty terms and counted more valid forms than modern logic does; the difference is a convention, not a controversy (SEP Smith 2022; the 15/9/24 count only if confirmed against Hurley ch. 5). Why the course stops here: the informal-logic view (Johnson & Blair, Govier) that most real argument fails on acceptability and relevance rather than form, against the formal-first view that symbolic logic alone gives rigour; Cheng et al. 1986 as the evidence that abstract-logic training transfers poorly, quoted only from the paper. Contested question 2 in SOURCES, stated in both camps' terms, and the course's line said openly: the four conditional forms, the Venn check, and *forall x* for those who want more.
- **Mechanism:** a Venn diagram is a picture of every possible case at once. If the premises, once drawn, have already drawn the conclusion, no counterexample is possible and the argument is valid. If the conclusion needs something not yet drawn, that gap is the counterexample. So this is lesson 2's method done with pencil instead of imagination.
- **Worked examples:** (a) simple: the dogs-and-cats syllogism, diagrammed, showing that the two "all" premises leave the dog-not-cat region unshaded. (b) With a wrinkle: a valid syllogism with a "some" premise, where the X has to go on a line because the premises don't say which sub-region it's in, and the learner has to decide whether the conclusion is still forced. Then the everyday version: "Some of the applicants are qualified. All the qualified applicants have degrees. So some applicants have degrees" (valid), against "Some applicants are qualified, so some are not" (not stated; implicature).
- **Predict:** before the Venn reveal, "is the dogs-and-cats argument valid? Most people say no because the conclusion is absurd; can you say why the form fails?" **Checkpoint:** after the square of opposition, "someone says 'all the trains were late'. What is the one thing you need to show to prove them wrong?" And after "some": "'Some of the students passed.' Does that tell you any failed?"
- **Misconceptions:** "some" implies "not all"; "no A are B" is the denial of "all A are B"; "a syllogism with a believable conclusion is valid" (lesson 2's belief bias, again); existential import as a live dispute.
- **Practice:** six two-premise syllogisms to test by Venn, at least two of which are valid with unbelievable conclusions and two invalid with believable ones (interleaved with lesson 3's conditional forms so the learner has to choose the tool). Then "Do it now": find one "all" or "no" claim in the week's reading and write down exactly what one case would refute it.
- **Sources:** Hurley chs. 4 to 5 (lightly); Copi chs. 5 to 6 (lightly); Aristotle, *Prior Analytics* via Smith 2022 (SEP); Govier ch. 7; SEP Informal Logic (Groarke) on the informal-logic movement; Cheng, Holyoak, Nisbett & Oliver 1986; Abrami et al. 2015 (abstract-level only).
- **Connects:** closes the deductive unit; the learner now has two fast tools for step 4. Lesson 5 opens the other kind of link, where a counterexample doesn't sink the argument.
- **Spaced review of:** the counterexample idea (lesson 2) as what a Venn gap is; modus tollens (lesson 3) as the shape of "one late train refutes 'all on time'".

### 5. Inductive strength: samples, analogies, causes, and best explanations (~50 min)
- **Objectives:** compare deductive validity with inductive strength and explain why new evidence can weaken a strong argument but never a valid one; evaluate a generalisation from a sample by size and representativeness; evaluate an argument from analogy by the relevance of the similarities; apply Mill's method of difference and concomitant variation to a causal claim and name the four explanations of a correlation; identify an inference to the best explanation and a conductive argument.
- **Core ideas:** an inductive argument claims only that the premises make the conclusion probable; strong/weak is a matter of degree, cogent is strong with acceptable premises (Hurley ch. 1). Defeasibility: adding a premise can turn a strong argument weak, which is why real evidence is always provisional and why "the argument was strong at the time" is a coherent thing to say. The three main families. Generalisation: sample size, representativeness, and the questions to ask of any survey (Weston ch. II; Hurley ch. 12 lightly). Analogy: the argument is only as good as the relevance of the shared features to the conclusion, and one relevant difference outweighs many irrelevant similarities (Govier ch. 11; Weston ch. III; Copi ch. 11). Cause: Mill's methods (1843, Book III ch. 8) in Bennett's wording, with the method of difference as the ancestor of the controlled experiment, and concomitant variation as the ancestor of "correlation is evidence of *some* causal connection". The four readings of a correlation (A causes B, B causes A, C causes both, chance) and post hoc. The fourth kind: inference to the best explanation (Harman 1965) with the criteria the lesson will use (explains more, explains it more simply, fits what else we know). Govier's conductive argument (ch. 12): independent reasons weighed against counter-considerations, which is what most practical decisions are, and where "but on the other hand" is a legitimate move, not a concession of defeat.
- **Mechanism:** a deductive link holds in every possible case; an inductive link holds in most of the cases the premises describe, so its strength depends on how well the premises cover the case in hand. That's why size and representativeness matter for samples (coverage), why relevance matters for analogy (the shared feature has to be the one doing the causal work), and why the method of difference is so strong (it isolates the one feature that varies). IBE is inductive because a better explanation could always turn up.
- **Worked examples:** (a) simple: a coin that came up heads 1,000 times, so the next toss will probably be heads: strong; then add the premise that the coin is now known to be fair and the argument weakens, which shows defeasibility on one line. Then a small-sample generalisation (twelve friends surveyed about a product) improved step by step: bigger, then representative, then the remaining weakness named. (b) With a wrinkle: Mill's own method-of-difference example, the man shot through the heart, laid out as the canon states it; then a correlation the learner must sort into the four readings, where the honest answer is "a common cause is more likely, and here is the test that would tell". The correlation must be a documented one, added to SOURCES.md at draft time, or a plainly hypothetical everyday one (shop sales and weather); never a social-policy correlation. (c) An IBE case (a house with muddy prints, a missing pie, and a dog) worked to show that the best explanation is chosen among rivals, not just fitted to the facts, and a conductive argument (whether to take a job in another city) with the reasons and counter-considerations weighed in the open.
- **Predict:** before the fair-coin premise is added, "does adding evidence ever make an argument worse?" Before the correlation is sorted, "which of the four readings do you expect, and why?" **Checkpoint:** after the definition of strength, "an argument with true premises and a strong link: what can still go wrong?" After analogy: "the two cases share ten features and differ in one; can the argument still be weak?"
- **Misconceptions:** "induction is the weak kind of reasoning" (it's the only kind that reaches new facts); "a strong argument that turned out wrong was a bad argument"; "correlation isn't causation" as a conversation-ender (Mill's fifth canon: it's evidence of some connection; the work is which); "more similarities make a better analogy"; "a single case can't prove anything" (one decisive method-of-difference case can); "if it's not deductive, anything goes".
- **Practice:** interleaved set of eight arguments: two generalisations, two analogies, two causal claims, one IBE, one conductive, each rated strong or weak with the reason named; three of them are deductive look-alikes from lessons 2 to 4 so the learner has to decide first which kind of link is being claimed. Then "Do it now": take one causal claim from the week's reading and write the four readings of it and which single observation would rule out the most.
- **Sources:** Hurley ch. 1 and chs. 9 to 10, 12 (lightly); Weston chs. II, III, V; Govier chs. 9 to 12; Copi chs. 11 to 12; Mill 1843 Book III ch. 8 (Bennett 2017); Harman 1965; SEP Inductive Logic (definition of degree of support); SEP Informal Logic (four argument kinds).
- **Connects:** fills step 4 for the inductive case; lesson 6 gives the number. Points forward to Statistics for Citizens and The Scientific Method, which own sampling and experimental design properly.
- **Spaced review of:** deduction vs induction as a difference in what is claimed, not direction (lesson 2); modus tollens as the shape of the method of difference (lesson 3); "some" and "all" in sample claims (lesson 4).

### 6. How much should the evidence move you: base rates, conjunctions, and the prosecutor's fallacy (~50 min)
- **Objectives:** calculate a posterior probability from a base rate, a sensitivity, and a false-positive rate using natural frequencies; explain why a conjunction can never be more probable than either of its parts, and why Linda fools people; identify the prosecutor's fallacy and state the comparison that a jury should make instead; explain the likelihood ratio as a measure of how much a piece of evidence should change a belief, and apply it to an argument from absence of evidence.
- **Core ideas:** the conjunction rule: adding a detail can only lower probability, yet 85% of 142 respondents judged "bank teller and active feminist" more probable than "bank teller", with "no effect of statistical sophistication" (Tversky & Kahneman 1983); a betting version cut violations to 56%; Gigerenzer and Hertwig's objection that "probable" is read as "plausible" is stated as a real objection. Base rates: Eddy's 95 of 100 physicians said 70 to 80% when the answer was 7.8% (via Gigerenzer & Hoffrage 1995, who report it), and the fix is counting: 1,000 women, 10 with cancer of whom 8 test positive, 990 without of whom 95 test positive, so 8 of 103 positives have cancer. Frequency formats raised Bayesian answers from 16% and 28% to 46% and 50% (Gigerenzer & Hoffrage Study 1). The prosecutor's fallacy: the Sally Clark expert squared 1 in 8,543 to get 1 in 73 million, which "would only be valid if SIDS cases arose independently within families", and the jury should have weighed "the relative likelihood of the deaths under each explanation, not just how unlikely they are under one explanation" (RSS 2001). The likelihood ratio (how much more likely is this evidence if the claim is true than if it's false) as one good measure of how much an argument should move you (Hahn & Oaksford 2007; SEP Inductive Logic), and its verdict on absence of evidence: weak when nobody looked, strong when the evidence would have been expected and wasn't found (Oaksford & Hahn 2004). Contested question 4 stated in both camps' terms: Kahneman and Tversky's reading (people are systematically irrational) and Gigerenzer's (people are well adapted to natural formats), both true of the data, disagreeing about the interpretation; and, briefly, that the philosophical foundations of Bayesian confirmation (the priors problem) are argued over. The course's line: Bayes by counting as a tool; likelihood ratio as a measure; foundations left open.
- **Mechanism:** a probability is a share of cases. Conjunction shrinks the share because the second condition removes cases from the first; a story feels more probable because it matches a stereotype, and matching is not counting. Base-rate neglect happens because the test's accuracy (80%) is vivid and the base rate (1%) is not, but the posterior is a ratio of two counts and the base rate sets the size of both. The prosecutor's fallacy swaps "probability of the evidence given innocence" for "probability of innocence given the evidence", and the swap is invisible in English because both sentences contain the same words. The likelihood ratio is the honest way to compare, and a jury (or a reader) needs both explanations' likelihoods, not one.
- **Worked examples:** (a) simple: Linda, as a predict block, then the rule, then the betting version. (b) The mammography problem worked by natural frequencies first and only then shown as the formula, so the learner sees the formula as a shorthand for counting they've already done; then a second screening problem with a gap for the learner to count. (c) With a wrinkle: Sally Clark. First the squaring (independence), then the deeper error (the jury needed the probability of two deaths under murder as well as under SIDS), then the likelihood-ratio framing that makes the comparison explicit. State exactly what the RSS statement says and no more; the case's later history is not in SOURCES.md and must be added there before the lesson mentions it. (d) Absence of evidence: "there is no evidence the drug is harmful" evaluated twice, once when no trial has been run and once after large trials, with the likelihood ratio doing the work.
- **Predict:** Linda; "what did the physicians say?"; "is 1 in 73 million the chance she's innocent?" **Checkpoint:** after the conjunction rule, "which is more probable: it rains tomorrow, or it rains tomorrow and the match is cancelled?" After natural frequencies, "double the base rate to 2%; without recomputing, does the posterior go up or down?"
- **Misconceptions:** more detail makes a story more probable; a positive test on a rare condition means you probably have it; "1 in 73 million chance of two SIDS deaths" is "1 in 73 million chance of innocence"; absence of evidence is never evidence of absence; "Bayes is a formula" (it's a count).
- **Practice:** three natural-frequency problems (one medical, one non-medical such as a spam filter or a quality-control test, one where the learner must first notice a missing base rate), one conjunction problem in a new dress, and one courtroom-style problem in which the learner writes the comparison the fact-finder should make. Then "Do it now": find one number in the week's news that is a probability or a rate, and write what count it is a share of and what base rate it is silent about.
- **Sources:** Tversky & Kahneman 1983; Gigerenzer & Hoffrage 1995; Royal Statistical Society 2001; Hahn & Oaksford 2007; Oaksford & Hahn 2004; SEP Inductive Logic (rev. 2025); Hurley ch. 11 (lightly).
- **Safety note (once, here):** education, not personal medical or legal advice; the lesson does not tell a learner how to read their own test result.
- **Connects:** gives step 4 a number for the inductive case. Points forward to Probability and Decisions (expected value) and Statistics for Citizens.
- **Spaced review of:** strength and defeasibility (lesson 5) as what a likelihood ratio measures; affirming the consequent (lesson 3) as the deductive cousin of the prosecutor's fallacy (both run the conditional backwards); belief bias (lesson 2) as why Linda is hard.

### 7. Reconstruction and charity: the strongest version first (~45 min)
- **Objectives:** reconstruct an argument from a real passage into standard form, supplying missing premises in the most plausible form and marking linked versus convergent support; apply Toulmin's layout to find the warrant, qualifier, and rebuttal of an argument; apply the principle of charity and Rapoport's rules to an argument you disagree with; evaluate a reconstructed argument with the acceptability, relevance, and grounds conditions.
- **Core ideas:** close analysis before deep analysis (Sinnott-Armstrong & Fogelin via Think Again and Van Cleave): mark the assuring terms ("clearly", "everyone agrees"), guarding terms ("probably", "in most cases"), and discounting terms ("although", "even though"), because they tell you what the arguer thinks is weak. Diagramming: premises that only work together (linked) versus premises that each support the conclusion on their own (convergent), and why the distinction matters when one premise falls (Govier ch. 2; SEP Informal Logic 3.2). Toulmin's layout in full: claim, data, warrant, backing, qualifier, rebuttal (Toulmin 1958 pp. 97 to 102 via Hitchcock 2002), with Hitchcock's point that a warrant is an inference-licence, not a premise, and that warrants are field-dependent (what licenses a step in law doesn't in medicine). The principle of charity: reconstruct the version the arguer would sign, and if there's a choice between a version that's valid with a doubtful premise and one that's strong with a plausible premise, take the one the arguer would take. Rapoport's rules (Dennett 2013; exact wording to be read from the book): re-express the position so well the other side thanks you; list agreements; say what you learned; only then criticise. "Steelman" as the internet word for the older principle. Then step 5 of the check: Govier's ARG conditions (ch. 4): premises acceptable (ch. 5 on when to accept: common knowledge, testimony, authority, with the sources of belief the CSUN syllabus lists), relevant (ch. 6), and adequate grounds (which is the validity/strength test from lessons 2 to 6). Contested question 3 (logic vs dialectic vs rhetoric as the theory of argument) as a "go deeper" note: this course evaluates arguments as products; other schools evaluate the discussion or the persuasion, and the SEP Informal Logic entry maps them.
- **Mechanism:** a bad reconstruction is the commonest way to lose an argument you should have won: you refute a version the other person never held, they notice, and the exchange is over. Charity is not politeness; it's accuracy about what you're evaluating. Toulmin's warrant is the missing premise of lesson 1 seen from the arguer's side, as a rule they're relying on, and the rebuttal slot is where the exceptions to that rule get written down, which is why the layout finds weaknesses that a premise list hides.
- **Worked examples:** (a) simple: Toulmin's Harry laid out in all six slots, then the same argument in standard form, with the warrant appearing as the bracketed premise, so the learner sees the two layouts are one argument viewed twice. (b) With a wrinkle: a real passage (from Van Cleave ch. 1's CC BY exercises, or a documented public text added to SOURCES.md at draft time; not a political op-ed) that contains an assuring term hiding a weak premise, a guarding term the writer uses honestly, and one convergent and one linked premise. Reconstructed with a gap: the learner supplies the missing premise before the reveal, then the ARG evaluation. (c) Rapoport's rules run on a position the learner finds wrong, chosen by the learner from a short list of non-political everyday disagreements (whether children should have homework, whether cities should build more cycle lanes, whether a family should get a dog), with the lesson modelling one of them.
- **Predict:** before the reveal of the missing premise in (b), "what does this writer need to be true that they haven't said?" Before ARG, "this argument is valid; can it still be a bad argument, and on which condition?" **Checkpoint:** after linked/convergent, "one of three premises is false; when does the argument survive?" After the warrant, "name the warrant in 'she's a doctor, so she'll know'."
- **Misconceptions:** "reconstruction means putting it in my words" (no: in theirs, made explicit); "charity means agreeing"; "a steelman is a version nobody holds" (it's the version the best defender holds; if no one holds it, it's not their argument); "relevance is obvious" (it's the condition most real arguments fail, and it takes work to show); "acceptability means I accept it" (it means a reasonable person in the arguer's audience would).
- **Practice:** two real passages (sourced) reconstructed with diagrams and ARG verdicts; one Toulmin layout of the learner's own folder argument; and the Rapoport exercise written out and, if possible, shown to someone who holds the view, with their reaction logged. Then "Do it now": pick the folder argument you most disagree with; write its steelman. That's the project's first draft.
- **Sources:** Govier chs. 2, 4 to 6; Toulmin 1958 via Hitchcock 2002; Sinnott-Armstrong & Fogelin (via Think Again I and Van Cleave ch. 1); Dennett 2013 (wording to verify); SEP Informal Logic (Groarke) secs. 3.2, 4.1, 5; Copi ch. 2; CSUN PHIL 200 objectives on sources of belief.
- **Connects:** completes the six-step check. Lesson 8 can now teach fallacy names as hypotheses, because the learner has the tests that decide them.
- **Spaced review of:** missing premises (lesson 1) as warrants; validity vs strength (lessons 2 and 5) as the grounds condition; the likelihood ratio (lesson 6) as a way to say how much a premise supports a conclusion.

### 8. Fallacies: the names, the patterns, and why the experts warn you about the names (~55 min)
- **Objectives:** identify the standard informal fallacies by name and group in real cases (relevance, weak induction, presumption, ambiguity, illicit transference); explain, for a given fallacy, the cogent argument that shares its surface form and the critical questions that tell them apart; apply Walton's six critical questions to an appeal to expert opinion; explain the fallacy fallacy and Massey's asymmetry and why they make a fallacy name a hypothesis rather than a verdict.
- **Core ideas:** the list, taught by Hurley's five groups, with each fallacy given as a two-line schema in the Lander appendix style, a real named case, and its cogent twin. Aristotle's thirteen (*Sophistical Refutations* 4 to 5: six depending on language, seven not), so the learner sees the list is 2,300 years old, was built for a specific game (refutation in dialectical debate), and was extended piecemeal since (Locke's ad arguments, Mill, Whately; SEP Hansen 2024 sec. 2). The SEP working definition: "an argument that seems to be better than it really is." Then the critique at full strength, as contested question 1: Hamblin 1970 (the standard treatment "as debased, worn-out and dogmatic a treatment as could be imagined"); Finocchiaro (textbook fallacies are rare in real discourse); Massey's asymmetry (there's a method for proving validity, none for proving invalidity, so "no argument can be convicted of being a fallacy on logical grounds"); Boudry, Paglieri & Pigliucci's Fallacy Fork (define a fallacy strictly and it almost never occurs; loosely and it's often cogent). And the defenders and reformers: Walton's schemes with critical questions; pragma-dialectics' ten rules; Johnson & Blair's ARS sort. What would settle it (corpus studies; instruction studies; Abrami 2015 shows critical-thinking instruction helps but doesn't isolate labels). The course's line, said openly: teach the names because you'll meet them and they index real patterns; teach each with its critical questions and its cogent twin; a name is a hypothesis to test with the six-step check, not a verdict. The set pieces: appeal to authority against Walton's six critical questions (expertise, field, opinion, trustworthiness, consistency, backup evidence), and IEP's line that "much of our knowledge properly comes from listening to authorities"; ad hominem against Walton's thesis that no ad hominem is inherently fallacious (a witness's honesty is relevant when the claim rests on their word; IEP's Kepler example); slippery slope and argument from ignorance as inductive patterns that can be strong (lesson 6's likelihood ratio decides); begging the question as circularity, with the everyday "raises the question" usage acknowledged and kept apart; the fallacy fallacy.
- **Mechanism:** every fallacy on the list is a legitimate move done in the wrong place. Appeal to authority is how most knowledge arrives; it fails when the authority is out of field or the experts disagree. Attacking the person is how cross-examination works; it fails when the claim doesn't rest on the person. The name tells you which pattern you're looking at; the critical questions tell you whether this instance is the good or the bad version; only the check decides. That's why the fork bites: a name strict enough to be always bad names almost nothing real.
- **Worked examples:** (a) simple: an appeal to expert opinion run through all six critical questions on a documented case (an advertising-standards ruling on an expert or celebrity endorsement, or a court's assessment of expert testimony; to be found and added to SOURCES.md at draft time), where the argument passes some questions and fails one, so the verdict is "weak here, and here's the question it fails". (b) With a wrinkle: the same "attack the person" pattern in two documented contexts, one where it's irrelevant (a claim that stands on evidence) and one where it's the whole point (a claim that stands on testimony, as in cross-examination); the learner decides before the lesson does. (c) Argument from ignorance done twice on the same drug-safety claim (the lesson 6 example, now with its name), and a slippery slope with a documented chain of mechanisms versus one with none. (d) Straw man shown as the failure of lesson 7's charity, on a documented instance.
- **Predict:** before the critique, "how often do you think these patterns appear, in their fallacious form, in real argument? The people who study this disagree." Before the ad hominem pair, "which of these two is the fallacy?" **Checkpoint:** after the six questions, "an expert in field X asserts something in field Y; which question fails?" After the fallacy fallacy, "you've shown the argument for a claim is circular; what do you now know about the claim?"
- **Misconceptions:** any appeal to authority is a fallacy; ad hominem is always a fallacy; slippery slope and argument from ignorance are always fallacies; "begs the question" means "raises the question"; naming the fallacy settles the matter (IEP: "cannot substitute for a detailed examination of the passage and its context"); a fallacious argument has a false conclusion; the list is complete, or coherent, or ancient in its current form.
- **Practice:** twelve real, sourced passages (the drafting session finds them; see neutrality notes), of which at least four are the cogent twin, not the fallacy, and the learner must say which and why using the critical questions rather than the name. Then a "fallacy card" exercise: the learner writes the schema, the critical questions, and the cogent twin for two fallacies not covered in the lesson body, using IEP's list to find them. Then "Do it now": run the fallacy check on your steelman from lesson 7, then on your own first reaction to it.
- **Sources:** Hurley ch. 3; Copi ch. 4 (codes only if verified); Aristotle, *Sophistical Refutations* 4 to 5 (Pickard-Cambridge); Hamblin 1970 via SEP; SEP Fallacies (Hansen 2024) secs. 1 to 3; IEP Fallacies (Dowden); Walton 1997, 1998; Walton, Reed & Macagno 2008; Finocchiaro 1981; Massey 1981; Boudry, Paglieri & Pigliucci 2015; van Eemeren & Grootendorst 1992/2004; Johnson & Blair 1977; Lander PHIL 103 appendix; Abrami et al. 2015; Wireless Philosophy videos as "go deeper".
- **Connects:** the names are shortcuts for the check the learner already owns; lesson 9 applies them, with the statistics from lesson 6, to the news.
- **Spaced review of:** ARG conditions (lesson 7) as what the critical questions test; likelihood ratio (lesson 6) on ignorance and slope; belief bias (lesson 2) as why you spot fallacies faster on the other side.

### 9. Bad reasoning in the news and everyday life (~45 min)
- **Objectives:** evaluate a source using the rules for sources (cite, informed, impartial, cross-check) and explain how to reason when qualified experts disagree; convert a relative-risk headline into absolute terms and identify a missing base rate or denominator; identify survivorship bias and explain what Wald's analysis actually did; apply a repeatable checklist to a news argument and identify motivated reasoning in your own reading of it.
- **Core ideas:** sources (Weston ch. IV; CSUN objective 2): cite them, prefer informed and impartial ones, cross-check, and notice what an interested source has reason to leave out. Expert disagreement: the six critical questions again, plus what to do when they're all satisfied on both sides (look for the consensus statement, the size of each camp, and what evidence would settle it: standards 3.1 turned into a reading habit). Statistics in headlines: relative versus absolute risk ("doubles the risk" of something with a base rate of 1 in 10,000), missing denominators, and base rates (lesson 6) in the wild. Survivorship: what Mangel & Samaniego 1984 report about Wald's work estimating aircraft vulnerability from the damage on planes that returned; the lesson sticks to the primary account and says that the popular "armour the undamaged parts" version goes beyond what the memoranda say, which is itself a lesson in sources. Motivated reasoning as belief bias (lesson 2) at scale: you check the link on the other side's arguments and check the conclusion on your own. A repeatable checklist that is the six-step check plus three questions for the source (who says, how do they know, who disagrees) and two for the number (share of what, compared with what).
- **Mechanism:** news is arguments with the premises compressed and the source doing the work of the warrant. A headline that gives a relative risk without an absolute one has removed the base rate, which lesson 6 showed is where the answer lives. Survivorship is a sampling error (lesson 5): the returned planes are the sample, and the population includes the ones that didn't return, so the damage you see is the damage you can survive. Motivated reasoning is not stupidity; it's the fast conclusion-check from lesson 2 running before the slow link-check, on a topic where you already have a conclusion.
- **Worked examples:** (a) simple: Wald. What the damage pattern on returning aircraft can and can't tell you, worked from Mangel & Samaniego, ending with what the actual method was. (b) With a wrinkle: a relative-risk headline, documented and dated (found at draft time and added to SOURCES.md; a health or consumer example, not a political one), converted to absolute terms and to natural frequencies, then the follow-up question the headline should have prompted. (c) A matched pair: the same reasoning pattern (say, a hasty generalisation from one incident, or a post hoc claim about a policy and a subsequent statistic) in two documented instances from opposite political sides, dated and cited, evaluated with the same checklist to the same verdict. If a clean matched pair can't be found for a pattern, use a non-partisan case or drop the pattern; never run one side alone.
- **Predict:** "where would you put the armour?" before Wald is worked. "How many extra cases is that?" before the absolute-risk conversion. **Checkpoint:** after the sources rules, "two qualified experts disagree; name three things you'd look for next." After motivated reasoning, "you found a fallacy in an argument you disliked in ten seconds; what should that speed tell you?"
- **Misconceptions:** "the media lies" as a general claim (an unfalsifiable premise, and itself a hasty generalisation; the lesson teaches source-by-source evaluation instead); "experts disagreeing means nobody knows"; "a doubling of risk is a lot" (depends entirely on the base); "I'm not biased, they are"; survivorship as a story about clever Wald rather than about samples.
- **Practice:** three documented news arguments (sourced, dated, balanced as above) run through the full checklist, with the verdict and the one question the learner would ask the writer. One headline-to-natural-frequency conversion. Then "Do it now": take the argument in your folder that you most agreed with on first reading; run the checklist on it as if the other side had written it.
- **Sources:** Weston ch. IV; CSUN PHIL 200 (objective 2, sources of belief); Mangel & Samaniego 1984; Gigerenzer & Hoffrage 1995 (frequency formats, again); Walton 1997 (six questions, again); Evans, Barston & Pollard 1983 (abstract); Hurley ch. 12 (lightly); new documented cases added to SOURCES.md at draft time.
- **Connects:** everything in the course applied to the arguments the learner actually meets. Points forward to How We Know (evidence hierarchies, expert disagreement) and Statistics for Citizens (sampling, risk). Lesson 10 turns the tools on the learner's own writing.
- **Spaced review of:** base rates and natural frequencies (lesson 6); sampling and representativeness (lesson 5); belief bias (lesson 2); the six critical questions (lesson 8).

### 10. Building an argument that holds up (~45 min)
- **Objectives:** build a short argument in standard form with acceptable premises and a link you can name (valid or strong); build an extended one-page argument that anticipates the strongest objection and either answers it or concedes it with a qualifier; apply your own fallacy check and the ARG conditions to your draft and revise it; explain the difference between winning an argument and being right, and why the second is the one this course teaches.
- **Core ideas:** Weston's rules for short arguments (read from the book at draft time: identify premises and conclusion, present ideas in a natural order, start from reliable premises, be concrete and concise, build on substance not overtone, use consistent terms) and for extended arguments and argumentative essays (chs. VII to VIII: explore the issue, make a definite claim, develop the argument fully, consider objections, consider alternatives). Toulmin's qualifier and rebuttal (lesson 7) as the honest way to hold a claim that has exceptions: "presumably" and "unless" are strengths, not weaknesses, because they say exactly how far the argument reaches. Conductive form (lesson 5) as the shape most real arguments should take: reasons for, counter-considerations weighed, verdict. Anticipating objections by steelmanning your own opposition (lesson 7's Rapoport rules turned round). Conceding what should be conceded, and how a concession makes the rest more credible. Consistency of terms as the guard against equivocation (lesson 8). Writing for a reader who will run the six-step check on you.
- **Mechanism:** an argument that survives is one that its best opponent has already been through. Every tool in the course is a place where a reader can stop you: an unstated premise (lesson 1), an invalid form (lessons 2 to 4), a weak sample or analogy (lesson 5), a missing base rate (lesson 6), a straw-manned opponent (lesson 7), a named pattern that fails its critical questions (lesson 8), a source that doesn't hold up (lesson 9). Writing the argument is running the check in reverse, on yourself, before anyone else does.
- **Worked examples:** (a) simple: a short argument built from scratch on an everyday practical question (whether to buy a used car outright or lease; whether to learn a second language this year), shown at each of Weston's rules, with the first draft's weak premise found and replaced. (b) With a wrinkle: an extended argument in which the strongest objection turns out to be right about part of the claim, so the arguer does not abandon the claim but qualifies it (Toulmin's "presumably" and "unless" written into the conclusion) and the argument ends up narrower and stronger. The learner drafts the qualified conclusion before the reveal. (c) A conductive argument written out with reasons for, counter-considerations, and the weighing made explicit, then the same argument with the counter-considerations silently dropped, so the learner sees why the first version is the more persuasive one to a careful reader.
- **Predict:** "your objection is partly right; what are your three options?" before the qualifier move. **Checkpoint:** after Weston's rules, "which rule does an argument that uses 'freedom' in two senses break, and what fallacy from lesson 8 is that?" After concession, "you've conceded a point; is your argument now weaker or stronger, and to which reader?"
- **Misconceptions:** "a strong argument has no weaknesses" (it has stated ones); "conceding is losing"; "more premises make a stronger argument" (convergent premises help; linked ones multiply the ways to fail); "qualifiers are hedging" (a qualifier is precision; hedging is vagueness, and the style guide's difference between them applies to arguments too); "winning is the goal".
- **Practice:** the learner writes a short argument (five lines) and an extended one (one page) on a question from their folder, runs the fallacy check and the ARG conditions on their own draft, writes the best objection in Rapoport form, and revises. This is the project, and the lesson says so; the practice section is its final stage.
- **Sources:** Weston chs. I, VI to VIII, Appendix I; Govier chs. 4 and 12; Toulmin 1958 via Hitchcock 2002; Dennett 2013; Hurley ch. 3 (for the self-check list).
- **Connects:** the whole course in reverse. Points forward to Writing Clearly (if and when that course exists) and to any Foval course in which the learner has to argue: history, economics, ethics, apologetics.
- **Spaced review of:** missing premises (lesson 1), validity vs strength (lessons 2 and 5), Toulmin (lesson 7), the critical questions (lesson 8), sources (lesson 9), all as items on the self-check.

## Assessments

- **Course-end test** (`assessments/final-test.md`): 20 items, 30 minutes, pass mark 80%. Blueprint: 2 on argument identification and missing premises (lesson 1); 3 on validity and soundness including one belief-bias trap, a valid argument with an unbelievable conclusion (lesson 2); 3 on the conditional forms, at least one on "only if" or necessary/sufficient (lesson 3); 1 Venn check (lesson 4); 3 on inductive strength, one each on samples, analogy, and cause, with one requiring the four readings of a correlation (lesson 5); 3 on probability, one conjunction, one base-rate problem to be counted in natural frequencies, one prosecutor's-fallacy comparison (lesson 6); 2 on reconstruction and ARG, one requiring the learner to pick the most charitable missing premise (lesson 7); 2 on fallacies, each a pair where the learner must say which of two same-shaped arguments is the cogent one (lesson 8); 1 on a relative-risk or survivorship case (lesson 9). At least 14 of the 20 are application items with cases not in the lessons, and every argument used in the test obeys the example rules above. Distractors are the misconceptions listed lesson by lesson.
- **Project** (`assessments/project-take-an-argument-apart.md`): "Take a real argument apart and answer it." The learner picks one argument from the folder they built through the course (something they actually read or heard, quoted with source and date; a real argument they encountered, not one assigned) and produces: (1) the original, quoted; (2) a reconstruction in standard form, missing premises in brackets, with a linked/convergent diagram and a Toulmin layout naming the warrant, qualifier, and rebuttal; (3) a steelman paragraph that passes Rapoport's rules; (4) an evaluation: the kind of link claimed, its verdict (valid/invalid or strong/weak, with a counterexample or the weakness named), and the ARG conditions on the premises, with the six critical questions run on any appeal to authority and a natural-frequency count on any probability claim; (5) a fallacy check listing every named pattern the argument contains and, for each, whether its critical questions convict it or acquit it; (6) the learner's own one-page counter-argument or endorsement, written by Weston's rules, with the strongest objection to their own position stated in Rapoport form and either answered or conceded with a qualifier; (7) a short note on what they'd have concluded before the course and whether that changed, and why. Rubric, 12 points, 9 to pass: the reconstruction is faithful and the missing premises are ones the arguer would accept (2); the steelman would satisfy a holder of the view (2); the link is correctly classified and correctly judged, with the counterexample or weakness actually shown (2); the ARG and critical-question evaluation is specific to the premises, not generic (2); the fallacy check names patterns and tests them rather than labelling (1); the learner's own argument has acceptable premises, a nameable link, and an honestly stated objection (2); the qualifier, if used, matches the strength of the argument (1). Political and religious subjects are allowed in the project, because the learner chooses the argument, and the rubric rewards charity and evaluation, not the conclusion reached; the project text says this in so many words.

## Neutrality notes

The course is not a sensitive domain and `sensitive_domain` stays false. Four things need handling anyway.

1. **Lessons 8 and 9 must be neutrality-audited before publication** regardless of the course flag, because their examples come from public argument and public argument is mostly political. Every example in those two lessons must be a named, dated, citable instance found at draft time (court opinions, legislative records such as Hansard or the Congressional Record, retracted papers, advertising-standards rulings, corrections columns, published expert-testimony assessments), added to SOURCES.md with its source before the lesson uses it. For each reasoning pattern, use either a non-partisan documented case (Sally Clark, Wald, Eddy's physicians, Linda, a consumer or health headline, an advertising ruling) or a matched pair from opposite sides of the political spectrum, evaluated with the same tools to the same verdict. A pattern that can't be documented on both sides is illustrated with a non-partisan case or left out. Never an invented political example; never a single-sided real one. The auditor should record which perspectives they checked against and count the examples per side.
2. **No religious claims as fallacy specimens or as examples of any bad reasoning**, in any lesson, per standards 3.7 and the course conventions above. Where a textbook example involves a religious claim, replace it. The project allows religious subjects because the learner chooses and the rubric rewards charity.
3. **The four contested questions inside the discipline** are taught as contested, in the terms each camp would accept, at the lesson named: fallacy theory (lesson 8), how much formal logic (lesson 4), theories of argument (lesson 7, go deeper), Bayesian vs classical and rational vs adapted (lesson 6). Existential import is a convention, not a controversy, and gets a footnote in lesson 4. The course states its own line on each openly and says why. None of these is political.
4. **Belief bias and motivated reasoning are taught symmetrically.** The lesson 2 and lesson 9 material on checking the other side's link and your own side's conclusion applies to every reader, and the text should never imply which side the reader is on.

Figures are quoted exactly as SOURCES.md records them; the "via secondary source" items are read from the source or omitted, never carried into a lesson under a flag.
