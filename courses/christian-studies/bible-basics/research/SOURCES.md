# Sources: The Bible: What It Is and How to Read It

*Stage 1 research. Written before any lesson is drafted. This is a working document: for each source, say what it is good for and what it establishes.*

Research date: September 2026. This is a School of Christian Studies course (`standpoint: christian`; standards 3.7). It is taught from within the faith and says so. It is still held to Parts 1, 2, and 4 in full, and it must keep three kinds of claim distinct in its own text: what history and archaeology establish, what is argued from evidence, and what is held by faith. This file sorts every claim that way.

Where a figure, date, or quotation below is given, it was read from the primary text or a standard reference unless marked "via secondary source". Marked items go on the fact-checker's list. Two sources I could not open are flagged as such rather than paraphrased from memory.

## Canonical textbooks

- **Fee & Stuart, *How to Read the Bible for All Its Worth* (4th ed., Zondervan 2014)** — the most-assigned introductory hermeneutics text in evangelical colleges and seminaries, and the backbone of this course. Full text read (PDF). Chapters: 1 The Need to Interpret; 2 The Basic Tool: A Good Translation; 3 The Epistles: Learning to Think Contextually; 4 The Epistles: The Hermeneutical Questions; 5 The Old Testament Narratives: Their Proper Use; 6 Acts: The Question of Historical Precedent; 7 The Gospels: One Story, Many Dimensions; 8 The Parables; 9 The Law(s): Covenant Stipulations for Israel; 10 The Prophets: Enforcing the Covenant in Israel; 11 The Psalms; 12 Wisdom; 13 The Revelation; appendix on commentaries. What it establishes, with exact wording: the "basic rule" that "a text cannot mean what it never could have meant to its author or readers" (ch. 4, repeating ch. 1); "over 40 percent of the Old Testament is narrative" and the Old Testament is "three-quarters of the bulk of the Bible" (ch. 5); the three levels of narrative (the whole plan of God, the story of Israel, the individual stories); the ten principles for interpreting narratives (ch. 5, ending "God is the hero of all biblical narratives"); "THINK PARAGRAPHS" for the epistles (ch. 3) and "THINK ORACLES" for the prophets (ch. 10); the "comparable particulars" test for applying an epistle (ch. 4); and the prophecy statistics: "Less than 2 percent of Old Testament prophecy is messianic. Less than 5 percent specifically describes the new-covenant age. Less than 1 percent concerns events yet to come in our time" (ch. 10; the authors give no source, so quote it as their estimate). Chapter 2 defines formal equivalence, functional equivalence, and free translation, places KJV/NASB/NKJV/HCSB/ESV/NRSV on the formal side, NIV and NJB/REB/NLT in the middle, and The Message as free, and concludes: "the basic translation for reading and studying should be something in the NIV/NRSV range", with a formal-equivalent version "helpful as a second source".
- **Fee & Stuart, *How to Read the Bible Book by Book: A Guided Tour* (Zondervan 2002)** — the companion, one entry per book with Orienting Data, Overview, Specific Advice for Reading, and A Walk Through. Best for: the "how to start a book you've never read" lesson, and for our reading-plan project.
- **Duvall & Hays, *Grasping God's Word* (4th ed., Zondervan 2020)** — the other standard undergraduate hermeneutics text (Ouachita Baptist; widely used at Baptist and non-denominational colleges). The Interpretive Journey: grasp the text in their town, measure the width of the river to cross (differences of time, culture, covenant), cross the principlizing bridge, consult the biblical map (check the principle against the whole Bible), grasp the text in our town. Best for: a teachable procedure, and its exercises on observation (repetition, contrast, cause-effect) which we can adapt.
- **Köstenberger & Patterson, *Invitation to Biblical Interpretation* (2nd ed., Kregel 2021)** — the "hermeneutical triad" of history, literature, theology; a graduate-level check on our genre lessons.
- **Osborne, *The Hermeneutical Spiral* (2nd ed., IVP 2006)** and **Vanhoozer, *Is There a Meaning in This Text?* (Zondervan 1998)** — the two evangelical theory books. Osborne: interpretation spirals between the text's horizon and the reader's toward the intended meaning; three levels (what it meant, what it means for me, how to share it). Vanhoozer: a defence of authorial meaning against postmodern scepticism, treating meaning as communicative action. Best for: one paragraph in the interpretation lesson on why "what it means to me" is not the first question, cited rather than explained at length.
- **Longman & Dillard, *An Introduction to the Old Testament* (2nd ed., Zondervan 2006)** — the standard evangelical OT introduction (Westminster/Westmont lineage), book by book with historical background, literary analysis, and theological message. Longman uses the phrase "essentially Mosaic" for the Pentateuch: Moses as the substantial source, later editing allowed. That is the phrase we should use, since it is the mainstream evangelical position and it is honest about the seams. Companion for the critical side: **Collins, *Introduction to the Hebrew Bible* (3rd ed., Fortress 2018)**.
- **Carson & Moo, *An Introduction to the New Testament* (2nd ed., Zondervan 2005)** — the standard evangelical NT introduction; each book gets contents, authorship, provenance, date, text, adoption into the canon, and theology. Its dating positions are the conservative reference point (see Contested question 3). Note: I did not open the book itself; its dates below are as I recall them and must be checked against it in the fact-check pass.
- **Wright & Bird, *The New Testament in Its World* (Zondervan Academic / SPCK, 2019)** — the current single-volume NT introduction for students, written to help a reader "think like a first-century believer while reading the text responsibly for today"; covers the Jewish and Greco-Roman world, the historical Jesus, resurrection, Paul, each book, and the making of the canon. I could not retrieve the table of contents (publisher page down); treat its chapter structure as unread and use it for Go Deeper links, not for quoted claims.
- **Bruce, *The Canon of Scripture* (IVP 1988)** — still the standard one-volume history of the canon for students, and unusually fair. Chapter 12 is the Muratorian Fragment; chapter 17 "Athanasius and After". His thesis: the canon was not imposed by a council but recognised over time by widespread use, with apostolicity, orthodoxy, and use in worship as the working tests. Pair with **Kruger, *Canon Revisited* (Crossway 2012)**, the "self-authenticating" model (canon grounded in God's providence, the books' own attributes, and the Spirit's testimony, with historical evidence as corroboration), and **McDonald, *The Biblical Canon* (3rd ed., Baker 2007) / *The Formation of the Biblical Canon* (2 vols., T&T Clark 2017)**, the community-determined model (no early council fixed the list; recognition was slow and local). Kruger and McDonald are the two live positions; give both.
- **Metzger & Ehrman, *The Text of the New Testament: Its Transmission, Corruption, and Restoration* (4th ed., OUP 2005)** — the standard textbook on NT textual criticism, co-written by the field's most respected conservative and its most famous sceptic, which is itself a lesson. Best for: materials (papyrus, parchment, codex), the great uncials, the kinds of scribal error, and the method. Pair with **Metzger, *A Textual Commentary on the Greek New Testament* (2nd ed., 1994)** for the three famous passages, and for the Old Testament **Tov, *Textual Criticism of the Hebrew Bible* (3rd ed., Fortress 2012)**. Accessible one-volume version of the whole story: **Wegner, *The Journey from Texts to Translations* (Baker 1999)**.
- **Bartholomew & Goheen, *The Drama of Scripture* (3rd ed., Baker Academic 2024)** — the Bible as a drama in six acts: creation; fall; Israel (redemption initiated); Jesus (redemption accomplished); the church (mission); new creation (redemption completed). The most-used "big story" text in Christian colleges. Simpler alternatives: **Roberts, *God's Big Picture* (IVP 2002)**, eight stages under the kingdom theme, "God's people in God's place under God's rule and blessing"; **Goldsworthy, *According to Plan* (IVP 1991)**. Best for: the Genesis-to-Revelation lesson; we should adopt one scheme, name the others, and say they are teaching devices, not Scripture's own outline.
- **Wenham, *Christ and the Bible* (3rd ed., Wipf & Stock 2009 reprint)** — the classic argument that a Christian's doctrine of Scripture starts from Jesus' own use of it. Best for: the "why Christians trust the Bible" section, stated as a faith position built on an argument.
- For the critical-scholarship perspective, which the course must present in its own terms: **Ehrman, *The New Testament: A Historical Introduction to the Early Christian Writings* (OUP; Yale's set text)**; **Hayes, *Introduction to the Bible* (Yale UP 2012)**, the book of her lectures; **Ehrman, *Misquoting Jesus* (HarperOne 2005)**, the popular case that variants matter, with its own appendix concession (below); **Enns, *Inspiration and Incarnation* (2nd ed., Baker 2015)**, an evangelical who accepts most critical conclusions and reframes inspiration. **Brown, *An Introduction to the New Testament* (Yale 1997)** is the Catholic critical standard.

## Primary sources

**The biblical text.** The course quotes a modern translation with permission terms that allow it (ESV and CSB both permit up to 500 verses without written permission in non-commercial use; NET is free by design). Passages the course depends on: Luke 24:27 and 24:44 (Jesus reading "Moses and all the Prophets" and "the Law of Moses, the Prophets and the Psalms" as about himself, which is also the earliest Christian witness to the threefold Jewish canon); 2 Timothy 3:16 ("God-breathed"); 2 Peter 3:15-16 (Paul's letters ranked with "the other Scriptures"); Genesis 36:31 and Deuteronomy 34 (the standard internal evidence for post-Mosaic editing); John 21:25 (narratives are selective); Nehemiah 8:8 (reading with the sense given); Acts 17:11 (the Bereans).

**Canon.**
- **Prologue to Sirach (c. 132 BC)** — Ben Sira's grandson, translating in Egypt, refers three times to "the Law, the Prophets, and the rest of the books" (or "the other books of our fathers"): the earliest witness to a three-part collection, with the third part not yet named.
- **Josephus, *Against Apion* 1.8 (1.37-43), c. AD 95** — the Jews have "only twenty-two books, which contain the records of all the past times; which are justly believed to be divine", and "no one has been so bold as either to add any thing to them, to take any thing from them, or to make any change in them" (Whiston translation). Twenty-two is the Hebrew canon counted with Ruth joined to Judges and Lamentations to Jeremiah; the rabbinic count of 24 (Torah 5, Prophets 8, Writings 11) is the same books.
- **Babylonian Talmud, *Bava Batra* 14b-15a** — a baraita giving the order of the Prophets and the Writings (the Torah is assumed). Shows the tripartite canon as the norm, and that the question being settled was order, not membership.
- **Athanasius, 39th Festal Letter (Easter 367)** — read in the NPNF translation at New Advent. He lists 22 OT books (Esther is not among them; Baruch and the Letter of Jeremiah are counted with Jeremiah), then the New Testament: "four Gospels", Acts, "seven Catholic Epistles (James, Peter two, John three, Jude)", "fourteen Epistles of Paul" (Hebrews included), and "the Revelation of John". Then: "These are fountains of salvation... In these alone is proclaimed the doctrine of godliness. Let no man add to these, neither let him take ought from these." Separately he names books "appointed by the Fathers to be read" to catechumens: Wisdom of Solomon, Sirach, Esther, Judith, Tobit, the Didache, and the Shepherd. This is the first surviving list of exactly our 27 NT books. Two things a lesson must not get wrong: he does not list Esther as canonical, and he does not create the list; he reports it.
- **The Muratorian Fragment** — a damaged Latin list in a 7th/8th-century codex in the Ambrosian Library, Milan, published by Muratori in 1740. Lists the four Gospels (the start, covering Matthew and most of Mark, is lost), Acts, thirteen Pauline letters, Jude, two letters of John, Wisdom of Solomon, and Revelation (and the Apocalypse of Peter, "though some of us will not have it read in church"); says the Shepherd of Hermas "ought indeed to be read" but not publicly in church as Scripture. It does not mention Hebrews, James, 1 or 2 Peter, or (clearly) a third letter of John. Date: traditionally late 2nd century (Rome); Sundberg (1973) and Hahneman (1992) argued for the 4th century and the East; Verheyden (2003) found none of their arguments convincing; Schnabel's JETS 57 (2014) survey concludes the traditional date still holds and that the fragment reflects one local church's usage rather than a universal decision. Rothschild's 2022 Mohr Siebeck edition is the current critical text. Teach: probably c. 180-200; disputed; either way it shows a core of about 22 books recognised early and a fringe still under discussion.
- **Marcion (Rome, c. 144)** — an edited Luke plus ten Pauline letters, and rejection of the Old Testament. The standard evidence that the church already had a wider collection to defend, and a cautionary tale about cutting the Old Testament off from the New.
- **Papias (c. 110-130), quoted in Eusebius, *Ecclesiastical History* 3.39** — "Mark, having become the interpreter of Peter, wrote down accurately, though not in order, whatever he remembered of the things said or done by the Lord"; "Matthew composed the oracles in the Hebrew dialect, and each one interpreted them as he was able." The earliest external testimony on Gospel authorship; critical scholars doubt it, conservatives rely on it; the lesson gives it as evidence and says what each side does with it.
- **Irenaeus, *Against Heresies* 3.1.1 and 3.11.8 (c. 180)** — the four Gospels and their traditional authors, and the argument that there can be "neither more nor fewer" than four, as there are four winds and four zones of the world. Establishes: the fourfold Gospel was fixed in the mainstream church by 180, a century and a half before Nicaea.
- **Eusebius, *Ecclesiastical History* 3.25 (c. 325)** — sorts the books into recognised (homologoumena), disputed (antilegomena: James, Jude, 2 Peter, 2 and 3 John), and spurious (Acts of Paul, Shepherd, Apocalypse of Peter, Barnabas, Didache), with Revelation placed ambiguously. The best single snapshot of the state of the question on the eve of Nicaea, and proof that Nicaea did not need to settle it.
- **Councils of Hippo (393) and Carthage (397, 419)** — regional North African councils (Augustine present) that listed a canon matching the later Catholic one, including the deuterocanonical books; Carthage asked for the list to be confirmed by "the church across the sea" (Rome).
- **Jerome, *Prologus Galeatus* (c. 391) and the Vulgate (completed c. 405)** — translated the OT from Hebrew ("Hebraica veritas") and counted the books the Jews did not have as "ecclesiastical" books, read for edification but not for establishing doctrine. The medieval West inherited both the wider Vulgate and Jerome's reservation, which is why the question was still open at the Reformation.
- **Luther's 1534 German Bible** — places the Apocrypha between the testaments under the heading "Apocrypha: these books are not held equal to the Holy Scriptures, but are useful and good to read" (title page; read via John Meade's photograph of the contents page and the LSTC Gruber Collection description).
- **Council of Trent, Session IV, 8 April 1546, *Decree Concerning the Canonical Scriptures*** — the Catholic canon (46 OT, 27 NT) defined with an anathema on anyone who does not receive the books "entire with all their parts" as in the Vulgate. The reported vote (24 for, 15 against, 16 abstaining) is via secondary source (Wikipedia, citing Metzger); verify or omit.
- **Synod of Jerusalem (1672)** — Greek Orthodox affirmation of a canon close to Trent's; being a regional synod, it binds only where received. Orthodox Bibles also carry 1 Esdras, 3 Maccabees, the Prayer of Manasseh, and Psalm 151 (the *anagignoskomena*, "things read"), with 4 Maccabees in an appendix. The Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo canon is the largest, 81 books including Enoch and Jubilees.
- **King James Version (1611)** — included the Apocrypha between the testaments, as English Bibles before it had. The British and Foreign Bible Society stopped funding Bibles with the Apocrypha in 1826 (the "Apocrypha controversy"); whether the motive was theological, financial, or both is itself debated; do not state one motive as fact.

**Text and transmission.**
- **Institut für Neutestamentliche Textforschung (INTF, Münster), *Kurzgefasste Liste*** — the official register of Greek NT manuscripts: about 4,250 in 1950, 5,460 in 1983, roughly 5,800 by 2017 (INTF blog); in four classes (papyri, majuscules, minuscules, lectionaries). Quote "about 5,800 Greek manuscripts" and add that most are medieval and fragmentary; the number is not the argument, the age and spread are.
- **Dead Sea Scrolls (found 1947-56)** — over 230 biblical manuscripts (Israel Antiquities Authority Leon Levy Library), with every Hebrew Bible book represented except Esther (and Nehemiah only if counted as separate from Ezra). The Great Isaiah Scroll (1QIsa^a), the only complete biblical book, is dated c. 125 BC by palaeography, with radiocarbon ranges of roughly 350-100 BC; a 2025 combined palaeographic and radiocarbon study suggested slightly older. It is about a thousand years older than the Masoretic codices and agrees with them overwhelmingly, with variants that are mostly spelling and small wording; that is the settled finding. Two cautions for the lesson: the scrolls also show a plural text (some books in Septuagint-like and other forms), so "the text never changed" is too strong; and a good deal of what is online about "word-for-word identical" is overstated.
- **Masoretic codices** — Aleppo Codex (c. 930, partly lost in 1947) and Leningrad Codex (1008/1009, the oldest complete Hebrew Bible; the base of the BHS/BHQ editions every modern OT translation uses).
- **Ketef Hinnom silver scrolls (found 1979, late 7th/early 6th century BC)** — two tiny silver amulets with a form of the priestly blessing of Numbers 6:24-26; the oldest surviving biblical text, from the First Temple period.
- **Greek NT manuscripts to name**: P52 (John 18, John Rylands Library), traditionally c. 125 but Nongbri (2005) showed the hand fits anywhere from the early second to early third century, so say "second century"; P46 (Pauline letters, c. 200); P75 (Luke and John, c. 200-225); Codex Sinaiticus and Codex Vaticanus (both 4th century; Sinaiticus is the oldest complete NT). Read the dates from the Rylands, Chester Beatty/Michigan, and British Library descriptions; P46 "125-150" appears on some sites and is not the standard date.
- **The three famous variants**: Mark 16:9-20 (absent from Sinaiticus and Vaticanus; different vocabulary and style; Metzger, *Textual Commentary*); John 7:53-8:11 (absent from the earliest witnesses, floats to other locations in some manuscripts; most scholars think it a genuine early tradition not originally part of John; NA28/UBS5 double-bracket it); 1 John 5:7-8, the "Johannine Comma" (no Greek manuscript before the 14th century; entered the Textus Receptus in Erasmus's third edition of 1522; omitted or footnoted in every major modern translation since the RV of 1881). Every mainstream English translation marks the first two with brackets or a note. These are the worked examples for the textual lesson because the learner can see them in their own Bible.
- **On variant counts**: Wallace (2013) and Ehrman both put the total at around 400,000, counting a difference once no matter how many manuscripts carry it; the vast majority are spelling and word order that cannot be translated. Wallace's figure that fewer than 1 percent are both "meaningful and viable" is via secondary source (STR summary of his bible.org essay, which I could not fetch); verify. Ehrman, in the 2005 paperback appendix to *Misquoting Jesus*: "The position I argue for in Misquoting Jesus does not actually stand at odds with Prof. Metzger's position that the essential Christian beliefs are not affected by textual variants in the manuscript tradition of the New Testament" (via secondary; the quote is well attested but appears only in some printings).
- **Chapters and verses**: chapter divisions are usually credited to Stephen Langton (c. 1205-1227); verse numbers to Robert Estienne (Stephanus), 1551 Greek NT and 1553 French Bible; the Geneva Bible (1560) was the first English Bible with them. The Protestant Bible has 1,189 chapters and about 31,100 verses (KJV count; via secondary). Reading the whole Bible aloud at pulpit pace takes roughly 70 hours (Crossway infographic; via secondary).

**Doctrine of Scripture (the faith positions, in their own words).**
- **Chicago Statement on Biblical Inerrancy (1978)** — the evangelical reference statement; PDF at the ETS archive. Nineteen affirm/deny articles. Article XIII: inerrancy is not "negated by Biblical phenomena such as a lack of modern technical precision, irregularities of grammar or spelling, observational descriptions of nature, the reporting of falsehoods, the use of hyperbole and round numbers, the topical arrangement of material, variant selections of material in parallel accounts, or the use of free citations"; the exposition adds that "history must be treated as history, poetry as poetry". Article X locates inerrancy in the autographs. Critics on the evangelical left (Rogers & McKim 1979) argued inerrancy in this form was not the historic position; Woodbridge (1982) replied that it was.
- **Vatican II, *Dei Verbum* 11 (1965)** — "the books of Scripture must be acknowledged as teaching solidly, faithfully and without error that truth which God wanted put into sacred writings for the sake of salvation." Whether "for the sake of salvation" limits the scope of inerrancy or states its purpose is a live intra-Catholic debate (the JSR symposium "For the Sake of Our Salvation", 2016).
- **Lausanne Covenant (1974)** — "without error in all that it affirms"; **PCUSA Confession of 1967** — Scripture as the witness to the Word, to be read "with literary and historical understanding", the mainline formulation; **Orthodox**: Scripture as the heart of Holy Tradition, interpreted within the Church (Ware, *The Orthodox Church*; OCA's *The Orthodox Faith* vol. 1).

## What the field considers essential

Dependency order; this becomes the outline backbone.

1. **What the Bible is and how it is laid out.** Two testaments; 39 + 27 = 66 books in the Protestant Bible, 73 in the Catholic, more in Orthodox Bibles; the Hebrew Bible's 24 books in three parts (Torah, Prophets, Writings) versus the Christian OT's four (Law, History, Wisdom/Poetry, Prophets), same books in a different order that ends with Malachi looking forward; the NT's four (Gospels, Acts, Letters, Revelation). Why the order is not chronological, and what a chapter and verse number is and is not (Langton, Estienne). Nothing else can be taught until the learner can find things.
2. **The big story.** One story from creation to new creation, told with a six-act (or similar) scheme; the covenants (Noah, Abraham, Sinai, David, the new covenant) as the spine; where each book sits on the timeline; the intertestamental gap. Fee & Stuart's three levels of narrative belong here. Learners who have this can place any passage.
3. **Genre is the first interpretive decision.** The Fee & Stuart method: identify the kind of writing, learn the rules of that kind, then read. The genres and their rules: narrative (ten principles; God is the hero; description is not prescription); law (covenant stipulations for Israel; how a Christian reads it now, the moral/civil/ceremonial and other proposals); poetry and Psalms (parallelism, imagery, the psalm types: lament, praise, thanksgiving, royal, wisdom, imprecatory); wisdom (Proverbs as generalisations not promises; Job and Ecclesiastes as counter-voices); prophecy (covenant enforcement; oracles; near and far horizons; the 2/5/1 percent statistics); Gospels (four portraits of one Jesus; the kingdom; parables as one-point stories with a punch); Acts (history first, precedent only with care); epistles (occasional documents; think paragraphs; the basic rule; comparable particulars); apocalyptic (Daniel, Revelation; symbol, not code; the four reading schools named fairly).
4. **How the canon formed.** The Jewish canon by the first century (Sirach prologue, Josephus, Luke 24:44); the NT core recognised by 180 (Irenaeus, Muratorian) with the edges (Hebrews, James, 2 Peter, 2-3 John, Jude, Revelation) settling through the 4th century (Eusebius, Athanasius 367, Hippo/Carthage); the deuterocanon question from Jerome to Luther to Trent; what Nicaea did and did not do. The criteria the church used and the two models (Bruce/Kruger vs McDonald) of what was happening.
5. **How the text was transmitted and how reliable it is.** Materials and copying; the Dead Sea Scrolls and the Masoretes for the OT; the manuscript count, the early papyri, and the great codices for the NT; what a textual variant is and how many there are; the three famous passages; what textual criticism has established (the wording is recoverable to a very high degree; no doctrine hangs on a disputed reading, as Metzger and Ehrman both say) and what remains open (individual readings, the ending of Mark).
6. **Translations.** Source texts (BHS, NA28/UBS5, and why the KJV differs: Textus Receptus); formal vs functional vs free; the spectrum chart; the honest trade-offs; gender language; how to pick one (one main reading Bible in the NIV/CSB/NRSV range, one formal for study, one free for freshness) and why the "most accurate" question is malformed.
7. **How to read it: the practical method.** Observation, interpretation, application (Hendricks; Duvall & Hays); context in widening circles (sentence, paragraph, book, testament, Bible); the questions to ask any passage; tools and how to use them (cross-references, a study Bible's notes, a concordance, STEP/Blue Letter for the original words without knowing the languages); a reading plan that survives February (M'Cheyne's four chapters, a Gospel-first plan, or the BibleProject one-year plan); reading in community.
8. **What Christians believe about this book, and what others make of it.** Inspiration, inerrancy, infallibility, and authority as the tradition uses the words; the range within Christianity; the critical view stated so a Yale student would recognise it; what is established, argued, and believed.

## Common misconceptions

- **"The Bible was written hundreds of years after the events."** For the NT, the whole critical mainstream dates every book between about AD 50 and 120: Paul's undisputed letters in the 50s, within twenty-five years of the crucifixion, and the Gospels within the lifetime of the second generation. The conservative dates are earlier still. Four centuries is nobody's position. For the OT the gap between event and final text is real and larger (see Contested question 2), which is why the course must say so rather than let the NT answer stand for both.
- **"It's been translated so many times it can't be trusted"** (the "telephone game"). Modern translations are made directly from Hebrew, Aramaic, and Greek editions built from thousands of manuscripts, not from earlier translations. The chain is two links, not fifty. What is true is that the KJV translators worked from a late Greek text, which is why a modern Bible and the KJV differ at 1 John 5:7 and elsewhere.
- **"Constantine (or the Council of Nicaea, 325) chose the books."** No record of Nicaea's business mentions the canon; Irenaeus had the four Gospels a century and a half earlier; Eusebius, who attended, lists disputed books in his own history. The Da Vinci Code (2003) put this into circulation. The councils that did list the books were Hippo and Carthage, sixty years later, and they were confirming use, not choosing.
- **"The lost gospels were suppressed."** Thomas, Peter, Judas and the rest are second- and third-century texts; the fourfold Gospel predates most of them. The learner should know they exist, what they are, and why they were not received (date, content, use), rather than hearing that they were hidden.
- **"The Dead Sea Scrolls prove the Bible has never changed a word."** They establish a remarkably stable text over a thousand years, and they also show more than one textual form in circulation. Both halves are true.
- **"A literal reading is the faithful reading."** The Chicago Statement itself says poetry must be read as poetry and hyperbole as hyperbole. Genre first is the conservative position, not a liberal concession.
- **"Prophecy means prediction."** Fee & Stuart: under 2 percent messianic, under 1 percent about our future; the prophets were covenant prosecutors speaking to their own generation.
- **"The Old Testament is law, the New Testament is grace" / "Christians don't need the Old Testament."** Marcion's position, rejected by the church in the second century; the New Testament quotes the Old hundreds of times and Jesus taught from it (Luke 24).
- **"A proverb is a promise."** Proverbs 22:6 is the standard example; wisdom literature states how life generally goes, and Job and Ecclesiastes are in the same Bible to prevent the misreading.
- **"Chapter and verse divisions are part of the text."** They are medieval and early-modern aids; some fall in unhelpful places (e.g. Isaiah 52:13-53:12 split across a chapter break).
- **"Everyone in the story is a role model."** Narrative principle 4: often "just the opposite".
- **"The most literal translation is the most accurate."** Formal equivalence preserves form at the cost of sense in idioms and wordplay; every translation interprets; the honest question is which trade-off you want for which purpose.

## Contested questions and the positions on each

Classified per standards 3.1 and 3.7. "Established" means accepted across the expert community regardless of confession; "argued" means a live scholarly dispute; "faith" means a claim held on religious grounds that evidence bears on but does not settle.

1. **The extent of the canon (Protestant 66 / Catholic 73 / Orthodox more).** *Protestant case:* the Hebrew canon Jesus and the apostles used had no deuterocanon; Josephus counts 22; the NT never quotes the deuterocanonical books as Scripture; Jerome and many fathers distinguished them; Athanasius's list has them as books "to be read" but outside the canon. *Catholic and Orthodox case:* the early church's Bible was the Greek Septuagint, which carried these books; the NT alludes to them (Hebrews 11:35 and 2 Maccabees 7 is the usual example); Hippo and Carthage listed them; the church read them as Scripture for over a thousand years before Trent defined what was already practised; Protestants are the innovators. *What is established:* the historical facts above are not in dispute; each side agrees on what Josephus, Athanasius, Jerome, Luther, and Trent said. *What is argued:* how much weight each witness carries, and whether the Greek-speaking Jewish community of Alexandria had a wider canon (most scholars now doubt an "Alexandrian canon"). *Classification:* the history is established; the conclusion is a **faith and ecclesial question**. The course, being Protestant in practice, may say which canon it uses and why, and must state the other case so a Catholic reader recognises it. Also say plainly that no Christian doctrine depends on the difference except purgatory (2 Maccabees 12) and that the deuterocanon is worth reading whatever one's view.

2. **Authorship and dating of the Pentateuch.** *Critical position (Wellhausen 1878 and successors):* the Pentateuch is composed from sources (classically J, E, D, P) written between the 9th and 5th centuries BC and combined after the exile; evidence is doublets, divine-name patterns, contradictions, and post-Mosaic references (Genesis 36:31, Deuteronomy 34). The field has since split into neo-documentarians (Baden, Stackert, Schwartz), who defend four sources by literary criteria alone, and supplementary/fragmentary models, mainly in Europe, which see a core expanded in layers; nearly all agree on a distinct Priestly stratum. *Conservative position:* Moses is the substantial author, as the Pentateuch and the rest of the Bible say (Exodus 24:4; Joshua 8:31; Mark 12:26), with later editorial updating ("essentially Mosaic", Longman & Dillard); the doublets and name-patterns are literary features, not seams (Cassuto, Kikawada & Quinn, Garrett); the critical reconstruction rests on an evolutionary theory of Israelite religion that archaeology has not confirmed. *What would settle it:* it will not be settled by evidence now available; there are no manuscripts of the sources and no external reference to Moses writing. *Classification:* **argued**, with the critical position the majority in universities and the conservative position the majority in evangelical seminaries; both must pass the Turing test. The course teaches the final form, says who wrote what is disputed, and says what a Christian may reasonably hold either way.

3. **Dating and authorship of the Gospels.** *Critical mainstream (Ehrman's summary):* Mark c. 70, Matthew and Luke c. 80-85, John c. 90-95; all anonymous, the names attached in the second century; the main lever is that Mark 13 presupposes the destruction of the Temple. *Conservative:* Mark late 50s to 60s, Matthew and Luke in the 60s, Acts before Paul's death (c. 62, since Acts ends with him alive), John 80s-90s (Carson & Moo; verify their exact ranges in the book); the names are early and unanimous (Papias, Irenaeus, the manuscript titles); Mark 13 is prophecy, and the Gospels never mention the Temple's fall as past. Robinson (*Redating the New Testament*, 1976), no conservative, dated the whole NT before 70 on the same silence. *What is established:* every Gospel was written within about seventy years of the crucifixion; Paul's letters are earlier; there is no fourth-century composition. *What is argued:* the decade, and whether the traditional authors wrote them. *Classification:* **argued**; the course gives both ranges, and it does not need the early dates to make its case for the Gospels' reliability.

4. **Inerrancy, infallibility, and other views of Scripture within Christianity.** *Inerrancy (Chicago 1978):* Scripture is without error in all it affirms, including history and science, in the autographs, read by its own genres and conventions. *Infallibility (Fuller after 1972; Lausanne's wording is read both ways):* Scripture is trustworthy and cannot fail in its purpose of teaching faith and practice; whether every incidental detail is accurate is a different question. *Catholic (Dei Verbum 11):* without error in "that truth which God wanted put into sacred writings for the sake of salvation", with the scope debated. *Orthodox:* Scripture within Tradition, read in the Church. *Mainline (Confession of 1967):* Scripture the normative witness to the Word, read with literary and historical criticism. *Enns (incarnational):* the Bible is fully God's and fully human, and its human marks include ancient cosmology and diverse voices. *Classification:* a **value/faith question within Christianity**; the course states each in its own terms, may say which it holds, and must not treat the others as unbelief. What history establishes is only that the church has always called Scripture inspired and authoritative, and that the word "inerrancy" as a slogan is modern.

5. **How the Old Testament relates to the New.** *Covenant theology:* one covenant of grace unfolding; the NT interprets the OT; typology and fulfilment; the church as the Israel of God. *Dispensationalism:* Israel and the church remain distinct; OT promises to Israel retain a literal future referent; the NT does not change what OT texts meant. *Progressive covenantalism and progressive dispensationalism* sit between. All agree on Luke 24:27 (Jesus in all the Scriptures) and on Beale's observation that the OT authors did not exhaustively grasp what they wrote. *Classification:* **contested within the faith**; present as a range, name the reading practices each produces (how each handles Ezekiel 40-48, say), and do not resolve it.

6. **What the text-critical evidence shows.** *Established:* the NT is by far the best-attested ancient text; the wording is recoverable to a very high degree; no article of the creeds depends on a disputed variant (Metzger and Ehrman agree in print). *Argued:* whether some early variants were doctrinally motivated (Ehrman, *Orthodox Corruption of Scripture*, 1993) or mostly accidental (Wallace, Hurtado); the original ending of Mark; whether "original text" is a recoverable concept (Epp, Parker). *Classification:* the reliability claim is **established** at the level the course needs; the mechanism questions are **argued**.

7. **The historicity of the OT narratives (patriarchs, exodus, conquest, united monarchy).** The course does not need this to teach Bible basics, but a learner will ask. *Established:* a king David existed (Tel Dan stele, 9th century BC, "House of David", found 1993); the priestly blessing was in use in the 7th century BC (Ketef Hinnom); the Babylonian exile and return happened; Pilate existed (Caesarea inscription, 1961). *Argued:* the scale and date of the exodus and conquest, the size of David's kingdom (maximalist vs minimalist), the historicity of the patriarchal narratives. *Faith:* that the events happened as Scripture describes where archaeology is silent. The course names all three and points to *Faith and Science* and *Hard Questions* for the rest.

8. **How to read Revelation (and other apocalyptic).** Preterist, historicist, futurist, idealist, and mixed readings; each has serious defenders (Beale, Osborne, Ladd, and the Four Views volume). *Classification:* **contested within the faith**; the course teaches the genre's conventions (symbolic numbers, OT allusion, visions as pictures) and presents the four schools fairly.

## Open concerns about scope

- **Eight essentials, eight hours.** This maps naturally onto about ten lessons: (1) what the Bible is and how to find your way around it; (2) the big story, Genesis to Revelation; (3) reading narrative and law; (4) reading poetry, Psalms, and wisdom; (5) reading the prophets; (6) reading the Gospels, Acts, and parables; (7) reading the letters and Revelation; (8) how the canon formed, and the Catholic and Orthodox Bibles; (9) how the text reached us, and translations; (10) how to actually read: method, tools, a plan. Lesson 9 may need to split (text; translations). Project: read one short book (Ruth, Jonah, Mark, Philippians) with the method and write a one-page reading guide in the *Book by Book* format. Estimated hours should rise to 10.
- **Two sources not read.** Wright & Bird's table of contents and Carson & Moo's exact dating ranges were not retrieved; both are cited only for Go Deeper and for "as I recall" positions that the fact-check must confirm.
- **Secondary-source figures to verify:** Trent vote (24-15-16); Wallace's "under 1 percent meaningful and viable"; Ehrman's appendix wording; retailer reading-grade levels; 31,102 verses; 70 hours aloud; "over 230" DSS biblical manuscripts (the IAA figure; Tov's count is higher if fragments are split).
- **"Forty authors over 1,500 years."** This common line assumes conservative dating (Moses c. 1400 BC to John c. 95); on critical dating the span is under a thousand years. Use it only with that caveat, or replace with "roughly a thousand to fifteen hundred years, depending on when you date the earliest books".
- **Overlap with *How to Study the Bible*.** That course does inductive study in depth. This one should teach genre and method at the level of "what kind of text is this and what does that change", and leave observation worksheets, word studies, and cross-referencing technique to the next course. Draw the line in the outline.
- **Neutrality audit.** Required (sensitive domain). The auditor should check against a Catholic reader (canon), an Orthodox reader (Scripture and Tradition), a Jewish reader (the Tanakh is not "the Old Testament" to them; its order matters; the course should say so), a critical scholar (Hayes/Martin/Ehrman positions), and an inerrantist (that the course has not quietly conceded the critical view where it only meant to describe it).
