# Outline: The Bible: What It Is and How to Read It

*Stage 2. Built from SOURCES.md. Twelve lessons, a final test, a two-week project. Sequence follows the dependency order in "What the field considers essential": first the map (layout, then the one story), then genre as the first interpretive decision (five lessons, one per family of writing, each built on the Fee & Stuart method), then where the book came from (canon, text, what Christians believe about it and what others make of it), then the two practical lessons (choosing a translation; a way of reading you can keep). Misconceptions are named inside the lesson where the learner would meet them and gathered again in the final test. The eight essentials became twelve lessons because genre is really five ideas, and text and translations each need a sitting of their own; nothing here is padding, and each lesson ends at a place a phone reader can stop.*

**This is a School of Christian Studies course** (`standpoint: christian`; standards 3.7). Every lesson carries the site's label and the course says on its first line that it teaches from within the Christian faith. It is held to Parts 1, 2, and 4 in full.

## Conventions for every lesson

1. **Open with a passage.** The first thing on the page is Scripture, quoted, a few verses at most, chosen because the lesson's idea changes how you read it. The lesson then shows the reading most people bring to it, then the reading the lesson teaches. Quote the CSB or ESV (both allow up to 500 verses in non-commercial use without written permission) and keep a running count of quoted verses across the course in REVIEWS.md so the total stays under 500; use the NET (free by design) for any overflow and for translators' notes.
2. **Worked examples are readings.** In this course a worked example is a real passage read badly and then read well, with the reasoning shown. Each lesson has at least two: one simple, one with a wrinkle that shows the edge of the rule.
3. **Every claim about history, manuscripts, dates, or councils is sourced,** by name and year in the text and in `## Sources`. Figures marked "via secondary source" in SOURCES.md are either given with that flag or left out until the fact-check confirms them. Never round a manuscript count up, never give a date more precisely than the source does, never say "identical" when the source says "overwhelmingly agrees".
4. **Say which kind of claim it is.** The course sorts its claims in its own text, in a consistent phrasing the learner will come to expect: *History establishes...*, *Scholars argue about...*, *We hold by faith that...* Faith commitments are stated as the course's own, in the first person plural ("we believe the Bible is God's Word"), never disguised as neutral fact ("the Bible is God's Word"). Both are honest; only the first is labelled.
5. **Other views in their own terms.** Where the course describes a Catholic, Orthodox, Jewish, critical-scholarly, or non-inerrantist reading, it uses that group's own words for itself (deuterocanonical books, not "the Apocrypha", when speaking for Catholics; Tanakh or Hebrew Bible, not only "Old Testament", when speaking for Jews; critical scholars, not "liberals"; inerrantists, which is their own word). Each description is written to pass the Turing test in standards 3.2 and the neutrality audit checks it against the five readers named at the end of this outline.
6. **The learner reads actual Scripture in every practice.** Not "reflect on", but "open Ruth, read all four chapters, and do this". Practice comes before the quiz and uses the lesson's method on a passage the lesson did not cover.
7. **Think while reading.** Two or three `:::predict` blocks per lesson (guess how a passage reads before the lesson tells you; guess a number before it is given), one `:::checkpoint` after the mechanism, one `:::exercise` before the quiz. The site adds free recall before the quiz. Placement is noted per lesson below.
8. **Link, don't duplicate.** Every genre lesson links the matching BibleProject video. Every history lesson's Go Deeper includes at least one resource from the critical side (Hayes RLST 145 or Martin RLST 152 by lecture number) so the learner can hear the other view at full strength from its own teachers.
9. **The course uses the Protestant sixty-six books** and says so in lesson 1, names the Catholic and Orthodox canons with respect, and gives the case for them in lesson 8. Where Christian traditions differ (canon, law, Revelation, inerrancy) the course presents the range and may say which view it holds.
10. **Voice.** A professor talking to one student who has a Bible open on the table. No em dashes, none of the banned vocabulary, no preaching at the reader, no "amazing". Humility about what we don't know is part of the teaching.

## Where this course stops and the next ones begin

- ***How to Study the Bible*** does inductive study in depth. This course teaches the *kind* of text you are holding and what that changes, plus one repeatable way to read a whole book. It does not teach observation worksheets, word studies, Strong's numbers beyond "this tool exists", cross-reference technique, or how to use commentaries. Lesson 12 names these tools and hands the learner on.
- ***The Story of Scripture*** walks the six acts one by one. Lesson 2 here gives the map in a single sitting so that every later lesson can place a passage on it.
- ***The Case for Christianity*** argues for the resurrection and the historical Jesus. Lesson 6 and 9 here establish only that the Gospels are early and their text recoverable; they do not make the historical case.
- ***Faith and Science*** handles Genesis 1 and origins. Lesson 3 says Genesis 1 is a genre question and points there.
- ***Hard Questions*** handles Bible difficulties, Old Testament violence, and the historicity debates in depth. Lessons 3 and 10 name them and point there.
- ***World Religions*** (neutral) describes Christianity alongside other faiths; the neutral school's own ***The Bible: Text, History, Influence*** treats the book as literature and history without a standpoint. This course is where the case is made and the label is on it.

## Lesson sequence

### 1. Finding your way around: what the Bible is and how it is laid out (~40 min)
- **Opens with:** Luke 24:44, Jesus naming "the Law of Moses, the Prophets, and the Psalms". Three parts, in the first century, from the mouth of the person the book is about.
- **Objectives:** identify the section and genre of any book from its position; explain why the Christian Old Testament and the Jewish Tanakh contain the same books in a different order and why the order matters; explain what chapter and verse numbers are and are not; state what the course believes about this book and how it will label that belief.
- **Core ideas:** two testaments; 39 + 27 = 66 in the Protestant Bible, 73 in the Catholic, more in Orthodox Bibles (the full case for each waits until lesson 8; here we only say the three exist and which this course uses). The Hebrew Bible's 24 books in three parts (Torah, Nevi'im, Ketuvim) against the Christian Old Testament's four (Law, History, Poetry and Wisdom, Prophets): same books, different order, different last word (the Tanakh ends with Chronicles and the call to go up to Jerusalem; the Christian Old Testament ends with Malachi looking forward). The New Testament's four (Gospels, Acts, Letters, Revelation). Why the order is not chronological. Chapters (Langton, c. 1205–1227) and verses (Estienne, 1551 and 1553; the Geneva Bible 1560 the first English Bible with them). The course's stance stated in the first person plural, once, plainly: we believe this book is God's Word; the label on this course tells you that; the lesson will always say which claims are history, which are argued, and which are ours by faith.
- **Mechanism:** the order is a *reading* order, not a timeline: a library arranged by shelf. Knowing the shelves lets you predict what kind of book you are holding before you read a word, and that prediction (lesson 3) is the first thing that goes wrong for new readers.
- **Worked examples:** (a) simple: find Habakkuk, Philemon, and Daniel from a closed Bible using the sections, then say what kind of book each is from its shelf alone; note that Daniel sits among the Prophets in a Christian Bible and among the Writings in the Tanakh, and what that difference says about how each community reads him. (b) With a wrinkle: Isaiah 52:13 to 53:12. A reader who starts at 53:1 (where the chapter number invites them to) misses the servant introduced at 52:13 and reads three verses before finding out who "he" is. Chapter breaks are medieval; the poem is not. Also the mirror case, Psalm 9 and 10, one acrostic cut in two.
- **Misconceptions:** "the books are in the order they were written"; "chapter and verse are part of the text"; "the Old Testament is Judaism's Bible with a Christian sequel" (the Tanakh is a living Scripture read on its own terms by Jews today, and the course will say so whenever it speaks of it); "forty authors over 1,500 years" (only on conservative dating; say "roughly a thousand to fifteen hundred years depending on when you date the earliest books").
- **Predict / checkpoint:** predict how many books are in a Bible before the count is given (most guess wrong in both directions); predict what the last book of the Tanakh is; checkpoint after the four-section scheme: "1 Kings 17, what kind of book, and what would you expect to find?"
- **Practice:** with a physical or app Bible, time yourself finding ten references drawn from every section; then read 2 Chronicles 36:22–23 and Malachi 4:4–6 back to back and write two sentences on how each ending sets up what a reader expects next.
- **Sources:** Fee & Stuart 2014 ch. 1; Longman & Dillard 2006 (introduction); Hayes RLST 145 lecture 1 ("The Parts of the Whole"); Josephus, *Against Apion* 1.8 for the 22-book count; *Bava Batra* 14b–15a for the order; SOURCES.md on Langton and Estienne (chapter/verse counts and "70 hours aloud" are via secondary; flag or omit).
- **Connects:** everything depends on being able to find things; lesson 2 puts the shelves on a timeline; lesson 8 returns to the counts.

### 2. One story: Genesis to Revelation in a single sitting (~45 min)
- **Opens with:** Luke 24:27, "beginning with Moses and all the Prophets, he interpreted to them in all the Scriptures the things concerning himself."
- **Objectives:** place any book of the Bible on the timeline of the one story; explain the covenants (Noah, Abraham, Sinai, David, the new covenant) as the story's spine; apply Fee & Stuart's three levels of narrative to a passage; explain why a passage's place in the story changes what it can mean.
- **Core ideas:** the six-act scheme from Bartholomew & Goheen (creation; fall; Israel; Jesus; church; new creation), adopted as this course's teaching device, with Roberts's eight-stage kingdom scheme and Goldsworthy named as alternatives, and the plain statement that these schemes are teaching devices, not Scripture's own outline. The covenants as the spine. Where each block of books sits (the Pentateuch, the conquest and judges, the united and divided kingdoms, exile, return, the four-hundred-year gap, Jesus, the church). The three levels of narrative (the whole plan of God; Israel; the individual story) and why the top level is the one most readers never see. The course's own reading stated as such: we read the whole as pointing to Christ because Jesus did (Luke 24), and we say that a Jewish reader reads the same books as a different story with its own integrity.
- **Mechanism:** meaning depends on position. A promise given to exiles in Babylon, a law given at Sinai, a psalm sung in the second Temple: each carries its place in the story with it. A reader who knows where they are can ask "what did this mean *then*?", which is the basic rule of lesson 3 in its simplest form.
- **Worked examples:** (a) simple: Ruth. Read badly as a stand-alone romance; read well as a story in the days of the judges that ends with David's genealogy, so that a famine, a foreigner, and a kinsman-redeemer become the way God keeps the promise to Abraham on its way to the king. Then the Jewish placement (among the Writings, read at Shavuot) as a reminder that position is a reading decision. (b) With a wrinkle: Jeremiah 29:11, "plans to prosper you". Read badly as a personal promise on a graduation card; read well as a letter to exiles told to build houses and wait seventy years, so the "plans" include the exile. Then the harder question: may a Christian still take comfort from it? Yes, but through the new covenant and the promise it belongs to, not by lifting the sentence out. This previews the "comparable particulars" test of lesson 7.
- **Misconceptions:** "the Old Testament is law, the New Testament is grace" (Marcion's move, rejected by the church in the second century; grace is in Exodus 34:6 and law is in the Sermon on the Mount); "Christians don't need the Old Testament" (the New quotes it hundreds of times; Jesus taught from it); "the timeline is the order of the books".
- **Predict / checkpoint:** predict where the four-hundred-year gap falls and which books are on each side; predict who the audience of Jeremiah 29 is before it's revealed; checkpoint: "Nehemiah 8: which act, which covenant, and what is being renewed?"
- **Practice:** draw the six acts on one page and place ten named books on it without looking; then read Jeremiah 29:1–14 whole and write the original addressee, the original promise, and one sentence on what a Christian may and may not take from it.
- **Sources:** Bartholomew & Goheen 2024; Roberts 2002; Goldsworthy 1991; Fee & Stuart 2014 ch. 5 (three levels); Luke 24; Jeremiah 29; the Marcion evidence in Bruce 1988. For the Jewish reading of the same books: Hayes RLST 145 lecture 1 and *The Jewish Study Bible* introduction.
- **Connects:** builds on lesson 1's shelves; the timeline is used in every genre lesson to answer "what did it mean then?"; lesson 4 returns to the covenants in detail.
- **Spaced review of:** lesson 1's Tanakh/Old Testament order (the two endings, now explained as two ways of reading the same story).
- **Neutrality note:** the historicity of the events on the timeline (patriarchs, exodus, conquest, the size of David's kingdom) is named as argued and deferred to lesson 10 and *Hard Questions*. The Tel Dan stele (1993, "House of David") and the exile are given as established.

### 3. Genre first: the reading rule that changes everything, and how to read narrative (~45 min)
- **Opens with:** 1 Samuel 17:47, "the battle is the LORD's", and the question of who the hero of the chapter is.
- **Objectives:** explain why identifying the kind of writing is the first interpretive decision and state Fee & Stuart's basic rule; apply the ten principles for Old Testament narrative to a story; distinguish description from prescription in a narrative; identify when a narrative's silence is being read as approval.
- **Core ideas:** the Fee & Stuart method: what kind of writing is this, what are the rules of that kind, then read. The basic rule: "a text cannot mean what it never could have meant to its author or readers". Narrative is over forty percent of the Old Testament and the Old Testament is three-quarters of the Bible, so this is the genre most readers meet most. The ten principles, especially: narratives don't usually teach doctrine directly; they record what happened, not what should have happened; people in them are often examples of the opposite; God is the hero of all biblical narratives. The narrator's voice (what the text itself evaluates, as at 2 Samuel 11:27) against what it merely reports.
- **Mechanism:** genres are contracts between writer and reader. A first-century reader opening a letter, a Psalm, or a vision knew which rules applied without being told, the way you know a recipe is not a novel. We have lost the reflexes and have to learn them as rules. Get the contract wrong and every sentence is misread while feeling perfectly clear.
- **Worked examples:** (a) simple: David and Goliath. Read badly as "face your giants"; read well on all three levels: an individual story of an unlikely shepherd, the story of Israel's true king against Saul's failure, and God's rescue of his people through the king he chose. Show the narrator's clues (the armour Saul offers, 17:38–39; the speech in 17:45–47). (b) With a wrinkle: Gideon's fleece (Judges 6:36–40). Read badly as a method for getting guidance; read well as a story of doubt in which God is patient, framed by a promise already given (6:14–16). The wrinkle: the story does not condemn Gideon, and readers take silence as approval; principle: a narrative reports, and the evaluation must come from elsewhere in Scripture.
- **Misconceptions:** "a literal reading is the faithful reading" (the Chicago Statement itself says poetry must be read as poetry; genre first is the conservative position, not a concession); "everyone in the story is a role model"; "if the Bible records it, the Bible recommends it"; "Genesis 1 must be read as one particular genre" (a live question among Christians; pointed to *Faith and Science*).
- **Predict / checkpoint:** predict who the hero of 1 Samuel 17 is on each level before the answer; predict what the text says about Gideon's fleece afterwards (nothing); checkpoint after the mechanism: "Judges 11, Jephthah's vow: which principle stops the reader treating this as a model, and what does the narrator actually evaluate?"
- **Practice:** read Genesis 25:19–34 and 27 (Jacob and Esau) and write, in three lines, the story on each level; mark every place the text evaluates a character and every place it merely reports; then say what a reader would get wrong by treating Jacob as a model.
- **Sources:** Fee & Stuart 2014 chs. 1 and 5 (the basic rule, the statistics, the ten principles); Duvall & Hays 2020 (the Interpretive Journey as a second statement of the same method); Osborne 2006 and Vanhoozer 1998 for one paragraph on why "what it means to me" is not the first question; Chicago Statement 1978, Article XIII and exposition; BibleProject "Plot, Character, Setting" videos.
- **Connects:** lesson 2 gave the top level of narrative; this lesson gives the method every genre lesson uses; lesson 6 applies narrative rules to the Gospels and Acts.
- **Spaced review of:** the three levels (lesson 2), now applied to a single chapter.

### 4. Covenant made, covenant enforced: reading the law and the prophets (~50 min)
- **Opens with:** Leviticus 19:18 and 19:19 side by side: "love your neighbour as yourself" and "do not wear clothing woven of two kinds of material", separated by one verse.
- **Objectives:** explain what the Old Testament law is (covenant stipulations for Israel) and compare the main Christian proposals for how it applies now; explain what a prophet was and apply the near-and-far-horizon reading to an oracle; identify "prophecy as prediction" errors in a reading; state the range of views on how the Old Testament relates to the New in each view's own terms.
- **Core ideas:** the law as the terms of the Sinai covenant (Exodus 19–24), read in its ancient Near Eastern treaty form; the 613 as a Jewish count (Maimonides), given as such. How a Christian reads it now, the proposals presented as a range: the traditional moral/civil/ceremonial division (Aquinas, the Westminster Confession); Fee & Stuart's view that the old covenant is not our covenant and what is not renewed in the new is not binding as command but all of it is God's word to us; covenant theology and dispensationalism as the two frameworks behind different answers, with progressive covenantalism and progressive dispensationalism between; the Jewish reading, for which the law is not a problem to be solved but the gift at the heart of the covenant. Then the prophets: covenant enforcers speaking to their own generation, calling Israel back to Deuteronomy's blessings and curses; "think oracles"; the prophetic books as collections of oracles, not continuous argument; near horizon and far horizon; Fee & Stuart's estimate (theirs, unsourced, quoted as such) that less than two percent of Old Testament prophecy is messianic, less than five percent describes the new-covenant age, less than one percent concerns events still future to us.
- **Mechanism:** law and prophecy are two halves of one relationship. Stipulations mean nothing without a covenant; enforcement means nothing without stipulations. Read Amos with Deuteronomy 28 open and the "predictions" turn out to be the covenant's own sanctions read aloud to people who had signed them. Once the learner sees that, "prophecy means telling the future" collapses on its own.
- **Worked examples:** (a) simple: Leviticus 19. Take the two verses side by side and show how each proposal sorts them, honestly including where each proposal struggles (the moral/civil/ceremonial lines are not drawn in the text; the "renewed in the new covenant" test needs a full New Testament in hand). Then Amos 5:21–24 read badly as a prediction and well as a covenant lawsuit, with Deuteronomy 28 open. (b) With a wrinkle: Isaiah 7:14. The near horizon (a sign to Ahaz in his own lifetime, 7:16) and the far horizon Matthew 1:23 reads in it. Give the Jewish reading in its own terms (*almah*, a young woman, a child born in Ahaz's day), the Christian reading in its own terms (the Septuagint's *parthenos*, Matthew's fulfilment, the fuller sense the apostles found), and say which we hold and why, and that a reader in either tradition should recognise their view here.
- **Misconceptions:** "prophecy means prediction"; "the law is abolished so the Old Testament is irrelevant" and its mirror "Christians are inconsistent for eating shellfish while quoting Leviticus 18" (the honest answer is that every Christian tradition has a principled way of sorting, and the lesson has just shown three); "the prophets wrote books" (they spoke; the books are collections); "Isaiah 53 is obviously about Jesus and only a hostile reader could miss it" (Jewish tradition reads the servant as Israel, with reasons; the course gives them).
- **Predict / checkpoint:** predict, before the figures, what percentage of Old Testament prophecy is about the Messiah; predict what Deuteronomy 28 says will happen to an unfaithful Israel before reading Amos 4:6–11; checkpoint after the mechanism: "Hosea 4:1–3: what is the charge, which covenant is being enforced, and what is the sanction?"
- **Practice:** read Deuteronomy 28:15–24 and then Amos 4:6–12, and list every sanction Amos repeats; then read Micah 6:1–8 and identify the lawsuit form (summons, witnesses, charge, verdict). Finally, pick one law from Exodus 21–23 and write how each of the three proposals would handle it.
- **Sources:** Fee & Stuart 2014 chs. 9 and 10; Duvall & Hays 2020 (crossing the river, the width of the covenant difference); Longman & Dillard 2006 on the Pentateuch and the prophets; Hayes RLST 145 lectures on biblical law and on Amos and Hosea; SOURCES.md Contested question 5 (the four positions); Isaiah 7 with the LXX and Matthew 1; *The Jewish Study Bible* note on Isaiah 7:14 for the Jewish reading in its own words; BibleProject "The Law" and "The Prophets" videos.
- **Connects:** lesson 2's covenants are now the operating system; lesson 6's kingdom and lesson 7's Revelation both lean on the prophets; lesson 10 returns to who wrote the Pentateuch.
- **Spaced review of:** the covenants (lesson 2); the basic rule (lesson 3), now applied to a prophet's own audience.
- **Neutrality note:** this lesson carries contested question 5 (Old Testament to New) and the Isaiah 7:14 Turing test for a Jewish reader. Authorship of the Pentateuch is named as argued and deferred to lesson 10 so that this lesson stays about reading.

### 5. Reading poetry, the Psalms, and wisdom (~45 min)
- **Opens with:** Proverbs 26:4 and 26:5, "do not answer a fool according to his folly" and "answer a fool according to his folly", one after the other.
- **Objectives:** identify parallelism and imagery in a Hebrew poem and explain what they do; classify a psalm by type (lament, praise, thanksgiving, royal, wisdom, imprecatory) and read it by its shape; explain why a proverb is not a promise; explain how Job and Ecclesiastes function as counter-voices within wisdom.
- **Core ideas:** Hebrew poetry works by parallel lines, not rhyme (Psalm 19:1 as the demonstration); imagery is the argument, not decoration. The Psalms as Israel's prayer book and songbook: prayers to God that became God's word to us. The types and their shapes; the lament's turn (Psalm 13: address, complaint, petition, trust, praise). Wisdom as generalisation: Proverbs describes how life usually goes under God's order; Job and Ecclesiastes are in the same canon so that nobody mistakes "usually" for "always". Imprecatory psalms as vengeance handed over to God rather than taken.
- **Mechanism:** poetry compresses; the reader supplies what a prose writer would have spelled out. Reading a psalm as a list of propositions strips out exactly the part that carries the meaning. A proverb compresses in a different way: it states a pattern in the fewest possible words and leaves the exceptions to the reader's judgement, which is what wisdom *is*.
- **Worked examples:** (a) simple: Psalm 13. Read badly as a list of doctrinal claims (God forgets, then God is good, which is it?); read well as a lament with a shape, where the movement from verse 1 to verse 6 is the point. Then Psalm 19:1 for parallelism. (b) With a wrinkle: Proverbs 22:6, "train up a child", read badly as a guarantee to parents, read well as a generalisation, with Proverbs 26:4–5 as proof that proverbs are situational by design and Job 42:7 (God rebukes the friends who quoted true-sounding wisdom at the wrong moment) as the canon's own warning. A second wrinkle: Psalm 137:9, read badly as an endorsement, read well as a prayer from Babylon that hands the rage to God.
- **Misconceptions:** "a proverb is a promise"; "the Psalms teach doctrine directly" (they teach it through prayer, which is different); "Ecclesiastes is a cynic who got in by mistake"; "the violent psalms should be skipped"; "Hebrew poetry rhymes in the original".
- **Predict / checkpoint:** predict what Psalm 13 will say by its last verse after reading only its first two; predict which of Proverbs 26:4 and 26:5 is "correct"; checkpoint after the mechanism: "Psalm 42: what type is it, where is the turn, and what does the refrain do?"
- **Practice:** read Psalm 22 whole and mark the turn; then read Ecclesiastes 1:1–11 and Proverbs 3:1–10 back to back and write one paragraph on how the two voices are both wisdom. Then find three parallel pairs in Psalm 1 and say what the second line adds each time.
- **Sources:** Fee & Stuart 2014 chs. 11 and 12; Longman & Dillard 2006 on Psalms, Proverbs, Job, Ecclesiastes; Hayes RLST 145 lectures on wisdom and on Psalms; BibleProject "Poetry", "Metaphor", "Wisdom" videos.
- **Connects:** the genre reflex from lesson 3 applied to compressed writing; lesson 6 uses the same reflex on parables.
- **Spaced review of:** the basic rule (lesson 3) and Israel's exile (lesson 2), the setting of Psalm 137.

### 6. Reading the Gospels, the parables, and Acts (~50 min)
- **Opens with:** Luke 15:1–2, "the Pharisees and the scribes grumbled", the setting that every reading of the prodigal son depends on.
- **Objectives:** explain why there are four Gospels and how to read one story in four portraits; apply the "one point with a punch" rule to a parable and identify allegorising; distinguish what Acts describes from what it prescribes; state the conservative and critical dating and authorship of the Gospels in each side's own terms and say what both agree on.
- **Core ideas:** the Gospels as a genre of their own (ancient biography with a theological aim; John 21:25 on selection); four portraits, one Jesus; the kingdom of God as the centre of his preaching; the Synoptic relationship at the "know it exists" level; topical arrangement (the Temple cleansing in John 2 and in the Synoptics' final week) as something the Chicago Statement itself allows. Parables as stories with one main point and a punch aimed at the first hearers; the history of allegorising (Augustine on the good Samaritan, given fairly as the reading of a great teacher we do not follow). Acts: history first, precedent only where Scripture elsewhere commands it. Who wrote the Gospels and when: Papias (via Eusebius 3.39) and Irenaeus 3.1.1 as the earliest testimony; the critical mainstream (Mark c. 70, Matthew and Luke c. 80–85, John c. 90–95, anonymous, with Mark 13 as the lever) and the conservative case (Mark late 50s–60s, Acts before c. 62, John 80s–90s, the names early and unanimous, Mark 13 as prophecy, Robinson's *Redating* as a non-conservative who dated everything before 70), and what both agree on: every Gospel within about seventy years of the crucifixion, Paul earlier, no fourth-century composition.
- **Mechanism:** a parable works on the hearer the way a trap works on the foot: you walk in agreeing and find yourself caught (Nathan to David, 2 Samuel 12, as the Old Testament model). Allegorising defuses the trap by turning the story into a code, which is why it feels pious and misses the point. Acts, like all narrative (lesson 3), reports; the reader who turns every report into a rule builds a church out of Acts 2:44 and Acts 1:26 alike.
- **Worked examples:** (a) simple: the prodigal son (Luke 15:11–32). Read badly as a coded allegory (the ring is X, the robe is Y) and as a story about the younger son alone; read well with 15:1–2 as the target and the older brother as the punch, the story left open with the older brother outside. (b) With a wrinkle: Acts 2:44–45 and 4:32–35 (goods in common) against Acts 1:26 (casting lots for an apostle). Both are described, neither is commanded; show the test (is it commanded elsewhere? is it consistent with the rest of the book?) and where honest readers disagree (baptism and the Spirit in Acts 8, 10, 19, given as a real intra-Christian debate). A third, brief: the two Temple cleansings, read badly as a contradiction to be forced, read well as topical arrangement, with the honest note that some readers hold there were two events.
- **Misconceptions:** "the Gospels were written hundreds of years later" (no scholar of any stripe holds this; four centuries is nobody's position); "the parables are allegories with a key"; "if the early church did it, we must"; "the differences between the Gospels are errors" and its mirror "the differences are all easily harmonised" (say what topical arrangement is and where genuine puzzles remain); "the lost gospels were suppressed" (Thomas, Peter, Judas as second- and third-century texts; say what they are and why they were not received, without sneering; Martin's Thomas lecture as the other side).
- **Predict / checkpoint:** predict who the prodigal son is aimed at before 15:1–2 is shown; predict which decade a critical scholar and a conservative each date Mark to; checkpoint after the parable mechanism: "Luke 18:1–8, the unjust judge: what is the one point, and what goes wrong if you make the judge stand for God?"
- **Practice:** read Mark 4:1–34 and, for each parable, write the one point and the punch; then read Acts 6:1–7 and write what it describes, what (if anything) it prescribes, and how you decided.
- **Sources:** Fee & Stuart 2014 chs. 6, 7, 8; Carson & Moo 2005 for the conservative dates (not opened at Stage 1; ranges must be confirmed against the book in the fact-check); Ehrman, *Historical Introduction*, for the critical dates; Papias in Eusebius 3.39; Irenaeus 3.1.1; Robinson 1976; Wright & Bird 2019 for Go Deeper; Martin RLST 152 lectures on Mark, Thomas, and the Gospels as history; Chicago Statement Article XIII ("topical arrangement of material", "variant selections of material in parallel accounts"); BibleProject "The Gospel" and "Parables" videos.
- **Connects:** lesson 3's narrative rules applied to Gospels and Acts; lesson 4's prophets behind the kingdom; lesson 9 handles the manuscripts of the Gospels; *The Case for Christianity* and *The Life and Teachings of Jesus* take it from here.
- **Spaced review of:** description versus prescription (lesson 3); the kingdom in the covenants (lessons 2 and 4).
- **Neutrality note:** this lesson carries contested question 3. Both dating positions in their own terms, both ranges given, the shared ground stated, and the course's own reliability case explicitly not resting on the early dates.

### 7. Reading the letters and Revelation (~50 min)
- **Opens with:** Philippians 4:13, "I can do all things through him who strengthens me", and then verses 11 and 12.
- **Objectives:** apply "think paragraphs" and the occasional nature of a letter to a passage; apply the basic rule and the "comparable particulars" test to decide what a first-century instruction asks of a reader now; explain how apocalyptic works (symbol, Old Testament allusion, vision as picture) and read a passage of Revelation by those conventions; compare the four main schools of reading Revelation in their own terms.
- **Core ideas:** letters are occasional: written to someone, about something, and we hear one side of the call. Think paragraphs, not verses. The basic rule again, now doing real work: a text cannot mean what it could not have meant to its first readers. Comparable particulars: where our situation matches theirs, the word applies directly; where it doesn't, find the principle the particular expresses, and be honest that Christians disagree about which particulars are cultural (head coverings, holy kisses, women's roles named as the standing example, presented as a real debate with serious people on each side). Then apocalyptic: Daniel and Revelation as a known genre with conventions (numbers as symbols, images drawn from the Old Testament, visions as pictures rather than a code or a timeline); Revelation as letter, prophecy, and apocalypse at once; the preterist, historicist, futurist, and idealist readings, and the mixed readings most commentators now hold, each stated so its holders recognise it.
- **Mechanism:** a letter is half of a conversation; the meaning sits in the gap between what the writer says and what the readers already knew. Reconstructing the situation is not optional scholarship, it is reading. Apocalyptic works the other way round: it says everything and means it as picture, so the reader who decodes it into events has changed the genre.
- **Worked examples:** (a) simple: Philippians 4:13, read badly as a promise of achievement, read well as the conclusion of 4:10–13, contentment in hunger and plenty, written from prison. Then 1 Corinthians 13, read at weddings and written to a church fighting over gifts (chapters 12–14). (b) With a wrinkle: 1 Corinthians 11:2–16 (head coverings). Walk the comparable-particulars test: what is the particular, what principle does Paul himself give, do our particulars match, and why Christians land differently, with the reasons on each side given as they give them. (c) Revelation 13:18 and the number 666, read badly as a code for a modern figure, read well as gematria in a first-century Roman context (Nero Caesar as the common scholarly reading, given as argued), and Revelation 12 read as a picture built from Genesis 3 and the exodus rather than a timeline.
- **Misconceptions:** "a verse means what it says on its own"; "everything Paul told the Corinthians is for us as written" and its mirror "everything cultural can be dismissed"; "Revelation is a coded timeline of the modern world"; "Revelation was written about events already past, so it has nothing to say" (the preterist reading does not say this; give it fairly); "apocalyptic is prophecy, so lesson 4's rules apply unchanged" (they overlap and differ; say how).
- **Predict / checkpoint:** predict what Philippians 4:11–12 says before it is shown; predict how many "senses" 1 Corinthians 13 changes when chapter 12 is read first; checkpoint after the letters mechanism: "Philemon: reconstruct the situation from the letter alone in four lines"; a second checkpoint after apocalyptic: "Revelation 1:12–16: name three Old Testament sources for the images."
- **Practice:** read Philemon (one sitting, 25 verses) and write the situation, the request, and the one paragraph that carries the argument; then read Revelation 4–5 and list every image you can trace to Isaiah 6, Ezekiel 1, or Daniel 7, and write in one sentence what the picture is doing rather than what it "predicts".
- **Sources:** Fee & Stuart 2014 chs. 3, 4, 13; Duvall & Hays 2020 on the principlizing bridge; Osborne 2006 (also his Revelation commentary for the mixed reading); Beale, Ladd, and the Four Views volume for the schools; Martin RLST 152 apocalyptic lecture; BibleProject "Apocalyptic" video.
- **Connects:** the basic rule (lesson 3) now at full strength; the prophets and Daniel (lesson 4) as the raw material of Revelation; lesson 12's "read a whole letter" method uses Philemon again.
- **Spaced review of:** near and far horizons (lesson 4); the punch of a parable (lesson 6) as a cousin of the apocalyptic picture.
- **Neutrality note:** this lesson carries contested question 8. The four schools presented in their own terms with named defenders; the course may say which mixed reading it prefers, and does not treat any school as unserious. Ezekiel 40–48 is used as the example of how covenant and dispensational readers diverge, closing the loop from lesson 4.

### 8. How the canon formed, and why Catholic and Orthodox Bibles have more books (~50 min)
- **Opens with:** 2 Peter 3:15–16, Paul's letters ranked with "the other Scriptures", inside the New Testament itself.
- **Objectives:** explain, with dated evidence, how the Jewish canon and the New Testament canon came to be recognised, and what the Council of Nicaea did and did not do; explain why Protestant, Catholic, and Orthodox Bibles differ and give each tradition's case in its own terms; compare the two scholarly models (recognition by use; community determination) of what was happening; sort a claim about the canon as established, argued, or held by faith.
- **Core ideas:** the Jewish canon by the first century: the Sirach prologue (c. 132 BC, "the Law, the Prophets, and the rest of the books"), Josephus (c. AD 95, twenty-two books, "no one has been so bold as to add"), Luke 24:44, *Bava Batra* on order. The New Testament: the core recognised by about 180 (Irenaeus on the four Gospels; the Muratorian Fragment, c. 180–200 on the traditional dating, with the Sundberg/Hahneman challenge and the Verheyden/Schnabel reply given as argued), Marcion as the negative proof that a wider collection already existed, the edges (Hebrews, James, 2 Peter, 2–3 John, Jude, Revelation) settling through the fourth century (Eusebius 3.25's three lists; Athanasius's Festal Letter 39 in 367 as the first surviving list of exactly our 27; Hippo 393 and Carthage 397/419). The criteria the church actually applied: apostolic origin, orthodoxy, and use in worship (Bruce). The two models: Bruce and Kruger (recognition; Kruger's self-authenticating account with God's providence, the books' own attributes, and the Spirit's witness, corroborated by history) against McDonald (community determination, slow and local). The deuterocanon question from the Septuagint to Jerome's "ecclesiastical books" to Luther's 1534 heading to Trent's 1546 decree to the Synod of Jerusalem 1672 and the wider Orthodox and Ethiopian canons; the KJV of 1611 had the Apocrypha, and the 1826 Bible Society decision, with the motive left open as SOURCES.md says.
- **Mechanism:** a canon is not made by a vote; it is the shape a community's worship has already taken, written down when someone asks. That is why the evidence looks the way it does: local lists that mostly agree, a few books argued over for two centuries, and councils that confirm rather than choose. Once the learner sees the mechanism, the Nicaea story is not just false but the wrong *kind* of story.
- **Worked examples:** (a) simple: "Constantine chose the books at Nicaea in 325." Walk the dated evidence in order (Irenaeus 180; Eusebius, who attended Nicaea, listing disputed books in his own history; Athanasius forty-two years later; Hippo and Carthage sixty years later) and show that no record of Nicaea's business mentions the canon. Name *The Da Vinci Code* (2003) as where most people heard it. (b) With a wrinkle: Hebrews 11:35 and 2 Maccabees 7, and Jude 14–15 quoting 1 Enoch. What does a New Testament allusion or quotation show about a book's status? Give the Catholic reading (the early church's Bible was the Greek Septuagint with these books in it; they were read as Scripture for over a thousand years; Trent defined what was practised) and the Protestant reading (the canon Jesus and the apostles used was the Hebrew one; the New Testament never cites a deuterocanonical book *as Scripture*; Jerome and many fathers distinguished them) with Athanasius's list as the hard case that each side reads its own way (he counts Baruch with Jeremiah, omits Esther, and puts Wisdom and Tobit among books "to be read"). Say what is established (what everyone said), what is argued (how much each witness weighs; whether Alexandria had a wider canon, which most scholars now doubt), and what is held by faith (which list is Scripture). Say which canon we use and why, and that no doctrine turns on the difference except purgatory's proof text in 2 Maccabees 12, and that the deuterocanonical books are worth reading whatever one holds.
- **Misconceptions:** "Nicaea/Constantine chose the books"; "the lost gospels were suppressed" (from lesson 6, now with the criteria in hand); "the Apocrypha were added by Rome at Trent" (they were in every Christian Bible for a millennium; Trent defined, Luther set apart, and both were responding to Jerome); "Athanasius invented the list" (he reports it; the letter's own words show that); "the Protestant canon is the 'original' one" (a Catholic reader would say the Protestant list is the innovation; give the claim as each side puts it).
- **Predict / checkpoint:** predict the date of the first surviving list of exactly our 27 New Testament books; predict what Eusebius, present at Nicaea, would list as disputed; checkpoint after the mechanism: "The Shepherd of Hermas was loved, early, and read in churches. Which criterion kept it out, and who says so?"
- **Practice:** read Athanasius's Festal Letter 39 (the canon section, free at New Advent) and Eusebius 3.25 and build a two-column table: what each lists as received, disputed, or "to be read"; then read 2 Maccabees 7 and Hebrews 11:32–38 and write one paragraph each on how a Catholic and a Protestant reader would explain the link.
- **Sources:** Bruce 1988 (chs. 12 and 17 especially); Kruger 2012; McDonald 2007/2017; Sirach prologue; Josephus, *Against Apion* 1.8; *Bava Batra* 14b–15a; Papias and Irenaeus; Eusebius 3.25; Athanasius, Festal Letter 39; Muratorian Fragment with Sundberg 1973, Hahneman 1992, Verheyden 2003, Schnabel 2014, Rothschild 2022; Hippo and Carthage; Jerome, *Prologus Galeatus*; Luther 1534; Trent Session IV (the reported vote is via secondary; give it only if confirmed); Synod of Jerusalem 1672; Martin RLST 152 lecture 2 as the other side; Kruger's BiblicalTraining lectures for Go Deeper.
- **Connects:** answers the question lesson 1 deferred; lesson 9 asks the next question (how do we know the words); lesson 10 asks what we make of all this.
- **Spaced review of:** lesson 1's book counts and the Tanakh's three parts; lesson 2's Luke 24.
- **Neutrality note:** this lesson carries contested question 1. The Catholic and Orthodox readers are the audit's primary check here; a Catholic should be able to say "yes, that is our case and our reasons". The words "Apocrypha" and "deuterocanonical" are both used and each is attributed to the tradition that uses it.

### 9. How the text reached us, and how reliable it is (~50 min)
- **Opens with:** Mark 16:8, and then the bracket or footnote the learner's own Bible prints before verse 9.
- **Objectives:** describe how the Old and New Testament texts were copied and what the Dead Sea Scrolls, the Masoretic codices, and the Greek manuscripts show; explain what a textual variant is, roughly how many there are, and what kinds; read the three famous passages in a modern Bible and explain the note; state fairly what textual criticism has established about reliability and what remains argued.
- **Core ideas:** materials (papyrus, parchment, scroll, codex) and how scribes worked; the kinds of scribal error (sight, hearing, memory, judgement) and why most variants are spelling and word order. The Old Testament: the Ketef Hinnom silver scrolls (late 7th/early 6th century BC, the priestly blessing of Numbers 6, the oldest surviving biblical text); the Dead Sea Scrolls (1947–56, over 230 biblical manuscripts on the IAA count, every book but Esther; the Great Isaiah Scroll, c. 125 BC by palaeography, about a thousand years older than the Masoretic codices and agreeing with them overwhelmingly, with the honest second half: the scrolls also show more than one textual form in circulation); the Aleppo and Leningrad codices and why every modern Old Testament rests on Leningrad. The New Testament: about 5,800 Greek manuscripts on the INTF register, most medieval and fragmentary, so the age and spread are the argument, not the count; P52 ("second century", not "125", after Nongbri 2005), P46 (c. 200), P75 (c. 200–225), Sinaiticus and Vaticanus (4th century); the variant count around 400,000 by Wallace's and Ehrman's shared way of counting, the vast majority untranslatable; Wallace's "under one percent meaningful and viable" only if confirmed. What is established (the wording is recoverable to a very high degree; no article of the creeds depends on a disputed reading, as Metzger and Ehrman both say in print); what is argued (whether some early variants were doctrinally motivated, Ehrman 1993 against Wallace and Hurtado; Mark's original ending; whether "original text" is a recoverable concept, Epp and Parker).
- **Mechanism:** many independent copies are better than one perfect one. When copies diverge, the divergence itself locates the error, because an error made in Alexandria does not appear in Rome unless it was there first. That is the whole method in one sentence, and it is why more manuscripts, older manuscripts, and more widely spread manuscripts each help in a different way, and why the "telephone game" is the wrong picture: telephone is one chain, this is a tree.
- **Worked examples:** (a) simple: Mark 16:9–20 in the learner's own Bible. What the note says, what Sinaiticus and Vaticanus show, what Metzger's *Textual Commentary* says about vocabulary and style, what changes if the passage is not Mark's (nothing in the creeds; the snake-handling verse) and what does not (the resurrection is announced in 16:6). Then John 7:53–8:11 as the same shape (early witnesses lack it; it floats; most scholars think it a genuine early tradition not originally in John; double-bracketed in NA28). (b) With a wrinkle: 1 John 5:7–8, the Johannine Comma. A KJV reader finds the clearest Trinity verse in the Bible; a modern reader does not. No Greek manuscript before the 14th century; Erasmus's third edition of 1522; the RV of 1881 onward. The wrinkle is that the doctrine does not depend on it (the Trinity was defined at Nicaea and Constantinople from other texts), and that the KJV is not "wrong" but working from a late Greek text, which is the setup for lesson 11. A third, brief, for the Old Testament: Isaiah 53:11, where the Great Isaiah Scroll and the Septuagint read "he will see light" and the Masoretic text lacks "light"; show the footnote in NIV or ESV, so the learner sees a variant that a modern translation resolved *using* the scrolls (verify the reading against Tov and the BHS apparatus in the fact-check).
- **Misconceptions:** "it's been translated so many times it can't be trusted" (two links, not fifty; modern translations go straight to Hebrew and Greek editions); "the Dead Sea Scrolls prove not a word has changed" (both halves: remarkably stable and also plural); "400,000 variants means the text is hopeless" (what counts as a variant, and what kind most are); "there are no variants that matter" (three do, and the learner has just read them); "the Bible was written centuries after the events" (already handled for the Gospels in lesson 6; the Old Testament gap is real and larger, and this lesson says so rather than letting the New Testament answer stand for both).
- **Predict / checkpoint:** predict the number of Greek New Testament manuscripts before it is given, then predict what fraction are older than AD 400 (few); predict what Ehrman, the field's most famous sceptic, says about whether essential Christian beliefs are affected by variants, before his own words are quoted; checkpoint after the mechanism: "Two manuscripts disagree at one word. One is older, one is from a different region. Which matters more and why can't you say without more information?"
- **Practice:** in your own Bible, find and read the notes at Mark 16:9, John 7:53, and 1 John 5:7, and write in one sentence each what the note claims and what evidence it rests on; then open the Israel Museum's Great Isaiah Scroll viewer at Isaiah 53 and find the column, so the thousand-year-old text has been seen with your own eyes.
- **Sources:** Metzger & Ehrman 2005; Metzger, *Textual Commentary* 1994; Tov 2012; Wegner 1999; the INTF *Kurzgefasste Liste* figures by year; the IAA Leon Levy Library count; the Rylands, Chester Beatty/Michigan, and British Library manuscript descriptions for dates; Nongbri 2005 on P52; Wallace 2013; Ehrman 2005 with its appendix (quote via secondary until confirmed); Ehrman 1993; Wallace's BiblicalTraining lectures for Go Deeper; Ketef Hinnom and Tel Dan from the standard excavation reports as cited in SOURCES.md.
- **Connects:** lesson 8 gave the list of books; this lesson gives the words; lesson 11 explains why the KJV and the NIV differ at the three passages; *The Case for Christianity* uses the established result without repeating the evidence.
- **Spaced review of:** chapter and verse divisions (lesson 1), now seen as a layer added long after the manuscripts; the Gospels' dating (lesson 6) as the reason the early papyri matter.
- **Neutrality note:** this lesson carries contested question 6. The reliability claim is given as established, with Metzger and Ehrman as the two named witnesses precisely because they disagree about almost everything else; the mechanism questions are given as argued with both sides named.

### 10. What we believe about this book, and what others make of it (~45 min)
- **Opens with:** 2 Timothy 3:16, "all Scripture is God-breathed", and the question of what exactly that claim covers.
- **Objectives:** explain inspiration, inerrancy, infallibility, and authority as the Christian tradition uses the words and compare the main positions within Christianity in their own terms; describe the critical-scholarly view of the Bible so a Yale student would recognise it; sort a given claim about the Bible as established by history, argued from evidence, or held by faith; state what Christians of every position share.
- **Core ideas:** the course's own position stated in the first person plural and built as an argument, not asserted: we trust the Bible because Jesus did (Luke 24; Wenham's argument), because the apostles ranked their writings with Scripture (2 Peter 3:16), because the church has always received it as inspired, and because we have found it true. The range within Christianity, each in its own words: the Chicago Statement (1978; Article X on the autographs; Article XIII's list of what inerrancy does not exclude, "history must be treated as history, poetry as poetry"); infallibility (Fuller after 1972; Lausanne 1974's "without error in all that it affirms", read both ways); *Dei Verbum* 11 (1965; "for the sake of salvation", with the scope debated inside Catholicism); Orthodoxy (Scripture within Tradition, read in the Church); the mainline Confession of 1967 (the normative witness to the Word, read with literary and historical criticism); Enns's incarnational model. The historical facts that inerrancy as a slogan is modern and that the church has always called Scripture inspired and authoritative, and the Rogers & McKim versus Woodbridge dispute about which of those the tradition really held. The critical view in its own terms: the Bible as a library of ancient documents to be read like any other, with sources, seams, and development, taught (in Hayes's and Martin's words) without asking the student to believe or disbelieve. Then the two authorship questions the course deferred: the Pentateuch (Wellhausen's four sources; the neo-documentarian and supplementary models; the conservative "essentially Mosaic" with later editing; the evidence each side cites, Genesis 36:31 and Deuteronomy 34 among it; what would settle it, and that nothing available now will) and the historicity of the Old Testament narratives (established: David's house on the Tel Dan stele, Ketef Hinnom, the exile, Pilate's inscription; argued: the exodus, the conquest, the size of David's kingdom; faith: the rest, where archaeology is silent).
- **Mechanism:** the three-kind sort the course has used since lesson 1 is now stated as a tool. Most quarrels about the Bible are quarrels between someone asserting a faith claim as history and someone denying a history claim because of a faith disagreement. Naming which kind of claim is on the table ends most of them and clarifies the rest. The mechanism of *disagreement within Christianity* is also worth showing: nearly every position above accepts genre, phenomena, and the human authors; the differences are about what "without error" ranges over.
- **Worked examples:** (a) simple: Mark 4:31, the mustard seed as "the smallest of all seeds". Read badly as a botanical error that ends the conversation, or as a botanical fact that must be defended; read well by every position above (observational language and proverbial hyperbole; the Chicago Statement says so in its own text; Enns says so; Hayes would say so), which shows that genre and phenomena are common ground. (b) With a wrinkle: Deuteronomy 34 and Genesis 36:31. Moses' death narrated in the book Moses wrote; "before any king reigned over the Israelites" written as if by someone who has seen kings. Show how the critical reader uses these (seams and dates), how the conservative reader uses them ("essentially Mosaic", later editorial updating, which even Chicago allows), and how Longman's phrase is the honest mainstream evangelical position. Then sort the whole question: argued, majority in universities one way, majority in evangelical seminaries the other, not settleable by evidence in hand. Say what we hold, and that a Christian may reasonably hold either.
- **Misconceptions:** "inerrancy means reading everything literally" (Article XIII is the refutation, in the inerrantists' own statement); "non-inerrantist Christians don't believe the Bible" (they say what they believe in their own words above); "critical scholars are atheists" (many are practising Jews and Christians; Brown's Catholic introduction; Enns); "the church invented inerrancy in 1978" and its mirror "the church always used the word" (both wrong in a specific way; say how); "archaeology has disproved the Old Testament" and "archaeology has proved the Old Testament" (neither; say what it has done).
- **Predict / checkpoint:** predict whether the Chicago Statement allows for round numbers and topical arrangement before Article XIII is quoted; predict how Hayes teaches Genesis 1 (against *Enuma Elish*, without asking anyone to believe or disbelieve); checkpoint after the mechanism: "'The Gospels were written by eyewitnesses': which kind of claim, and what would each of three readers say?"
- **Practice:** take five claims (the Tel Dan stele names David's house; Moses wrote Deuteronomy; the Bible is without error in all it affirms; Isaiah 7:14 predicts Jesus; Paul wrote Romans) and sort each as established, argued, or faith, writing one line of reason; then read Chicago Article XIII and *Dei Verbum* 11 and write one paragraph on what they share and one on where they differ.
- **Sources:** Chicago Statement 1978 (ETS archive); *Dei Verbum* 11; Lausanne Covenant 1974; Confession of 1967; Ware and the OCA volume for Orthodoxy; Enns 2015; Wenham 2009; Rogers & McKim 1979 and Woodbridge 1982; Longman & Dillard 2006 ("essentially Mosaic"); Collins 2018 and Hayes RLST 145 lectures 2–4 for the critical position on the Pentateuch; Baden and the neo-documentarians as named in SOURCES.md; Cassuto, Kikawada & Quinn, Garrett for the conservative literary reply; Tel Dan, Ketef Hinnom, the Caesarea inscription as in SOURCES.md; the JSR symposium "For the Sake of Our Salvation" (2016) for the intra-Catholic debate.
- **Connects:** the synthesis of lessons 8 and 9, with the deferred questions from lessons 2, 4, and 6 closed here; *Faith and Science* and *Hard Questions* take the origins and difficulty questions further; the neutral *World Religions* describes the same faith from outside.
- **Spaced review of:** genre first (lesson 3) as the shared ground; the three-kind sort as it was used in lessons 2, 4, 6, 8, 9.
- **Neutrality note:** this lesson carries contested questions 2, 4, and 7 and is where the inerrantist and critical-scholar Turing tests are hardest. The inerrantist must not find that the course has quietly conceded the critical view while meaning only to describe it; the critical scholar must not find a straw version. The course states its own view once, labelled, and does not repeat it as a refrain.

### 11. Choosing a translation (~40 min)
- **Opens with:** Amos 4:6 in the KJV ("cleanness of teeth") and the NIV ("empty stomachs"): the same Hebrew, two honest translations, and the whole lesson in one verse.
- **Objectives:** explain what source texts modern translations use and why the KJV differs from them; compare formal, functional, and free translation and place the major English versions on the spectrum; explain the gender-language question in each side's own terms; choose a translation for reading and one for study with reasons you can defend.
- **Core ideas:** the source texts (BHS/BHQ from Leningrad; NA28/UBS5 from the manuscripts of lesson 9; the Textus Receptus behind the KJV and NKJV, and why that explains 1 John 5:7 and Mark 16). Wycliffe's 2025 figures (full Bible in 776 languages, New Testament in 1,798 more, 544 with nothing started) for scale. The spectrum: formal equivalence (KJV, NASB, NKJV, ESV, NRSV), functional (NIV, CSB's "optimal equivalence", NLT), free (The Message); Fee & Stuart's verdict that the basic reading and study Bible should be "something in the NIV/NRSV range" with a formal version "helpful as a second source", given as their advice and ours. Dates verified from publishers' prefaces (KJV 1611; RSV 1952; NASB 1971/1995/2020; NIV 1978/1984/2011; NKJV 1982; NRSV 1989/NRSVue 2021; NLT 1996/2004; ESV 2001, a revision of the RSV; HCSB 2004 to CSB 2017; NET 2005/2019). Reading-level grades given as rough and via secondary. The trade-offs stated honestly: formal preserves form and loses idiom and wordplay; functional preserves sense and hides the form; every translation interprets; "which is most accurate?" is a malformed question until you say accurate *to what* and *for what*. Gender language: what the NIV 2011, NRSV, and CSB do with *adelphoi* and generic "man", and what the ESV does, with the reasons each publisher gives in its own preface.
- **Mechanism:** translation is a chain of decisions, each a trade between the source language's form and the target language's sense, and no chain can keep both at every link. Once the learner sees the chain, the fights over "the best translation" become choices about which links to protect, and the NET's 60,932 notes become the place to watch the decisions being made.
- **Worked examples:** (a) simple: Amos 4:6 and Romans 12:20 ("heap burning coals") across NASB, NIV, NLT, and The Message, side by side on BibleGateway: what each keeps, what each loses, and which a first-time reader and a careful student would each want. (b) With a wrinkle: Psalm 1:1 ("Blessed is the man" ESV; "Blessed is the one" NIV 2011; "Happy are those" NRSV) and 1 Thessalonians 4:1 (*adelphoi* as "brothers" or "brothers and sisters"). Give the reasons on each side as their publishers give them (the ESV's concern for the singular and for the referent; the NIV's and NRSV's concern for what the Greek meant to its hearers), say that both are made by scholars who love the text, say which we use, and let the learner decide with reasons rather than tribe. A third, brief: 1 John 5:7 in the KJV and the NIV as the lesson 9 case seen from the translation side.
- **Misconceptions:** "the most literal translation is the most accurate"; "the KJV is the original" and its mirror "the KJV is unreliable" (a great translation of a late Greek text); "paraphrases are for children"; "one translation is the true one and the rest are agendas"; "translators change the text for gender reasons" and its mirror "the traditional renderings were neutral" (both sides have reasons; give them).
- **Predict / checkpoint:** predict where the NIV falls on the spectrum before the chart is shown; predict how many languages have a full Bible before the Wycliffe figure; checkpoint after the mechanism: "Isaiah 7:14: *almah* as 'virgin' (ESV) or 'young woman' (NRSV). Is this a translation decision, an interpretation decision, or both, and why can't a translator avoid it?"
- **Practice:** read John 1:1–18 in one formal, one functional, and one free translation on BibleGateway and list five places they differ, then say for each which trade-off produced the difference; then choose your reading Bible and your study Bible and write three sentences defending each choice.
- **Sources:** Fee & Stuart 2014 ch. 2; publishers' prefaces for each version (the primary sources for philosophy and dates); Wycliffe Global Alliance 2025; NET Bible notes; Wegner 1999 for the history of English versions; the ESV and NIV 2011 prefaces on gender language; reading-level grades via secondary and flagged.
- **Connects:** lesson 9's manuscripts are the source texts here; lesson 12 assumes the learner now owns two translations; *How to Study the Bible* teaches the tools that sit under the English.
- **Spaced review of:** the three famous passages (lesson 9) as translation cases; Isaiah 7:14 (lesson 4) as a translation decision.

### 12. A way of reading you can keep: method, tools, and a plan (~45 min)
- **Opens with:** Nehemiah 8:8, the Levites reading "clearly, and giving the sense, so that the people understood", and Acts 17:11, the Bereans examining the Scriptures daily.
- **Objectives:** apply a repeatable method (what kind of writing, where in the story, what did it mean then, what does it mean now) to a whole short book; use context in widening circles (sentence, paragraph, book, testament, Bible) on a passage; identify which tool answers which question (cross-references, a study Bible's notes, a concordance, STEP or Blue Letter for the original words, the NET notes); build a reading plan that survives February and say how you will keep it.
- **Core ideas:** the course's method gathered into four questions the learner can carry, mapped onto observation, interpretation, and application (Hendricks; Duvall & Hays) so the next course's vocabulary is already familiar. Context in widening circles as the single habit that prevents most misreadings the course has shown. How to start a book you have never read: the *Book by Book* pattern (orienting data, overview, advice for reading, a walk through). The tools, at the level of "this exists and answers this question", with the honest line that word studies, cross-reference technique, and commentaries belong to *How to Study the Bible*. Plans: M'Cheyne (1842; four chapters a day; Old Testament once, New Testament and Psalms twice a year), a Gospel-first plan, the BibleProject one-year plan, and the plain advice that a plan you keep beats a plan you admire; reading in community as the tradition's own norm (Nehemiah 8; Acts 17). The whole Bible read aloud is roughly 70 hours (via secondary; give as rough), which is less than most people's monthly screen time, said once and without a sermon.
- **Mechanism:** the method works because it forces the four decisions that every misreading in this course skipped: genre (lesson 3), position (lesson 2), then-meaning (the basic rule), now-meaning (comparable particulars). A plan works for the same reason retrieval and spacing work in *How to Learn Anything*: reading a little on a schedule beats reading a lot once, and a log makes the schedule real.
- **Worked examples:** (a) simple: Philemon, read whole in one sitting with the four questions, from a cold start, showing the orienting data (who, to whom, from where, why), the overview, and the one application that survives the comparable-particulars test. (b) With a wrinkle: Jonah. Four chapters, a prophet who is the anti-hero, and a genre question (narrative with satirical edge; the historicity question named and sorted, with the note that Jesus refers to Jonah in Matthew 12:40 and what each position makes of that); the ending's unanswered question (4:11) as a deliberate device; read badly as a fish story or a children's tale, read well as a book about God's mercy to enemies and a reader who resents it. The wrinkle: the method has to handle a book whose genre is itself argued.
- **Misconceptions:** "I need Hebrew and Greek to read well" (you need the tools and the questions; the languages come later if at all); "reading through in a year is the mark of a serious Christian" (a plan is a servant); "study Bible notes are Scripture" (they are one scholar's help, dated and fallible, and the learner should know who wrote them); "reading alone is the ideal" (the tradition's norm is reading together).
- **Predict / checkpoint:** predict how long the whole Bible takes to read aloud before the figure; predict what Jonah 4 will do with the plant before reading it; checkpoint after the mechanism: "Habakkuk 1:1–4: run the four questions in four lines."
- **Practice:** read Jonah whole (four chapters, one sitting) and write a half-page reading guide in the *Book by Book* pattern; then choose a plan, write the first fourteen days of it with actual passages and times, and set up the log you will use for the project.
- **Sources:** Fee & Stuart 2002 (*Book by Book*, for the pattern); Duvall & Hays 2020 (the Interpretive Journey); Hendricks's free DTS course; Fee & Stuart 2014 chs. 5 and 10 on Jonah's genre and the prophets; M'Cheyne calendar (mcheyne.info); BibleProject plan; STEP Bible, Blue Letter Bible, NET Bible, ESV.org, YouVersion; Nehemiah 8 and Acts 17; *How to Learn Anything* lessons 3 and 4 for the spacing argument.
- **Connects:** gathers lessons 2, 3, and 7 into a method; launches the project; hands on to *How to Study the Bible* and *The Story of Scripture*.
- **Spaced review of:** every genre lesson in one exercise (the checkpoint and the Jonah reading touch narrative, prophecy, and the basic rule); the timeline (lesson 2); translation choice (lesson 11).

## Assessments

- **Course-end test** (`assessments/final-test.md`): 20 items. Blueprint: 2 on layout and the two orders (lesson 1); 2 on the one story and placing a passage (lesson 2); 6 on genre, one each for narrative, law, poetry and wisdom, prophets, Gospels/parables/Acts, and letters/apocalyptic (lessons 3–7), every one of them a passage the course did not use with the question "which reading is right and why"; 3 on the canon, including one on Nicaea and one where the learner must give the Catholic case in its own terms (lesson 8); 3 on text and reliability, including one on the telephone-game claim and one on sorting a manuscript claim (lesson 9); 1 on sorting a claim as established, argued, or faith, with three readers' likely responses (lesson 10); 2 on translations (lesson 11); 1 on the method and plan (lesson 12). At least 12 items are application cases with a passage in front of the learner. Distractors are the misconceptions listed above. Pass mark 80%.
- **Project** (`assessments/project-read-one-book.md`): "Read one book by its genre, over two weeks, and write its guide." The learner picks one short book (Ruth, Jonah, Habakkuk, Mark, Philippians, or Philemon plus Colossians), writes a fourteen-day plan on day 1 (lesson 12), reads it on that plan with a dated log of what was read and one observation per sitting, reads it once more straight through at the end, and writes a two-page reading guide in the *Book by Book* pattern: orienting data (author and date with both positions if argued, audience, occasion, genre, place in the story), overview, three specific pieces of advice for reading it well, a walk through, and one paragraph on what a first-time reader will get wrong. Deliverable: the plan, the log, and the guide. Rubric (10 points): the genre is identified correctly and its rules are visibly used in the guide (3); the book is placed on the timeline and the then-meaning is stated before the now-meaning (2); every historical claim in the orienting data is sourced and any argued question is given with both positions (2); the log shows the plan was followed or honestly adjusted, with observations that could only come from reading the actual text (2); the guide would help a real first-time reader, named or imagined (1). 7/10 to pass. Because the site has no accounts, the log is the learner's own file; the guide can be shared with a friend or a study group, which is the tradition's own way of doing this.

## Neutrality notes

This is a sensitive domain (standards 3.4) and a standpoint course (3.7). The neutrality audit is mandatory for every lesson before publication and must record which of the five readers it checked against. Where each contested question from SOURCES.md is handled:

| Contested question | Lesson | How it is handled |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Extent of the canon | 8 (introduced in 1) | History established (what Josephus, Athanasius, Jerome, Luther, Trent said); weight of witnesses argued; which list is Scripture held by faith and ecclesially. Protestant, Catholic, and Orthodox cases each in their own words; the course says which canon it uses and why. |
| 2. Authorship and dating of the Pentateuch | 10 (deferred from 2 and 4) | Argued. Critical (documentary, neo-documentarian, supplementary) and conservative ("essentially Mosaic") positions with the evidence each cites; what would settle it (nothing now available); what a Christian may reasonably hold. |
| 3. Dating and authorship of the Gospels | 6 | Argued. Both ranges, both cases, the shared ground stated; the course's reliability case does not rest on the early dates. |
| 4. Inerrancy, infallibility, and other views | 10 | Value/faith question within Christianity. Chicago, infallibilist, Catholic, Orthodox, mainline, Enns each in their own words; what history establishes (the church always called Scripture inspired; "inerrancy" as a slogan is modern); the course states its own view once, labelled. |
| 5. How the Old Testament relates to the New | 4 (revisited in 7) | Contested within the faith. Covenant, dispensational, and the two progressive positions as a range; Ezekiel 40–48 as the example of divergent practice; not resolved. |
| 6. What the text-critical evidence shows | 9 | Reliability established (Metzger and Ehrman as joint witnesses); motivated variants, Mark's ending, and "original text" argued with both sides named. |
| 7. Historicity of Old Testament narratives | 10 (named in 2 and 3) | Established (Tel Dan, Ketef Hinnom, the exile, Pilate), argued (exodus, conquest, David's kingdom), faith (the rest) all named; deferred to *Hard Questions* and *Faith and Science* for depth. |
| 8. How to read Revelation | 7 | Contested within the faith. Four schools plus mixed readings, named defenders, each in its own terms; the course may say which mixed reading it prefers. |

**Readers the auditor checks against, and where each is most at risk:**
- **A Catholic reader:** lesson 8 (canon), lesson 10 (*Dei Verbum*). Should recognise the Septuagint argument, Hippo and Carthage, and "Trent defined what was practised" as their own case, and should find "deuterocanonical" used as their word.
- **An Orthodox reader:** lesson 8 (the wider canon, the Synod of Jerusalem, the *anagignoskomena*), lesson 10 (Scripture within Tradition, read in the Church). Should not find Orthodoxy folded into Catholicism.
- **A Jewish reader:** lessons 1, 2, 4, 5 (the Tanakh as a living Scripture with its own order and ending; Isaiah 7:14 and Isaiah 53 given in the Jewish reading's own terms; the law as gift, not problem). Should not find the Hebrew Bible described only as a prelude.
- **A critical scholar:** lessons 6, 8, 9, 10 (dating, canon models, textual mechanism, the Pentateuch). Should recognise Hayes's, Martin's, and Ehrman's positions as stated, and find the Yale lectures linked rather than hidden.
- **An inerrantist:** lessons 3, 6, 9, 10. Should find that the course has not quietly conceded the critical view where it only meant to describe it, that Chicago is quoted from its own text, and that genre-first is presented as the conservative position it is.

**Language rules specific to this course:** "Old Testament" is the course's term and is introduced alongside "Hebrew Bible" and "Tanakh" in lesson 1 with a sentence on who uses which; "Apocrypha" and "deuterocanonical" are both used and attributed; "critical scholars", not "liberals" or "sceptics", except where quoting; "inerrantists" is their own word and is used without irony; "conservative" and "critical" are used as the field uses them, as positions, not as compliments or insults. No sneering at any tradition or at unbelief, and no sneering at popular misreadings either: the learner who brought Jeremiah 29:11 on a card is the person this course is for.
