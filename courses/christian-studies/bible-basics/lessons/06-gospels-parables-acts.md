> Now the tax collectors and sinners were all drawing near to hear him. And the Pharisees and the scribes grumbled, saying, "This man receives sinners and eats with them."
>
> Luke 15:1–2 (ESV)

Most people who know the story of the prodigal son have never read those two verses, and it shows. They know the story as a picture of a sinner coming home to a forgiving father, and that reading isn't wrong. But it stops two-thirds of the way through, at the party, and it never asks who Jesus was talking to. Luke tells you. He was talking to religious people who were angry that he ate with the wrong crowd. Read the story with those people in front of you and it turns out the last character to appear, the older brother who won't come in, is the one the story was built to reach.

That is the whole lesson in miniature. The Gospels are the most read part of the Bible and the part most people think they already understand, because the stories are familiar. Familiarity is the problem. This lesson gives you three things: what kind of book a Gospel is and why there are four; how a parable works, and how a common pious reading defuses it; and how to read Acts without turning every report into a rule. At the end we'll settle who wrote the Gospels and when, giving both scholarly positions in their own words, because you'll be asked.

## What kind of book a Gospel is

Start with what the writers say about their own work. John ends his Gospel like this:

> Now there are also many other things that Jesus did. Were every one of them to be written, I suppose that the world itself could not contain the books that would be written.
>
> John 21:25 (ESV)

That's a writer telling you he selected. He chose some events and left out others, and he chose for a reason (he gives it at 20:31: so that you may believe). Luke opens his Gospel by saying he consulted eyewitness accounts and wrote an orderly account for a named reader. Mark starts with the words "the beginning of the gospel". None of them claims to be a transcript.

So what are they? The best current answer is that they are ancient biographies with a theological aim.[1] Ancient biography (the Greeks called it *bios*) was a known form. It told the life of a significant person to show his character and to commend a way of living, it did not have to run in strict chronological order, and it was happy to arrange material by topic. That's the genre contract from lesson 3, and it matters, because a reader who brings the contract of a modern documentary to a *bios* will find "contradictions" that the first readers would never have noticed.

The Gospels are also, all four of them, about one thing. The first words Jesus speaks in Mark are these:

> "The time is fulfilled, and the kingdom of God is at hand; repent and believe in the gospel."
>
> Mark 1:15 (ESV)

The kingdom of God is the centre of his preaching, and it's the thread from lesson 4: the covenant God made with Israel, enforced by the prophets, is now being kept by God himself arriving as king. Nearly every parable is about that kingdom, which is why the parables can't be read as timeless moral fables. They're announcements, told to a particular crowd, that something has started.

### Four portraits, one Jesus

Why four? Fee and Stuart's answer is the one I'd give you: one story, told from four angles, each writer selecting and arranging for his own readers.[1] Matthew writes with Israel's Scriptures constantly in view. Mark is fast, spare, and ends abruptly. Luke pairs his Gospel with Acts and cares about outsiders. John is different in shape and voice, and tells you at 20:31 exactly why he wrote.

The first three are called the Synoptics (from a Greek word for "seeing together") because they share so much material, often word for word. Scholars have argued for two centuries about how they relate, and the majority view is that Mark was written first and Matthew and Luke used him.[2] You don't need the details for this course. You need to know the relationship exists, because it explains why a story appears three times with small differences, and because it's the reason the same parable can carry a slightly different emphasis in Matthew and Luke. When you read a Gospel story, the first habit to build is: which Gospel am I in, and what has this writer put around it?

### Topical arrangement is not a mistake

This is where new readers get caught. John puts the cleansing of the Temple at the start of Jesus' ministry (John 2). Matthew, Mark and Luke put it in the last week. A reader with the documentary contract sees an error and has to choose: force the two into one, or decide the Bible has a mistake in it.

The genre contract dissolves the problem. Ancient biography arranged by topic, and John has reasons of his own for opening with the Temple. The evangelical inerrantists who wrote the Chicago Statement in 1978 said so in their own document: inerrancy is not negated by "the topical arrangement of material" or by "variant selections of material in parallel accounts".[3] That is the most conservative statement of Scripture's truthfulness in modern Protestantism, and it builds the genre contract into the definition. Reading a Gospel by its genre is not a concession to critics. It's what the inerrantists say they do.

I should be honest about the edge of this. Some careful readers hold that there were two cleansings, one early and one late, and they have reasons (the accounts differ in details). Others think one event, placed differently. The genre allows both, and the choice isn't a test of faith. What the genre doesn't allow is the panic.

## How a parable works: the trap

The Old Testament has a parable that shows you the mechanism in a single scene. King David has taken Bathsheba and had her husband killed. The prophet Nathan comes to him and tells a story about a rich man with many flocks who takes a poor man's only lamb to feed a guest. David is furious; the rich man deserves to die. Then Nathan says:

> "You are the man!"
>
> 2 Samuel 12:7 (ESV)

Look at what happened. David walked into the story agreeing with it. He judged the rich man freely, because the story wasn't about him, until it was. A parable works on the hearer the way a trap works on the foot: you step in willingly and the story closes. Its power depends on the hearer not seeing themselves until the last moment.

That's why Fee and Stuart insist that a parable is not a code and not a general lesson but a story with a point of reference the first hearers recognised and an unexpected turn that caught them.[4] Most parables make one main point, with a punch aimed at somebody in the room. To read one well you ask two questions. Who was listening? And what would have caught them?

### What allegorising does

Now the pious mistake. For most of church history the parables were read as allegories: every detail stands for something, and the reader's job is to find the key. The most famous example is Augustine on the good Samaritan, and I want to give it fairly, because he was one of the greatest teachers the church has had and the reading is not stupid.

Augustine took the man going down from Jerusalem to Jericho as Adam, fallen from the heavenly city into mortality. The robbers are the devil and his angels, who strip him of immortality and leave him half dead. The priest and Levite are the Old Testament priesthood and ministry, which could not save. The Samaritan is Christ (Augustine notes that "Samaritan" can mean "guardian"). The inn is the church, and the innkeeper the apostle Paul.[5] It's beautiful. It fits the whole of Christian doctrine into one story.

And it defuses the trap. Luke tells you who Jesus was talking to. A lawyer, wanting to justify himself, had asked:

> "And who is my neighbor?"
>
> Luke 10:29 (ESV)

After the story Jesus turns the question round:

> "Which of these three, do you think, proved to be a neighbor to the man who fell among the robbers?" He said, "The one who showed him mercy." And Jesus said to him, "You go, and do likewise."
>
> Luke 10:36–37 (ESV)

The lawyer had asked who counted as his neighbour, hoping for a limit. The story makes a Samaritan, the last person a Judean lawyer would choose, the one who acts as neighbour, and then asks the lawyer which man he'd rather be. That is the punch. Notice the lawyer can't even say the word "Samaritan"; he says "the one who showed him mercy". In Augustine's reading, the lawyer vanishes, the Samaritan is Christ, and nobody in the room is caught. The story becomes a diagram of salvation instead of a question the hearer has to answer about his own contempt.

We don't follow Augustine's reading, and the reason is the basic rule from lesson 3: a text cannot mean what it could not have meant to its author or first hearers.[6] Nobody on that road could have heard "the inn is the church". But be fair to the old readers: the church survived on allegorised parables for over a thousand years, and it was usually reaching for truths it had learned elsewhere in Scripture. The cost wasn't heresy. The cost was that the parables stopped catching anyone.

One caution the other way. Jesus himself gave points of reference for the sower (Mark 4:13–20): the seed is the word, the soils are kinds of hearers. That's not a licence to allegorise every parable; it's Jesus telling his disciples what the story's own referents were. The test is whether the first hearers could have recognised the reference. Seed, soil, a landowner, a father with two sons: yes. Two testaments on a shepherd's shoulders: no.

:::checkpoint Luke 18:1–8, the widow and the unjust judge. Luke tells you the point in verse 1 (pray and don't lose heart). What is the one point of the story, and what goes wrong if you make the judge stand for God?
The one point is an argument from the lesser to the greater: if even a judge who fears neither God nor man gives a persistent widow justice to get rid of her, how much more will God, who is nothing like that judge, answer those who cry to him. If you make the judge stand for God, you have God giving in to nagging because he can't be bothered, which is the opposite of the point. The judge is in the story to be unlike God. Allegorising can turn a parable inside out.
:::

## Worked example 1: the prodigal son, read twice

Read Luke 15:11–32 now, all of it, with 15:1–2 still in mind. Then come back.

**The reading most people bring.** The younger son demands his inheritance, wastes it, comes to his senses in a pigsty, and goes home rehearsing an apology. The father runs to him, cuts the apology short, and throws a feast. The story is about repentance and a father's forgiveness, and the reader is the younger son. Some readers go further and allegorise the details: the robe is Christ's righteousness, the ring is adoption, the shoes are the freedom of a son, the fattened calf is the sacrifice. That version has been preached for centuries and most of it is true as doctrine.

**The reading the genre asks for.** Ask the two questions. Who was listening? Luke told you: Pharisees and scribes, grumbling that Jesus receives sinners and eats with them. Jesus answers with three stories (a lost sheep, a lost coin, a lost son), each ending in a party over something found. So far the grumblers can nod along; who objects to a shepherd finding his sheep?

Then the third story keeps going after the party.

:::predict The younger son is home and the feast has started. There is one character we haven't met yet. Before you reread the ending: does the story tell you whether the older brother goes in?
No. The story ends with the father outside, pleading, and the older brother's answer never comes. Luke 15:32 is the father's last word and the parable stops. That silence is the punch, and the reason it's silent is that the people it was aimed at hadn't answered yet either.
:::

Here is the ending:

> But he was angry and refused to go in. His father came out and entreated him, but he answered his father, "Look, these many years I have served you, and I never disobeyed your command, yet you never gave me a young goat, that I might celebrate with my friends. But when this son of yours came, who has devoured your property with prostitutes, you killed the fattened calf for him!" And he said to him, "Son, you are always with me, and all that is mine is yours. It was fitting to celebrate and be glad, for this your brother was dead, and is alive; he was lost, and is found."
>
> Luke 15:28–32 (ESV)

Now do the next step yourself before reading on. The Pharisees and scribes have been listening to a story about a father who welcomes a wastrel. At which line do they discover the story is about them, and what exactly is the father asking them to do?

Here's my answer. They discover it at "this son of yours". The older brother has done everything right and is standing outside a party for someone who did everything wrong, complaining that the father receives sinners and eats with them. That is Luke 15:2 in the older brother's mouth. The father doesn't rebuke him; he goes out to him, calls him "son", and asks him to come in and call the wastrel "your brother". The story stops there because the question is now the hearers': will you come in? Nathan's trap has closed, and the last word is an invitation rather than a verdict.

Notice what the allegorised version lost. The robe and the ring and the calf all sit in the first two-thirds, and a reader busy decoding them arrives at the older brother with no energy left. The story about repentance is in the text. The story about the person who resents repentance is what the text was for.

## Reading Acts: history first

Acts is narrative, and everything from lesson 3 applies: it reports what happened, not what should have happened, and God is the hero. But Acts raises a question the Old Testament stories don't, because Acts is about the church, and the reader is in the church. If the first Christians did something, must we?

Fee and Stuart's answer, which we share, is that what Acts narrates or describes does not function as a command unless Scripture elsewhere commands it, or unless it can be shown that Luke meant it as a pattern.[7] Luke was writing history with a purpose (the gospel going from Jerusalem to Rome, the Spirit driving it, nothing stopping it), and a great deal of what he records is there to tell that story, not to set up a constitution.

So the test has two parts. Is this practice commanded elsewhere in Scripture? And is it consistent across the book, or does it change from story to story? A practice that is commanded elsewhere is binding because of the command, not because Acts reports it. A practice that appears once, or appears in several incompatible forms, is being described.

## Worked example 2: goods in common, and casting lots

:::predict Acts 2:44–45 says the first believers held everything in common and sold property to give to anyone in need. Before you read on: does Luke, anywhere in Acts, command this?
He doesn't. He describes it twice, warmly, and then in Acts 5:4 Peter tells Ananias that his land was his own before he sold it and the money his own afterwards, which only makes sense if selling was voluntary. The description is real and admirable; the command isn't there.
:::

Here are the two descriptions:

> And all who believed were together and had all things in common. And they were selling their possessions and belongings and distributing the proceeds to all, as any had need.
>
> Acts 2:44–45 (ESV)

> There was not a needy person among them, for as many as were owners of lands or houses sold them and brought the proceeds of what was sold and laid it at the apostles' feet, and it was distributed to each as any had need.
>
> Acts 4:34–35 (ESV)

Now the other passage. Before Pentecost the eleven pick a replacement for Judas:

> And they cast lots for them, and the lot fell on Matthias, and he was numbered with the eleven apostles.
>
> Acts 1:26 (ESV)

Run the test on both. Is holding goods in common commanded elsewhere? Generosity to the poor is commanded everywhere, from Deuteronomy to the letters, so the principle is binding. Selling all property into a common fund is not commanded anywhere, and Peter's words in Acts 5:4 show it was voluntary in Jerusalem itself. Is it consistent across Acts? Paul's churches in the letters own property and are told to give freely and proportionately (2 Corinthians 8–9), not to pool everything. So: the generosity is a rule, the common fund is a description of one church's response.

Is casting lots commanded elsewhere? No. Is it consistent across Acts? It happens once, before the Spirit comes, and never again; from Acts 6 onward the church chooses leaders by discussion, prayer, and laying on of hands. So the lot is described, not prescribed. A reader who turns every report into a rule would build a church that pools all its property and draws its leaders from a hat, and would have to explain why Acts itself stops doing both.

**The wrinkle.** Now the hard case, and I'm not going to resolve it, because Christians haven't. In Acts 8 the Samaritans believe and are baptised, and the Spirit comes on them only later when the apostles arrive. In Acts 10 Cornelius and his household receive the Spirit while Peter is still preaching, before baptism. In Acts 19 some disciples at Ephesus are baptised and receive the Spirit when Paul lays hands on them. Pentecostal and charismatic Christians read these as showing that receiving the Spirit can be a distinct event after conversion, and they have a serious case, since Luke plainly records it happening. Most Reformed, Lutheran, Catholic and Orthodox readers hold that these were unique moments as the gospel crossed each new boundary (Samaritans, Gentiles, disciples of John), and that the letters teach the Spirit is given at conversion; they also have a serious case. The consistency test tells you Luke isn't laying down a sequence, since he gives three different ones. What he *is* showing you is argued, at full strength, on both sides. This is a real disagreement between Christians who love the same book, and a lesson on reading Acts should say so rather than pick a side and pretend the test settled it.

:::checkpoint Acts 2:42 says the first believers devoted themselves to the apostles' teaching, fellowship, the breaking of bread, and the prayers. Which of these does the test make binding, and why?
All of them, but not because Acts 2:42 reports them. Teaching, fellowship, the Lord's Supper and prayer are commanded in the Gospels and the letters (for instance 1 Corinthians 11:23–26 for the Supper, and the Great Commission for teaching), and they run consistently through the whole of Acts. The verse describes a church doing what it was told to do. That's the pattern to look for: description that matches a command elsewhere is confirmation, not the source of the obligation.
:::

## Who wrote the Gospels, and when

You'll be asked this, usually in the form "weren't they written hundreds of years later?" The honest answer has three parts: what the earliest witnesses said, what the two scholarly camps hold, and what both camps agree on. Take them in order.

### The earliest testimony

The Gospels don't name their authors in the text. The names come from the titles in the manuscripts and from early Christian writers. The earliest is Papias, a bishop in Asia Minor writing around 110–130, whose words survive because Eusebius quoted them two centuries later:

> Mark, having become the interpreter of Peter, wrote down accurately, though not in order, whatsoever he remembered of the things said or done by Christ.[8]

And on Matthew: "Matthew wrote the oracles in the Hebrew language, and every one interpreted them as he was able."[8]

Around 180 Irenaeus, bishop of Lyon, names all four: Matthew "issued a written Gospel among the Hebrews in their own dialect"; Mark, "the disciple and interpreter of Peter", wrote down what Peter preached; Luke, "the companion of Paul", recorded the Gospel Paul preached; and John, "the disciple of the Lord", published his Gospel at Ephesus.[9] By 180 the four were fixed in the mainstream church, and Irenaeus argues elsewhere that there could be neither more nor fewer, as there are four winds.[9]

History establishes that this testimony exists and when it was written. What each side makes of it is where the argument starts.

### The critical mainstream, in its own terms

Bart Ehrman, whose textbook is the set text at Yale and many other universities, puts it this way: "Critical scholars are widely agreed that the earliest Gospel was Mark, written around 70 c.e.; that Matthew and Luke were written some years later, say, around 80–85 c.e.; and that John was the last Gospel, written around 90–95 c.e."[10] On this view the Gospels circulated anonymously and the names were attached in the second century; Papias's testimony is late, second-hand, and doesn't obviously describe the Mark and Matthew we have (our Matthew is a Greek composition, not a translation from Hebrew).

The main lever for the dates is Mark 13. Jesus says of the Temple:

> "Do you see these great buildings? There will not be left here one stone upon another that will not be thrown down."
>
> Mark 13:2 (ESV)

The Romans destroyed the Temple in AD 70. Critical scholars argue that a prediction this specific, given this much weight, is most likely recorded by someone who knows it has happened, so Mark is written around or after 70, and Matthew and Luke, who used Mark, later still.[10] A thoughtful critical scholar would add that this is an argument about probability, not proof, and that the field's ranges are wide.

### The conservative case, in its own terms

:::predict Before you read the conservative dates: a critical scholar puts Mark around 70. Which decade do you think a conservative scholar puts Mark in, and how far apart are the two positions really?
Carson and Moo, the standard evangelical introduction, put Mark "sometime in the late 50s or the 60s". That's ten to fifteen years earlier than the critical date, not centuries. The gap between the two camps is a decade or so per Gospel, and both are inside the first century.
:::

Carson and Moo's *Introduction to the New Testament* is the reference point for the conservative position. Their dates: Mark "sometime in the late 50s or the 60s"; Acts in the "mid-60s"; Luke in the "mid or late 60s"; Matthew "not long before 70"; John "tentatively 80–85".[11] (One flag for honesty: I've taken those ranges from a published transcription of their table, not from the book itself, and the fact-check should confirm them against the pages.)

Their arguments run like this. Acts ends with Paul alive and under house arrest in Rome, and never mentions his death (around 62), the persecution under Nero (64), or the fall of Jerusalem (70), which is strange if Luke was writing after all three; so Acts is early, Luke is earlier than Acts, and Mark is earlier than Luke. The names are early and unanimous: no manuscript, no early writer, ever attributes a Gospel to anyone else, and Mark and Luke are odd choices to invent, since neither was an apostle. As for Mark 13, the conservative reading is that it's prophecy; Jesus predicted the Temple's fall, and the Gospels never say "as has now happened", which they easily could have. On this view the critical argument assumes prediction is impossible, which is a philosophical premise, not a historical finding.

One more witness, because it matters that he's not a conservative. John A. T. Robinson, an English bishop on the liberal wing of his church, argued in *Redating the New Testament* (1976) that the entire New Testament was written before 70, on the ground that no book treats the Temple's destruction as past.[12] Most scholars didn't follow him, but his book is proof that the early dates aren't only held by people with a doctrinal reason to hold them.

### What both sides agree on

This is the ground under both camps, and it is the answer to the person at work.

- Every Gospel was written within about seventy years of the crucifixion, on either dating.[13]
- Paul's undisputed letters were written in the 50s, within about twenty-five years of the crucifixion, and are earlier than any Gospel.[13]
- Nothing in the New Testament was composed in the fourth century, or the third, or the second. "Hundreds of years later" is nobody's position.[13]

Scholars argue about the decade and about whether the traditional authors wrote them. The course holds the conservative view, and I'll say plainly that its case for the Gospels' reliability doesn't depend on winning the dating argument. A Gospel written in 80 by a second-generation Christian drawing on eyewitness tradition is still a first-century source about a first-century man, and the rest of that case belongs to *The Case for Christianity*, not here.

## What people get wrong

**"The Gospels were written hundreds of years after Jesus."** You've now seen both scholarly positions, and the latest date anyone serious gives for any Gospel is the mid-90s. The four-century claim usually comes from confusing the date of composition with the date of our oldest complete manuscripts (fourth century, which lesson 9 will show you), or from the Nicaea story, which lesson 8 takes apart.

**"The parables are allegories with a key."** They're stories with a point and a target. When you find yourself assigning a meaning to the robe, stop and ask who was listening. The test for a detail is whether the first hearers could have recognised the reference without a key.

**"If the early church did it, we must."** Acts describes; the letters and the Gospels command. Run the test. And its mirror: **"Acts is only history, so it can't teach anything."** Luke wrote history with a purpose, and the purpose (the gospel going out, the Spirit driving it) is a lesson in itself; what Acts can't do on its own is turn a report into a rule.

**"The differences between the Gospels are errors."** Most are what ancient biography did on purpose: topical arrangement, compression, selection, paraphrase. The Chicago Statement lists these as compatible with inerrancy.[3] And its mirror: **"Every difference is easily harmonised."** Some aren't. The day of the Last Supper in John against the Synoptics, and the details of the resurrection morning, have exercised careful readers for eighteen centuries. We hold by faith that the accounts are true; we don't pretend every seam is invisible, and the strained harmonisations that pretend it (the cock crowed six times, there were two blind men at two Jerichos) do the text no favours.

**"The lost gospels were suppressed."** There are other ancient texts called gospels: Thomas, Peter, Judas, Mary, and more. They're real, they're worth knowing about, and you can read them. They are also, on every scholarly dating, later than the four (Thomas is the earliest candidate, and most scholars put it in the second century), they were never in wide use across the churches, and Irenaeus's fourfold Gospel predates most of them.[9] They weren't hidden; they were known, discussed, and not received, for reasons (date, content, use) that lesson 8 goes into. If you want the strongest case for taking Thomas seriously as a source, Dale Martin's Yale lecture on it is free and fair.[14]
