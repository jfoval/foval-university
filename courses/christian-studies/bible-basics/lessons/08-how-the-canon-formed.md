> "And count the patience of our Lord as salvation, just as our beloved brother Paul also wrote to you according to the wisdom given him, as he does in all his letters when he speaks in them of these matters. There are some things in them that are hard to understand, which the ignorant and unstable twist to their own destruction, as they do the other Scriptures." (2 Peter 3:15–16, ESV)

Read that slowly. A New Testament writer is talking about Paul's letters, admits they're hard, and then ranks them with "the other Scriptures". Inside the New Testament itself, one set of Christian writings is already being treated as Scripture by another. Nobody voted. Nobody convened. A letter was read in a church, kept, copied, and read again, and at some point people were calling it Scripture and arguing about what it meant.

That's the whole story of the canon in miniature, and it's the story this lesson tells with dates attached. You'll need it, because you're going to meet two confident claims that both get it wrong: that the Emperor Constantine or the Council of Nicaea picked the books in 325, and that the Catholic Church "added" seven books to the Old Testament. By the end you'll be able to walk the actual evidence in order, say why Catholic and Orthodox Bibles are bigger than the one this course uses, give each side's case in words its own people would accept, and tell which parts of all this are history, which are argued, and which are faith.

## What a canon is, and how one actually forms

"Canon" comes from a Greek word for a measuring rod, and it came to mean the list of books a community treats as its rule. Lesson 1 gave you the counts: sixty-six books in a Protestant Bible, seventy-three in a Catholic one, more in the Orthodox churches. This lesson is about where those lists came from.

Here's the mechanism, and everything else hangs off it. A canon is not made by a vote. It's the shape a community's worship has already taken, written down when someone asks. Churches read certain books aloud on Sunday, copied them, taught from them, and defended them when challenged. When a bishop finally wrote a list, he was describing what his churches did, the way a librarian's catalogue describes shelves that were already full.

That predicts what the evidence should look like, and you can check the prediction as we go. If canons formed by decree, you'd expect one document, one date, and silence before it. If they formed by use, you'd expect local lists that mostly agree, a handful of books argued over for a long time, and councils that confirm rather than choose. Keep that prediction in your head. It's the test for everything below.

## The Jewish canon by the first century

Start with the Old Testament, because the first Christians inherited it rather than made it. Three witnesses, in order.

Around 132 BC a Jewish man translating his grandfather's book of wisdom in Egypt wrote a preface to it. It survives as the Prologue to Sirach, and three times it refers to a collection in three parts. In the NRSVue's wording: "the Law and the Prophets and the others that followed them", and later "the Law itself, the Prophets, and the rest of the books".[1] Two parts have names. The third is still just "the rest of the books". That is the earliest surviving description of the collection, and it's already three-part, with the third part's edges not yet named.

:::predict Around AD 95 the Jewish historian Josephus told a Greek audience how many books the Jews held sacred. Before you read on, guess the number. Then guess what a rabbi a century later would have said.
Josephus says twenty-two. The later rabbinic count is twenty-four. Same books; different arithmetic. Twenty-two comes from counting Ruth with Judges and Lamentations with Jeremiah, which brings the total to the number of letters in the Hebrew alphabet.
:::

Josephus, writing *Against Apion* around AD 95, says the Jews have "only twenty two books: which contain the records of all the past times: which are justly believed to be divine", and adds that through all those ages "no one has been so bold, as either to add any thing to them; to take any thing from them; or to make any change in them."[2] You should read that as a proud statement from an insider, not a neutral survey. But it tells you the collection was closed enough by the 90s for a Jew to boast about its fixed size.

The third witness is Jesus. In Luke 24:44 he tells the disciples that "everything written about me in the Law of Moses and the Prophets and the Psalms must be fulfilled" (ESV).[3] Law, Prophets, and a third part named by its biggest book: the same three-part shape the Sirach prologue described, now spoken in Jerusalem around AD 30. Lesson 1 used this verse for the layout. Here it does a second job: it's the earliest Christian witness to the Jewish three-part canon.

One more, from inside Judaism. A passage in the Babylonian Talmud, *Bava Batra* 14b–15a, records a tradition giving the order of the Prophets and the Writings.[4] What's striking is what the rabbis were arguing about: not which books belonged, but which order to copy them in. Membership was assumed.

Say this in the terms a Jewish reader uses, because it's their Scripture first. The Tanakh has twenty-four books in three parts: Torah, Nevi'im, Ketuvim. It's a complete Bible, read on its own terms in synagogues today, and it ends with Chronicles and the call to go up to Jerusalem, not with Malachi looking forward. The Christian Old Testament contains those same books, rearranged into four sections. The point of this section is that the list was Jewish and settled before Christians did anything with it. What Christians did next is where the disagreement starts, and we'll come to it.

:::checkpoint Josephus says twenty-two, the rabbis say twenty-four, and Athanasius (whom you'll meet below) says twenty-two for the Old Testament too. Are these three different canons?
No. Josephus and the rabbis count the same books; twenty-two joins Ruth to Judges and Lamentations to Jeremiah. Athanasius's twenty-two is a different matter: he reaches the number partly by leaving out Esther and counting Baruch with Jeremiah, so his list is not identical to either Jewish count. The number can be the same while the contents shift, which is why you always check what a list contains, never just its length.
:::

## The New Testament core by about 180

Now the new books. The evidence for the Gospels and Paul's letters comes fast and early, and it comes in an interesting form: someone trying to cut the collection down.

Around 144, in Rome, a shipowner's son named Marcion produced a Bible of his own: an edited Luke and ten letters of Paul, with the whole Old Testament rejected.[5] The church threw him out. What matters for us is what his edit proves. You can't cut down a collection that doesn't exist. Marcion is negative evidence that by the 140s the churches were already reading a wider set of Gospels and letters, wide enough that reducing it to Luke and Paul was a scandal.

Around 180, Irenaeus, bishop of Lyon, wrote *Against Heresies*. In book three he names the four Gospels with their traditional authors and then argues that there can be neither more nor fewer than four. His reasoning is that "since there are four zones of the world in which we live, and four principal winds", the church spread across the world should have four pillars.[6] You may find the argument odd. That's the point. Nobody argues from the four winds for a number that's still in question. Irenaeus is explaining a settled fact, not deciding one. By 180 the fourfold Gospel was fixed in the mainstream churches, a century and a half before Nicaea.

:::figure https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/1/1b/Muratorian_Fragment.jpg | A page of the Muratorian Fragment, a Latin manuscript with faded brown ink on parchment, listing the books of the New Testament
The Muratorian Fragment, Biblioteca Ambrosiana, Milan (Cod. J 101 sup.). The Latin copy is seventh or eighth century; the list it copies is much older, and how much older is argued below. Photograph: public domain, via Wikimedia Commons.
:::

The third witness is the one in the picture, and it comes with an honest caveat. The Muratorian Fragment is a damaged Latin list found in a codex in the Ambrosian Library in Milan and published by Ludovico Antonio Muratori in 1740.[7] Its opening is lost, so it begins mid-sentence; the first words we have call Luke "the third book of the Gospel", which tells you two came before it.[7] Then it lists John, Acts, thirteen letters of Paul, Jude, two letters of John, the Wisdom of Solomon (yes, an Old Testament book, in a New Testament list), and the Apocalypse of John. It mentions the Apocalypse of Peter but says "some of us are not willing that the latter be read in church."[7] And it deals with the Shepherd of Hermas, a hugely popular book, like this: it "ought indeed to be read", but not publicly in church among the prophets or apostles, because Hermas wrote it "very recently, in our times", while his brother Pius was bishop of Rome.[7]

What it doesn't mention is as telling as what it does: no Hebrews, no James, no 1 or 2 Peter, and no clear third letter of John.[7]

Now the caveat. The traditional date is late second century, at Rome, mostly because of that "in our times" line about Pius, who was bishop in the middle of the second century. In 1973 Albert Sundberg argued the list was really fourth-century and Eastern, and Geoffrey Hahneman built the case out in 1992. Joseph Verheyden reviewed their arguments in 2003 and found none of them convincing; Eckhard Schnabel's 2014 survey concluded that the traditional date still holds and that the Fragment reflects one local church's practice rather than a universal decision.[8] Scholars argue about this, and I'd teach it as "probably about 180 to 200, disputed". Either way, notice what it shows: a core of roughly twenty-two books recognised early, and a fringe still under discussion, with the writer giving reasons. That's the shape the mechanism predicted.

## The edges settle: 200 to 400

Between Irenaeus and the end of the fourth century, the argument was about the edges: Hebrews, James, 2 Peter, 2 and 3 John, Jude, and Revelation. Three documents let you watch it settle.

:::predict Eusebius of Caesarea finished his *Ecclesiastical History* around 325, the year of Nicaea, and he attended the council. In it he sorts the Christian books into recognised, disputed, and rejected. Before reading on, write down which books you think he lists as disputed.
He lists James, Jude, 2 Peter, and 2 and 3 John as disputed "which are nevertheless recognized by many". Revelation he can't decide where to put and says so. If you guessed any of the Gospels, notice that: the Gospels are not in question for anyone in the mainstream church by 325.
:::

Eusebius's *History* 3.25 puts the four Gospels, Acts, Paul's letters, 1 John, and 1 Peter among the recognised books. Among the disputed, "which are nevertheless recognized by many", he lists James, Jude, 2 Peter, and 2 and 3 John. Among the rejected he names the Acts of Paul, the Shepherd, the Apocalypse of Peter, the Epistle of Barnabas, and the Teachings of the Apostles. And of Revelation he writes that it should be placed "if it really seem proper", promising to give the different opinions later.[9] This is the best single snapshot of the state of the question on the eve of Nicaea. Hold on to it, because it's also the evidence that kills the Nicaea story, and we'll use it that way in a moment.

:::predict What year is the first surviving document that lists exactly the twenty-seven books in your New Testament, no more and no fewer? Guess before you look.
367. It's a letter from Athanasius, bishop of Alexandria, sent to his churches before Easter. Most people guess earlier if they assume a council did it, and later if they've heard the medieval church "made" the Bible. Neither is right.
:::

Every year the bishop of Alexandria sent a letter announcing the date of Easter. Athanasius's thirty-ninth, for Easter 367, took the chance to list the books. For the Old Testament he gives "twenty-two books in number" (and we'll come back to what's in that list, because it's a trap for both sides). For the New Testament: "the four Gospels, according to Matthew, Mark, Luke, and John", then Acts, seven Catholic Epistles, "fourteen Epistles of Paul" (which includes Hebrews), and "the Revelation of John". Then the famous sentences: "These are fountains of salvation, that they who thirst may be satisfied with the living words they contain." And: "Let no man add to these, neither let him take ought from these."[10]

He isn't finished. He adds that there are "other books besides these not indeed included in the Canon, but appointed by the Fathers to be read by those who newly join us": the Wisdom of Solomon, Sirach, Esther, Judith, Tobit, the Teaching of the Apostles, and the Shepherd.[10]

Two things a lesson must not get wrong about this letter. First, Athanasius does not create the list. Read his words: he's reporting what the churches hold and telling them not to change it. The tone is a fence around a field that's already planted. Second, look at that second list. Esther is in it, not in his canon. So the first document with our exact New Testament does not have our exact Old Testament. Keep that for later.

Then the councils. In 393 a regional council at Hippo in North Africa, with Augustine present, listed a canon; the Third Council of Carthage repeated it in 397, and the African canons were compiled again in 419.[11] The Old Testament list included Tobit, Judith, two books of Maccabees, and Wisdom and Sirach among the five books of Solomon, matching the later Catholic canon. Note what the council then did. It asked that "the church across the sea", meaning Rome, be consulted to confirm the list.[11] A body that thought it was choosing the books would not have asked someone else to check its work. A body that thought it was confirming common practice would.

## Worked example 1: "Constantine chose the books at Nicaea"

You'll hear this from friends, in documentaries, and on the internet. Most people got it from Dan Brown's *The Da Vinci Code* (2003), where a character declares that the Bible "was collated by the pagan Roman Emperor Constantine the Great".[12] Let's walk the dated evidence in order and see whether there's room for it.

Around 180, Irenaeus has four Gospels and argues there can be no other number.[6] That's 145 years before the council. If Constantine picked the Gospels, he picked ones that had already been the only four for a century and a half.

Around 325, Eusebius, who sat at Nicaea, writes his own history and sorts the books. Which ones does he say are disputed? James, Jude, 2 Peter, 2 and 3 John, and he hedges on Revelation.[9] A man who had just watched an emperor settle the canon would not list the open questions in his own book, and the open questions were never about the Gospels.

In 367, Athanasius, forty-two years after Nicaea, lists our twenty-seven. He was at Nicaea too, as a young deacon. If the council had listed the books, the most obvious thing for him to write would be "as the great council decreed". He writes nothing of the kind. He tells the churches not to add or subtract, as if the list is something they already have.[10]

In 393 and 397, Hippo and Carthage list the books, nearly seventy years after Nicaea, and ask Rome to confirm.[11] If Nicaea had settled it, there'd be nothing to confirm.

And Nicaea itself? We have its creed and its twenty canons, and none of the surviving records of its business mention the canon of Scripture at all.[5] The council was about whether the Son is of the same substance as the Father, and about the date of Easter and some church discipline.

So the Nicaea story fails on the evidence. But notice something better than that. It fails as the wrong *kind* of story. Once you've seen the mechanism, "an emperor chose the books" is like saying a librarian decided which books people had been reading for two hundred years. It gets the direction of causation backwards. The lists exist because the reading came first.

Now sort the claims. *History establishes* that the four Gospels were fixed by about 180, that Nicaea's records contain no canon business, and that the fourth-century lists confirmed a collection already in use. *Scholars argue about* the date of the Muratorian Fragment and how early the fringe books were widely received. *We hold by faith* that these books, and not others, are God's Word. The Nicaea story isn't wrong on the faith question; it's wrong on the history, which is the one part anyone can check.

## The tests the church used, and what scholars say was going on

Nobody in the second century wrote out a checklist. But when you read the reasons given in the Muratorian Fragment, Eusebius, and Athanasius, three tests keep recurring, and F. F. Bruce's standard history of the canon draws them out: apostolic origin (written by an apostle or by someone in the apostolic circle, which is why the Shepherd failed), orthodoxy (agreement with the faith taught everywhere, which is why Marcion's Gospel failed), and continuous use in worship across the churches (which is why the short letters took so long to be settled: they were short, and not every church had them).[5]

Scholars agree on those tests and on the dates. What they argue about is what the process *was*. Two live positions, each in its own terms.

Bruce, and more recently Michael Kruger in *Canon Revisited* (2012), hold that the church *recognised* a canon it did not create. Kruger's version is called the self-authenticating model: the books carry their own authority as God's word, God's providence guided their preservation and reception, and the Holy Spirit brings believers to recognise them, with the historical evidence of early and wide use serving as corroboration of that recognition rather than its basis.[13] On this view a canonical book was canonical the day it was written, and the fourth-century lists are the church catching up with a fact.

Lee Martin McDonald, in *The Biblical Canon* (2007) and his two-volume *Formation of the Biblical Canon* (2017), holds that the canon was *community-determined*: no early authority fixed a list, recognition was slow and local, different regions read different collections for centuries, and the fourth-century lists represent choices made by churches under the pressures of their time.[14] On this view "canon" in the strict sense of a closed list is a fourth-century thing, and the earlier evidence shows Scripture (books treated as authoritative) without yet showing canon (a fixed boundary).

Both models read the same evidence. Both reject the Nicaea story. Both see the core-and-fringe pattern. The disagreement is over whether the process discovered authority or conferred it, and that question is not one that a new manuscript could settle, because it's a question about what kind of thing a canon is.

This course holds Kruger's kind of view, and I'll say so plainly and once: we believe the church received these books because they were God's word, not that they became God's word by being received. That's a faith commitment stated as one. A critical scholar reading McDonald can look at every date on the timeline above and agree with all of them. Dale Martin's second Yale lecture, linked below, tells the story from that side, and you should hear it from him.

## Why Catholic and Orthodox Bibles have more books

Now the Old Testament's edges, and the question a Catholic or Orthodox friend will raise.

By the first century most Jews outside Palestine read their Scriptures in Greek, in the translation called the Septuagint. The Greek manuscripts that survive from the Christian centuries carry, alongside the books of the Hebrew Bible, a set of further books written mostly in the last centuries before Christ: Tobit, Judith, the Wisdom of Solomon, Sirach (also called Ecclesiasticus), Baruch with the Letter of Jeremiah, 1 and 2 Maccabees, and additions to Esther and Daniel. The early church's Bible was overwhelmingly the Greek one, so these books came with it.

Catholics call these books *deuterocanonical*, "second canon", meaning a second tier of recognition, not a lesser authority. Protestants call them *the Apocrypha*, "hidden things", a word Jerome used. Each word is that tradition's own and this lesson uses each for the tradition that uses it.

Around 391, Jerome, translating the Old Testament afresh from Hebrew, wrote a preface that came to be called the *Prologus Galeatus*, the "helmeted prologue". In it he counts the twenty-two Hebrew books and says that Wisdom, Sirach, Judith, Tobit, and the Shepherd "are not in the canon."[15] In his preface to the books of Solomon he adds that as the church reads Judith, Tobit, and Maccabees "but does not admit them among the canonical Scriptures", so it should read Wisdom and Sirach "for the edification of the people, not to give authority to doctrines of the Church."[15] His Vulgate, finished around 405, kept the books anyway. So the medieval West inherited both: a Bible with the books in it, and its greatest translator's note saying they were a different kind of book. That is why the question was still open a thousand years later.

Martin Luther's 1534 German Bible printed them between the testaments under a heading, in the Gruber Collection's rendering, that they are books "not held equal to the Holy Scriptures, but are useful and good to read".[16] He kept them and moved them. So did the King James Bible of 1611, which placed the Apocrypha between the testaments as English Bibles before it had.[17] The British and Foreign Bible Society stopped funding Bibles containing the Apocrypha in 1826, and whether that decision was theological, financial, or both is itself debated; don't let anyone tell you one motive as fact.[17] That, not Luther, is why the Protestant Bible on your shelf has no Apocrypha at all.

The Catholic Church answered Luther at the Council of Trent. On 8 April 1546, the fourth session issued a decree listing the Old Testament books, "Tobias, Judith, Esther, Job", "Wisdom, Ecclesiasticus", "Jeremias, with Baruch", and "two books of the Machabees, the first and the second" among them, and declared that whoever does not receive "the said books entire with all their parts, as they have been used to be read in the Catholic Church, and as they are contained in the old Latin vulgate edition", "let him be anathema".[18] That gives the Catholic Bible its forty-six Old Testament books and seventy-three in total.

The Orthodox churches are not a bigger version of Catholicism, and an Orthodox reader would want you to know it. They received the Greek Old Testament directly, and their Bibles carry books the Catholic canon does not: 1 Esdras, 3 Maccabees, the Prayer of Manasseh, and Psalm 151, with 4 Maccabees often in an appendix.[19] The Greek term for the extra books is *anagignoskomena*, "things that are read". The Synod of Jerusalem in 1672, answering a Calvinist-leaning confession, called Wisdom, Judith, Tobit, the additions to Daniel, Maccabees, and Sirach "genuine parts of Scripture".[20] But it was a regional synod, and in Orthodoxy a regional synod binds where it is received; there is no Orthodox equivalent of Trent's single defining decree. Timothy Ware's standard introduction notes that many Orthodox scholars, following Athanasius and Jerome, hold the books to be part of the Bible while standing on a lower footing than the rest of the Old Testament, though not all agree.[21] The Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo Church has the largest canon of all, eighty-one books, including Enoch and Jubilees.[19]

## Worked example 2: what a New Testament quotation proves

Here's the hard case, and I want you to do part of it yourself.

Hebrews 11 is the roll-call of faith. Verse 35 says: "Women received back their dead by resurrection. Some were tortured, refusing to accept release, so that they might rise again to a better life" (ESV).[22] Now open 2 Maccabees 7, the story of seven brothers tortured to death under Antiochus for refusing to eat pork. The second brother tells the king, in the NRSVue, that "the King of the universe will raise us up to a renewal of everlasting life, because we have died for his laws."[23] Nothing in the Hebrew Bible matches Hebrews 11:35 as closely. The writer of Hebrews, listing heroes of faith, seems to be reaching for a story from a book Protestant Bibles don't contain.

A Catholic reader makes this case, in their own terms: the apostolic church's Bible was the Greek Septuagint, with these books in it; the New Testament alludes to them, as here; Hippo and Carthage listed them; the church read them as Scripture for well over a thousand years before Trent; Trent did not add anything, it defined what was already practised; and it is the Protestant list, drawn up in the sixteenth century on Jerome's minority view, that is the innovation.

A Protestant reader makes this case, in their own terms: the canon Jesus and the apostles used was the Hebrew one, and Luke 24:44 describes it in its three parts; Josephus counted twenty-two; the New Testament never cites a deuterocanonical book *as Scripture* with a formula like "it is written", though it alludes to many books it does not treat as Scripture; Jerome, who knew the Hebrew, distinguished them; and Athanasius's list has them among books "to be read" but outside the canon.

Now do the next step before I do. Jude 14–15 says: "It was also about these that Enoch, the seventh from Adam, prophesied, saying, 'Behold, the Lord comes with ten thousands of his holy ones, to execute judgment on all and to convict all the ungodly of all their deeds of ungodliness that they have committed in such an ungodly way, and of all the harsh things that ungodly sinners have spoken against him'" (ESV).[24] That is a quotation, and it comes from 1 Enoch, a book neither the Catholic nor the Protestant canon contains. Using the two cases you've just read, what does Jude do to the argument from allusion?

Here's mine. Jude shows that a New Testament writer can quote a book approvingly, even call its words a prophecy, without the church on either side of the Reformation concluding the book is Scripture. So a Protestant will say Hebrews 11:35 is an allusion of the same kind, and that allusion proves familiarity, not canonicity. A Catholic will reply that the argument from allusion was never their main argument; the main argument is the church's continuous use, which 2 Maccabees has and 1 Enoch (outside Ethiopia) does not. And an Ethiopian Orthodox reader will point out that their canon does contain 1 Enoch, which is a reminder that "what the church has always read" has a different answer in different places.

Then there's Athanasius, whom both sides reach for. His Old Testament is twenty-two books; Wisdom, Sirach, Judith, and Tobit are outside the canon, "to be read" by new converts. That helps the Protestant. But his twenty-two leave out Esther, which every Protestant Bible has, and count Baruch and the Letter of Jeremiah inside Jeremiah, which no Protestant Bible does.[10] Neither modern list matches his. He's a witness that the question was still being settled, not a referee who settled it.

So sort it. *History establishes* what Josephus, Athanasius, Jerome, Luther, and Trent said; both sides agree on every quotation above. *Scholars argue about* how much weight each witness should carry, and about whether the Greek-speaking Jews of Alexandria ever had a wider canon of their own; most scholars now doubt that an "Alexandrian canon" existed, which takes one old argument away from the Catholic side and leaves the argument from church use, which was always the stronger one.[5] *What is held by faith*, and by belonging to a church, is which list is Scripture.

This course uses the sixty-six books, for the Protestant reasons above, and we say so as our position. Two things belong alongside that. First, no Christian doctrine turns on the difference except one: the prayers for the dead in 2 Maccabees 12, where Judas Maccabeus collects money for a sin offering on behalf of fallen soldiers, and the narrator comments that it would have been "superfluous and foolish to pray for the dead" if he were not expecting them to rise; Catholic teaching cites this for purgatory.[23] Every other doctrine that Catholics and Protestants share or dispute is argued from books in both Bibles. Second, the deuterocanonical books are worth reading whatever you hold. Wisdom and Sirach were read by the apostles' generation; 1 Maccabees is the history behind Hanukkah and the world Jesus was born into; and you can't read Hebrews 11 as its first readers did without 2 Maccabees 7 in your head.

## What people get wrong

**"Nicaea, or Constantine, chose the books."** Walked above. Irenaeus 180, Eusebius listing the disputes in 325, Athanasius 367, Hippo and Carthage in the 390s, and no canon business in Nicaea's records.[5][6][9][10][11]

**"The lost gospels were suppressed."** Lesson 6 introduced Thomas, Peter, and Judas as second- and third-century texts. Now you have the criteria. They failed on date (Irenaeus already had four Gospels when most of them were written), on origin (nobody in the apostolic circle wrote them), and on use (no mainstream church read them in worship). Eusebius names the rejected books in the open, in a book he expected everyone to read.[9] Suppressed texts don't get catalogued by the suppressors.

**"Rome added the Apocrypha at Trent."** They were in the Septuagint, the Vulgate, and every Western Bible for a millennium. Trent defined; Luther set apart; both were reacting to Jerome.[15][16][18]

**"Athanasius invented the list."** He reports it and fences it. Read his sentence again: "Let no man add to these, neither let him take ought from these."[10] That's the voice of someone guarding something, not inventing it.

**"The Protestant canon is the original one."** Say that to a Catholic and you'll hear, with reason, that the Protestant Old Testament is the sixteenth-century list and theirs is the one the church read for a thousand years. Say the reverse to a Protestant and you'll hear that the apostles' Bible was the Hebrew one. Both are stating their case; neither is stating a fact the other has missed. Give the claim as each side puts it.

**"The Muratorian Fragment proves the canon was closed by 200."** It shows a core that was settled and a fringe that wasn't, and its own date is argued. Don't overstate your best evidence; the other side will notice.

:::callout Apocrypha and deuterocanonical
Both words are right, in the mouths of the people who use them. "Apocrypha" is the Protestant and Jerome's term; "deuterocanonical" is the Catholic term, coined in the sixteenth century to say "canonical, recognised second". If you're describing the Catholic view, use their word. If you're describing the Protestant view, use theirs. Using your own side's word for the other side's position is the quickest way to fail the test of describing a view so its holders recognise it.
:::
