Then he said to them, "These are my words that I spoke to you while I was still with you, that everything written about me in the Law of Moses and the Prophets and the Psalms must be fulfilled." (Luke 24:44, ESV)[1]

Most people read that sentence and hear one thing: Jesus says the whole Bible is about him. That is in it. But look at how he names the whole Bible. He doesn't say "the Scriptures" and leave it there. He gives three parts: the Law of Moses, the Prophets, and the Psalms. In the first century, that is how a Jew described the collection, in three shelves, and the third shelf was named by its biggest book. So the person this whole book is about, on the evening of his resurrection, is describing the Bible's layout.

That is what this lesson is for. Before you can read a book well, you have to be able to find it, and you have to know what kind of thing you have found. A Bible is not one book. It is a library of sixty-six (or seventy-three, or more, and we'll get to that) bound as one, arranged by shelf rather than by date, with numbers in the margins that nobody who wrote it ever saw. Once you can see the shelves, you can predict what kind of book you are holding before you read a word, and that prediction is the first thing that goes wrong for new readers.

## First, where this course stands

This is a School of Christian Studies course, and the label on it tells you so. We believe the Bible is God's Word: that in these books God speaks, and speaks truly, about himself and about us. That is a faith claim, and we hold it by faith. We will not disguise it as a neutral fact, and we will not pretend it is the only view a thoughtful person can hold.

So the course will keep three kinds of claim apart, in its own words, every time they come up. *History establishes* something when the documents and the digs settle it and scholars of every conviction agree. *Scholars argue about* something when serious, informed people disagree and the evidence does not yet settle it; there you will get each side in its own terms. *We hold by faith* something when it is ours as Christians and evidence bears on it without deciding it. You will see those three phrases so often that you'll start sorting claims yourself, which is the point.

One more thing about words. This course says "Old Testament", because that is the Christian name for the first and larger part of the Bible. Jews call the same books the Tanakh, or the Hebrew Bible, and for them it is not an "old" anything; it is Scripture, read on its own terms, in its own order, in synagogues this week. When the course speaks of how Jews read it, it will use their words. When a scholar reading it without any faith commitment is the subject, the course will say "critical scholar", because that is the field's own term.

## How many books?

:::predict Without looking, how many books are in the Bible on your table? Write a number down.
Most people guess wrong in both directions: some say "about thirty", some say "over a hundred". A Protestant Bible has sixty-six: thirty-nine in the Old Testament and twenty-seven in the New. If your Bible is Catholic, it has seventy-three, because it counts seven more books and some longer forms of Esther and Daniel in the Old Testament. If it is Orthodox, it has more still. Same New Testament in all three.
:::

Sixty-six is the count this course uses, and it says so now so that nothing is hidden. The Catholic count is forty-six Old Testament books plus twenty-seven, fixed at the Council of Trent in 1546.[10] Orthodox Bibles carry the Catholic books and a few more, such as 1 Esdras, 3 Maccabees, and Psalm 151.[11] The extra books are called deuterocanonical by Catholics and Orthodox, which is their word and means "second canon"; Protestants traditionally call them the Apocrypha. Each name belongs to the tradition that uses it.

Why the difference exists, and what each tradition says for itself, is lesson 8. It deserves a whole sitting, because it is a real disagreement with serious people on each side. For now, three things. History establishes what each of these Bibles contains. Which list is Scripture is a question the traditions answer by faith and by the authority of their churches. And no central Christian doctrine turns on the difference, which is why Catholics and Protestants have argued about it for five centuries without either side thinking the other has lost the gospel.

## The shelves of the Old Testament

Here is the layout of the Old Testament in a Christian Bible. Say the four groups out loud once; you will use them every time you open the book.

1. **Law**, five books: Genesis, Exodus, Leviticus, Numbers, Deuteronomy. The founding story from creation to the edge of the promised land, and the covenant made at Sinai.
2. **History**, twelve books: Joshua to Esther. Israel in the land, the kings, the fall of both kingdoms, exile, and return.
3. **Poetry and Wisdom**, five books: Job, Psalms, Proverbs, Ecclesiastes, Song of Songs. Prayers, songs, and reflection on how to live.
4. **Prophets**, seventeen books: Isaiah to Malachi. Five long ones (the "Major" Prophets, so called for length, not rank), then twelve short ones.

Five, twelve, five, seventeen: thirty-nine.

Christine Hayes, who teaches Yale's introductory Old Testament course, points out that this order has a logic, and it isn't the order of the Hebrew Bible. It comes from the Septuagint, the Greek translation made for Greek-speaking Jews from about the third century BC, which became the Old Testament of the early Greek-speaking church. In her words, "The Septuagint's rationale for ordering the books is temporal. They've clustered books Genesis through Esther, which tell of things past; the books of Job through the Song of Songs contain wisdom that applies to the present; and then the prophetic books, Isaiah to Malachi, contain or tell of things future."[2] Past, present, future. That is a reader's arrangement, and it ends with a book looking forward.

## The same books, another way

Now the Hebrew Bible, the Tanakh, which is what Jesus was describing in Luke 24. Tanakh is an acronym: **T**orah, **N**evi'im, **K**etuvim. Law, Prophets, Writings.

- **Torah**, five books. The same five.
- **Nevi'im**, the Prophets, eight books: Joshua, Judges, Samuel, Kings (the "Former Prophets", which a Christian Bible calls History), then Isaiah, Jeremiah, Ezekiel, and the Twelve counted as one book (the "Latter Prophets").
- **Ketuvim**, the Writings, eleven books: Psalms, Proverbs, Job, the five short scrolls read at festivals (Song of Songs, Ruth, Lamentations, Ecclesiastes, Esther), Daniel, Ezra-Nehemiah, and Chronicles.

Five, eight, eleven: twenty-four. Same books. Samuel, Kings, and Chronicles are each one book instead of two; the twelve short prophets are one; Ezra and Nehemiah are one. Count them the Christian way and you get thirty-nine. Josephus, writing around AD 95, counted twenty-two, by folding Ruth into Judges and Lamentations into Jeremiah: "only twenty two books: which contain the records of all the past times: which are justly believed to be divine."[3] Twenty-two, twenty-four, thirty-nine: one library, three ways of counting the shelves.

:::predict The Christian Old Testament ends with Malachi. What book do you think ends the Tanakh?
Chronicles. The Babylonian Talmud, in a passage that lists the order of the Prophets and the Writings, ends the Writings with "Ezra and Chronicles",[4] and printed Hebrew Bibles today follow that. So a Jewish reader closes the Bible on Cyrus's decree to go up and build the Temple in Jerusalem, and a Christian reader closes the Old Testament on Malachi's promise that Elijah is coming. We'll read both endings in the practice. Each sets up a different "what happens next".
:::

Two things from that Talmud passage are worth noticing, because they show what was being settled in those early centuries. First, its list of the Prophets runs "Jeremiah and Ezekiel, and Isaiah and the Twelve",[4] which is not the order in any Bible you can buy. Printed Hebrew Bibles put Isaiah first. So the question the rabbis were settling was the order of the shelf, not which books were on it; membership was already fixed. Second, Daniel sits in the Writings, not among the Prophets.[4] A Christian Bible, following the Septuagint, puts Daniel right after Ezekiel. Scholars argue about why the Tanakh shelves him with the Writings (the book's late final form, its Aramaic chapters, and its court-tale style are the usual suggestions), and the two placements do read him a little differently: a Christian reader meets Daniel as a prophet of things to come, a Jewish reader as a wise man in exile whose visions come at the end. Same book, different shelf, different first expectation.

## The New Testament's four shelves

The New Testament is shorter and simpler to map.

1. **Gospels**, four: Matthew, Mark, Luke, John. Four accounts of one life.
2. **Acts**, one: the church's first thirty years or so, by the author of Luke.
3. **Letters**, twenty-one: thirteen with Paul's name on them, arranged roughly by length (Romans is first because it is longest, not because it came first), then Hebrews, then seven from James, Peter, John, and Jude.
4. **Revelation**, one: a vision, in a kind of writing the first readers knew and we mostly don't.

Four, one, twenty-one, one: twenty-seven. This count is the same in every Christian Bible, Protestant, Catholic, and Orthodox.

Notice again that nothing here is in date order. On any dating, conservative or critical, Paul's letters are the earliest Christian writings we have, and they sit after the Gospels and Acts. The Gospels come first because they tell the story the letters assume. The letters are arranged by author and length. Revelation is last because it ends the story. It is a reading order, and a good one.

## The mechanism: a library, not a timeline

Here is the idea that makes the layout worth learning rather than just memorising.

The order of the books is a *reading* order. The books are grouped by the kind of writing they are, the way a library puts fiction on one wall, history on another, and poetry in a corner. So the shelf a book sits on tells you what kind of thing it is before you open it. A book among the History books is going to be narrative: people, events, a narrator reporting. A book among the Prophets is going to be oracles: speeches from God, delivered to a particular audience in a particular crisis, collected later. A Letter is one side of a conversation. A Gospel is a portrait of a life.

That prediction matters because reading is mostly done by expectation. You read a recipe differently from a novel, and you knew which one you were holding before the first sentence. The Bible's first readers had the same reflexes for their own kinds of writing. We have mostly lost them, and the shelves are the quickest way back. Lesson 3 will make this the first rule of reading; for now, it is enough to see that the shelf is a clue you can use from a closed book.

The mechanism cuts the other way too. Because the order is by kind, it is not by date. Job may be one of the oldest stories in the collection and it sits in the middle. Paul wrote before Mark, and Mark is printed first. If you assume the pages run in time order, you will misplace nearly everything, and lesson 2 will fix that by giving you the timeline the shelves don't.

:::checkpoint You open to 1 Kings 17 with no idea what it says. From its shelf alone: what kind of book is it, and what do you expect to find?
It is in the History section of the Christian Old Testament (the Former Prophets in the Tanakh). So expect narrative: named people, events in sequence, a narrator who mostly reports and only occasionally judges. You'd expect kings, since the book is called Kings, and in fact this is where Elijah walks in and confronts one. What you should not expect is a poem or a list of laws, and you should not assume that everything the people in it do is being recommended. That last habit is lesson 3's.
:::

## Worked example 1: three books from a closed Bible

Let's do the simple case, the one you'll do a hundred times. Find Habakkuk, Philemon, and Daniel without the contents page, and say what kind of book each is from its shelf.

**Habakkuk.** It's a prophet, and a short one, so it is among the twelve at the end of the Old Testament. The Twelve run Hosea, Joel, Amos, Obadiah, Jonah, Micah, Nahum, Habakkuk, Zephaniah, Haggai, Zechariah, Malachi. Habakkuk is eighth, so open near the end of the Old Testament and go back a few pages from Malachi. Kind of book: oracles, though Habakkuk is unusual because most of it is the prophet arguing with God rather than God speaking to the people. Still a prophet's book: expect speech, poetry, and a crisis (here, the coming Babylonians).

**Philemon.** It's a letter with Paul's name on it, so it is in the Letters, and since Paul's letters are arranged roughly by length and Philemon is one page, it is the last of them, just before Hebrews. Kind of book: one side of a conversation, about one situation, to one man and his house church. You'll be reconstructing the situation from the letter itself, which is lesson 7's skill.

**Daniel.** Now the wrinkle inside the simple case. In your Christian Bible, Daniel is a prophet, sitting right after Ezekiel and before the Twelve. Kind of book: half court stories (the lions' den, the furnace) and half visions. In the Tanakh, Daniel is in the Writings, between Esther and Ezra.[4] Nothing about the book changes. What changes is what a reader expects walking in. Christian readers have usually met Daniel as prophecy first; Jewish readers meet him as a wise exile first and reach the visions later. Knowing that both shelves exist keeps you from thinking your own is the only one.

Now you do the fourth. Where is Titus, and what kind of book is it? Decide before reading on.

Titus is a short letter of Paul's to one man, so it is in the Letters, near the end of Paul's group, after 1 and 2 Timothy and before Philemon. Expect instructions to a church leader about a specific place (Crete) and specific problems, and expect to have to work out the problems from the instructions.

## Chapters and verses: the numbers nobody wrote

Open any Bible and the page is full of numbers. It is easy to assume they are as old as the words. They are not.

History establishes the following. The chapter divisions used in nearly every Bible today are usually credited to Stephen Langton, later Archbishop of Canterbury, working in Paris in the early thirteenth century.[7] Verse numbers came three hundred years later: Robert Estienne, a Paris printer also known as Stephanus, numbered the verses of his Greek New Testament of 1551 and of a French Bible in 1553.[7] The Geneva Bible of 1560 was the first English Bible to carry them.[7] So the writers of the Bible never saw a chapter number, and the first Christians to read Romans read it as a letter without a single numeral in it.

Why does that matter? Because chapter and verse numbers are a finding aid, like page numbers, and they are wonderful for that. Two people in different countries can go to the same line in seconds. But they are not units of meaning. A verse is not a sentence the author marked off; often it is half a sentence. A chapter is not a section the author planned; often it cuts across one. Treat the numbers as the address of a line, not as a claim about where the thought begins and ends.

:::callout Older markers, same lesson
Jewish scribes had their own ways of dividing the text long before Langton: paragraph breaks in the manuscripts and, by the early Middle Ages, a system of verse-ends marked by the Masoretes. So "nobody divided the text before the 1200s" is too strong. What is true is that the chapter and verse numbers printed in your Bible are the medieval and early-modern layer, and that no biblical author used either.
:::

## Worked example 2: where the chapter number sends you wrong

Here is the case that shows why this is worth a whole section.

Isaiah 53 is the most famous chapter in the Prophets, the one about the suffering servant. Ask most people where to start and they say "verse 1", because the big number in the margin says so. Read it that way and this is what you get:

"Who has believed what he has heard from us? And to whom has the arm of the LORD been revealed? For he grew up before him like a young plant, and like a root out of dry ground; he had no form or majesty that we should look at him..." (Isaiah 53:1–2, ESV)[1]

Who is "he"? You have read two verses and the poem has not told you. That is because the poem did not start here. It started fifteen lines earlier, at what the chapter numbers call 52:13:

"Behold, my servant shall act wisely; he shall be high and lifted up, and shall be exalted." (Isaiah 52:13, ESV)[1]

There he is. The servant is announced, his exaltation is announced, and then his disfigurement, and then the nations' astonishment, all before the chapter break. The poem runs from 52:13 to 53:12, five stanzas of three verses each, and Langton's chapter line falls after the first stanza. A reader who starts at 53:1 walks in during the second verse of a song and spends three lines asking who the singer means.

Now the step for you. Read 52:13 to 53:12 in your own Bible, all fifteen verses, as one poem. Then answer, before reading on: whose voice speaks in the first stanza (52:13–15), and whose in the second (53:1–3)? Say it in a sentence.

Here's mine. The first stanza is God speaking ("my servant"); the second is a group of people speaking about what they had failed to see ("what he has heard from us", "we"). The shift of speaker is the hinge of the poem, and the chapter break lands exactly on it, so that a reader who obeys the number misses the hinge. Nobody did this on purpose. Langton was dividing a whole Bible by hand, and he was mostly right. But he was a reader like us, seven centuries ago, and his line is not the poet's.

Here's the mirror case, where the numbers split what belongs together. Psalms 9 and 10 in your Bible are two psalms. Read them together and the stanzas step through the Hebrew alphabet, with gaps, as one alphabetic poem; and the Septuagint, the Greek Old Testament, prints them as a single psalm of thirty-nine verses, with our Psalm 10 beginning at its verse 22.[8] Two numberings, one poem, and no fault in either; but a reader who only knows one numbering will think Psalm 10 has a strange abrupt opening, when it is simply the second half.

:::checkpoint A friend says: "The Bible has chapter numbers, so the chapters must be the sections the authors intended." What's the two-sentence answer?
The chapter numbers were added in the thirteenth century and the verse numbers in the sixteenth, so no author of the Bible ever saw them. They are a finding aid, usually well placed and sometimes badly, and the only way to know where a unit of thought starts and ends is to read across the numbers and look.
:::

## What people get wrong

**"The books are in the order they were written."** They are grouped by kind, and within each group by other rules (length, author, tradition). Paul's letters are earlier than the Gospels and printed later. Job sits in the middle and may be among the oldest material in the book. Lesson 2 gives you the timeline; the contents page never will.

**"Chapter and verse are part of the text."** You've seen the dates. They are the address of a line. When you meet a break that seems to fall in an odd place, it probably does, and you are free to read across it.

**"The Old Testament is Judaism's Bible with a Christian sequel."** Half true in a way that misleads. The books are the same, and Christians did receive them from Israel. But the Tanakh is a living Scripture, read today by Jews on its own terms, in its own order, with its own last word, and not as a prelude to anything. Whenever this course speaks of how Jews read it, it will say so in their terms, and lesson 2 will show you how the same books tell two stories, each with its own integrity.

**"Forty authors over fifteen hundred years."** You will hear this often, and it is a fine sentence on one set of dates: Moses around 1400 BC, John around AD 95. On the dates most critical scholars hold, the earliest books are much later and the span is under a thousand years. Scholars argue about the dates of the earliest books, and lesson 10 gives both cases. The honest form of the sentence is "roughly a thousand to fifteen hundred years, depending on when you date the earliest books". Either way, it is a library written across many centuries, by many hands, in three languages (Hebrew, a little Aramaic, and Greek), and that is remarkable enough without rounding.

**"The Bible I own is the Bible."** If yours has sixty-six books, a Catholic friend's has seventy-three, and an Orthodox friend's has more. History establishes what each contains. Which list is Scripture is answered by each tradition's faith and its church. This course uses sixty-six and will give you every side's case in lesson 8.

## Connections

Everything in this course depends on being able to find things and knowing what shelf they came from.

- Lesson 2 puts the shelves on a timeline, so that you can place any book in the one story from creation to new creation, and so that "which came first, Ezra or Isaiah?" has an answer the contents page can't give.
- Lesson 3 turns "what shelf is this on?" into the first rule of reading: identify the kind of writing before you decide what a sentence means. You have already started doing that with Habakkuk, Philemon, and Daniel.
- Lesson 8 comes back to the counts: sixty-six, seventy-three, and more, with each tradition's case in its own words.
- Lesson 9 comes back to the numbers in the margins, when you see manuscripts like the Isaiah scroll above and learn how the words reached your page.

## Go deeper

- **Christine Hayes, RLST 145 lecture 1, "The Parts of the Whole"** (Open Yale Courses, free video and transcript): fifty minutes on the layout of the Hebrew Bible from a critical scholar who never asks you to believe or disbelieve. The other side's best teacher, and the right place to hear the Jewish arrangement described by someone who works in it.
- **Fee and Stuart, *How to Read the Bible for All Its Worth*, chapter 1, "The Need to Interpret"**: the argument that the Bible is a library of kinds of writing and that reading each kind by its own rules is not a scholar's luxury but every reader's first job.
- **Longman and Dillard, *An Introduction to the Old Testament*, introduction**: the standard evangelical survey, book by book, with a clear account of how the Christian and Jewish arrangements relate.
- **Josephus, *Against Apion* 1.8** (Whiston translation, free online): two paragraphs from a Jewish historian around AD 95 counting his people's sacred books, worth reading in full to see how early the collection was fixed.
