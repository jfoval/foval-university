> I can do all things through him who strengthens me. (Philippians 4:13, ESV)[5]

You have seen this verse on a wristband, a gym wall, a graduation card. Read on its own, it sounds like a promise that whatever you set out to do, God will see you through. Athletes quote it before a match. Students quote it before an exam.

Here is a habit worth building before you read another verse of a New Testament letter: ask what the sentence just before it says.

:::predict Philippians 4:13 is the last sentence of a paragraph. Without looking, what do you think verses 11 and 12 are about? Success? Ministry? Something else?
They are about being hungry. "Not that I am speaking of being in need, for I have learned, in whatever situation I am, to be content. I know how to be brought low, and I know how to abound. In any and every circumstance, I have learned the secret of facing plenty and hunger, abundance and need." (Philippians 4:11-12, ESV)[5] Paul is in prison. The Philippians have sent him a gift, and he is thanking them while explaining that he would have been all right without it. "All things" is plenty and hunger, abundance and need. The verse is about contentment when things go wrong, not achievement when you want them to go right.
:::

That is the whole lesson in one verse. Letters mean what they mean inside their paragraphs and inside the situation that produced them. Take the sentence out and it will still sound like English, and it will say something Paul never said.

Revelation, at the other end of the New Testament, has the opposite problem. Nobody pulls a verse out of Revelation and puts it on a card. People do something stranger: they treat a book of pictures as a code and try to match it to the news. This lesson is about both mistakes, and they turn out to be the same mistake wearing two costumes.

## A letter is half a conversation

Twenty-one of the twenty-seven New Testament books are letters. Gordon Fee, who wrote the chapters on the epistles in the textbook this course is built on, says the one thing every one of them has in common is that they are "occasional documents", written out of a specific occasion to a specific set of readers.[1] Something had happened in Corinth or Galatia or Colossae. Someone had written to Paul, or news had reached him, and he wrote back.

That means we are reading one side of an exchange. Fee compares it to hearing one end of a telephone conversation and trying to work out who is on the other end and what they are saying.[1] We have the answers. We often don't know the questions.

This sounds like a problem for scholars. It isn't. It is a problem for anyone who opens a letter, because the meaning of a reply sits in the gap between what the writer says and what the readers already knew. If you skip the reconstruction you don't read the letter more simply; you read a different letter, one you have written yourself out of the fragments.

Two habits follow from this, and both come from Fee.

**Think paragraphs.** Fee's own emphasis: the necessary key to understanding a letter's argument is to learn to "THINK PARAGRAPHS", asking of each one what it says and why the writer says it at this point.[1] Verses are a sixteenth-century filing system (lesson 1). Paragraphs are the writer's units of thought. Philippians 4:13 is not a unit; 4:10-13 is.

**Apply the basic rule.** You met it in lesson 3: "a text cannot mean what it never could have meant to its author or readers."[2] In the narrative lessons this rule mostly kept us honest about the past. With the letters it starts doing real work, because letters are so quotable and so easy to quote against their own sense. Fee's example is 1 Corinthians 13:10, "when the perfect comes, the partial will be done away", which some readers take to mean that the finished New Testament has ended prophecy and tongues. Whatever you think about that question, Fee's point is that the Corinthians did not know there was going to be a New Testament, so the sentence could not have meant that to them, and therefore cannot mean it.[2]

## Worked example: the love chapter

1 Corinthians 13 is read at weddings. "Love is patient and kind; love does not envy or boast; it is not arrogant or rude." (1 Corinthians 13:4, ESV)[5] It is a beautiful thing to read at a wedding, and nothing in this lesson says stop.

But look at the paragraph on either side.

:::predict Read the last verse of chapter 12 and the first verse of chapter 14, the sentences that frame the love chapter. What do you expect them to be about, and how many "senses" of the chapter change once you know?
Chapter 12 ends: "But earnestly desire the higher gifts. And I will show you a still more excellent way." Chapter 14 opens: "Pursue love, and earnestly desire the spiritual gifts, especially that you may prophesy." (1 Corinthians 12:31; 14:1, ESV)[5] The love chapter sits inside three chapters about spiritual gifts in a church where people were competing over them. Read that way, almost every line changes its target. "Does not envy or boast" is aimed at people boasting about tongues. "If I speak in the tongues of men and of angels, but have not love" is not a poetic flourish; it is the presenting problem. The chapter is not describing a couple. It is telling a divided congregation what their gifts are for.
:::

Notice what did not happen. The chapter did not stop being about love, and it did not stop being true. What changed is that we now know what Paul was doing with it, and that gives us the right to use it elsewhere. A wedding is a place where two people are about to find out whether love is patient. The application is legitimate because the principle is the same. But we got there by reading the paragraph first, not by reading the wedding first.

:::checkpoint Philemon is twenty-five verses and takes five minutes. Read it now, then reconstruct the situation from the letter alone in four lines: who is writing, to whom, about whom, and what is being asked.
Paul, a prisoner, with Timothy, writes to Philemon, who hosts a church in his house (verses 1-2). It concerns Onesimus, Philemon's slave, who has been with Paul in prison and become a Christian there ("my child, Onesimus, whose father I became in my imprisonment", verse 10). Paul is sending him back. The request, never stated as a command, is that Philemon receive him "no longer as a bondservant but more than a bondservant, as a beloved brother" and welcome him "as you would receive me" (verses 16-17), with the closing hint that Philemon will "do even more than I say" (verse 21).[5] Everything else about the situation, including how Onesimus came to be with Paul, is something the letter assumes and we have to infer. Fee notes Philemon is one of the few letters occasioned from the writer's side rather than the readers'.[1]
:::

## When their situation is not ours

So far the rule has been: find out what it meant to them. The next question is the one people actually fight about: what does it ask of us?

Fee gives a second rule. "Whenever we share comparable particulars (i.e., similar specific life situations) with the first-century hearers, God's word to us is the same as his word to them."[2] "All have sinned" is still true. "Clothe yourselves with compassion" is still an instruction. Nothing about the gap between the first century and now changes those, because our particulars match theirs.

The hard cases are where the particulars don't match. Fee sorts them into two kinds.[2] Some first-century situations have no real counterpart for most modern readers: whether to eat at a feast in a pagan temple (1 Corinthians 8-10), for instance. Others could recur but are unlikely. For both, he suggests two steps. First, do the exegesis carefully enough to find the principle the instruction expresses. Second, and this is the part people skip, apply that principle only to genuinely comparable situations, not to anything you like.[2] The stumbling-block principle of 1 Corinthians 8 is his example: it is about inducing another believer to act against their conscience, not about anyone who happens to be offended, and it is regularly abused by long-standing Christians against younger ones.[2]

Duvall and Hays, in the other standard textbook, picture the same procedure as crossing a river. Measure how wide the river is between their town and ours (time, culture, covenant), cross it on a bridge made of the principle, check the principle against the rest of the Bible, and only then land in our town.[4]

Now the honest part. Fee says out loud what most readers know and don't say: nearly all Christians already do this. Evangelicals leave "a little wine for thy stomach's sake" in the first century, do not insist on head coverings, and do not exchange the holy kiss. Many of the same readers, he observes, "wince" when a woman's teaching in church is defended on the same grounds, and grow indignant when same-sex partnerships are.[2] His point is not that all these cases are alike. It is that you cannot pretend to be doing no sorting. Every reader sorts. The question is whether you sort with a method you could defend, or by instinct dressed up as literalism.

Fee offers guidelines for the sorting, worked out with his colleague David Scholer, and he is careful to say they are his current thinking and not settled doctrine.[2] Four of them do most of the work:

1. Distinguish the central core of the gospel (human fallenness, redemption through Christ's death and resurrection, his return) from what is peripheral to it. The holy kiss and head coverings, he says, "would seem to be less so".[2]
2. Distinguish what the New Testament treats as inherently moral (its sin lists never contain cultural items) from what is not.[2]
3. Note where the New Testament speaks with one voice and where it doesn't. He lists women's ministries as a place where the witness is not uniform, citing Phoebe the deacon, Junia among the apostles, and 1 Corinthians 11:5 alongside 1 Timothy 2:12.[2]
4. Distinguish, within a passage, the principle from its specific application. A writer can support a relative application with an absolute principle without making the application absolute.[2]

That fourth guideline is where our second worked example lives.

## Worked example, with a wrinkle: head coverings

1 Corinthians 11:2-16 tells the women of Corinth to cover their heads when they pray or prophesy, and the men not to. Almost no church in the West today requires it, and the ones that do are a small minority. Whatever you conclude, that is where the reading starts: with a passage most Christians have decided, one way or another, not to apply as written.

Walk the test.

**What is the particular?** A head covering (or, on another reading, a hairstyle) for a woman praying or prophesying in the assembly. Note in passing that the passage assumes women do pray and prophesy aloud; Fee points out that many who argue from this chapter for male headship deny the thing it takes for granted.[1]

**What reasons does Paul give?** This is your step. Read verses 3 to 16 and list every kind of reason he offers before reading on.

Here is my list. He argues from an order of headship: "the head of every man is Christ, the head of a wife is her husband, and the head of Christ is God" (verse 3).[5] He argues from creation: "man was not made from woman, but woman from man" (verse 8).[5] He gives a reason nobody has confidently explained: a wife "ought to have a symbol of authority on her head, because of the angels" (verse 10).[5] He appeals to their own judgement of what is proper (verse 13) and to "nature itself" and the disgrace of a man with long hair (verse 14).[5] And he closes with an appeal to practice: "If anyone is inclined to be contentious, we have no such practice, nor do the churches of God." (verse 16)[5]

**Which of these is principle, and which is application?** Fee's reading is that Paul appeals to the order of creation and redemption (verse 3) to establish a principle, that nothing should distract from the glory of God when the community is at worship, while the specific application seems relative, since Paul keeps appealing to "practice" and "nature".[2] On that reading, a woman who literally obeyed the text in most American churches "would thereby almost certainly abuse the spirit of the text by drawing attention to herself".[2] The principle survives; the covering was its first-century form.

**Do our particulars match?** Here Christians divide, and the division is the wrinkle. Three broad positions, each in its own terms:

*Some Christians keep the covering.* Their reasoning: Paul grounds the practice in creation and in headship, not in Corinthian custom; the creation order did not change in AD 100; and "we have no such practice" means the covering was the practice of every church, not one local rule. On this reading the particular and the principle are the same thing, and dropping the covering is dropping Paul.

*Many Christians who hold to male headship in church and home (they call themselves complementarians) drop the covering but keep the headship.* Their reasoning: verse 3 is the principle and verses 4 to 16 apply it in a culture where a covering signalled a married woman's honour; the sign was cultural, the order it signalled was not; and the same Paul who wrote this chapter wrote 1 Timothy 2:12 restricting women from teaching men, so the principle has other applications that remain.

*Many Christians who hold that men and women serve without role distinction (they call themselves egalitarians) read the whole passage as cultural.* Their reasoning: the passage assumes women praying and prophesying in public, which is the point being protected; "head" in Greek often means "source" rather than "authority over", which changes verse 3; verses 11 and 12 immediately balance the creation argument ("woman was made from man" and "man is now born of woman"); and the closing appeal to custom shows what kind of argument the whole thing was.

All three groups accept the comparable-particulars test. They land differently because they disagree about what the principle is and which verses carry it. That is not a failure of the method. The method's job is to make the disagreement visible and honest: each side has to say which particulars it is keeping and why. What the method rules out is pretending the text has no particulars at all, in either direction. "Everything Paul told the Corinthians is for us as written" has to explain the covering. "Everything cultural can be dismissed" has to explain why "all have sinned" survives and the covering doesn't.

We hold, with Fee, that the principle of verse 3 is Paul's and that the covering was its first-century application, and we say that a Christian who keeps the covering, or who reads the headship as cultural too, is reading the same passage seriously and deserves the same hearing.

:::callout What the test is not
The comparable-particulars test is a way of asking what a text asks of you now. It is not a way of getting rid of texts you dislike, and it is not a way of freezing first-century Corinth as the divinely ordained culture. Fee's own warning is that there is no such thing as a divinely ordained culture, and that readers who try to reject cultural relativity altogether usually end up adopting it inconsistently.[2] If the test only ever tells you what you already wanted to hear, you are not running it.
:::

## Apocalyptic works the other way round

Now turn to the last book. A letter hides half its meaning in the situation, so the reader's job is to reconstruct what is not on the page. Revelation does the opposite. It puts everything on the page, in pictures, and the reader's job is to see the pictures rather than to translate them into something else.

Fee describes Revelation as "a unique, finely blended combination of three distinct literary types: apocalypse, prophecy, and letter".[3] Each of those words tells you something about how to read it.

**It is a letter.** It opens "John to the seven churches that are in Asia: Grace to you and peace" (1:4)[5] and closes with a letter's blessing. So everything you have just learned applies: it was occasioned by real churches in real cities with real trouble, and it meant something to them first.[3]

**It is prophecy.** John calls it "this prophecy" (1:3) and expects it to be read aloud in church: "Blessed is the one who reads aloud the words of this prophecy, and blessed are those who hear, and who keep what is written in it."[5] Prophecy in the Bible's sense (lesson 4) is God's word spoken into a present situation, with judgment and salvation as its usual content. Revelation is that: a word from God, Fee says, "to some churches in the latter part of the first century who are undergoing persecution from without and some decay from within".[3]

**It is an apocalypse.** That is the genre we have no modern equivalent for. Dozens of Jewish and Christian apocalypses survive from about 200 BC to AD 200, and they share conventions: they grow out of Old Testament prophecy, especially Ezekiel, Daniel, and Zechariah; they were born in persecution; they are written literature from the start, not collected speeches; they work in visions and symbols; their images are fantasy rather than realism (a beast with seven heads, a woman clothed with the sun); and they are stylised, with a fondness for numbered sets and for numbers as symbols.[3] Revelation fits every one of those but one: it is not pseudonymous. John signs it and sends it to people he knows.[3]

Here is the mechanism, and it is the mirror of the letter mechanism. A letter's meaning sits partly off the page, so a reader who refuses to reconstruct gets less than the writer meant. An apocalypse's meaning sits entirely in the picture, so a reader who decodes the picture into events has changed the genre. Fee's rule for the visions is that they work like parables: see each one as a whole, because "the whole vision is trying to say something", and treat the details as either dramatic effect or as filling in the picture so the first readers could not mistake what was meant.[3] The sun turning black and the stars falling like figs in chapter 6 probably do not "mean" anything individually; they make the earthquake more terrible.[3] The locusts with crowns and human faces in chapter 9, on the other hand, are detailed so that a Roman-era reader would recognise the barbarian hordes on the empire's edge.[3]

Three conventions follow.

**Numbers are symbols.** Seven churches, seven seals, seven trumpets, seven bowls; twelve tribes and twelve apostles multiplied into 144,000; forty-two months; a thousand years. Fee places the "great fondness for the symbolic use of numbers" among the standard features of the genre.[3] A reader who takes 144,000 as a headcount has missed twelve times twelve times a thousand.

**The images come from the Old Testament.** John cites or echoes the Old Testament, on Fee's count, "over 250 times", so that "every significant moment in his narrative is imaged almost exclusively in Old Testament language".[3] The first readers knew their Scriptures and heard the echoes at once; we have to look them up. This is the single most useful thing you can do with Revelation: keep Daniel, Ezekiel, Isaiah, and Exodus open beside it.

**John interprets some of his own images, and those are the anchors.** Fee lists six: the one like a son of man is Christ; the lampstands are the churches; the seven stars are the churches' angels or messengers; the dragon is Satan; the seven heads are seven hills, and also seven kings; and the prostitute is the great city, which is Rome.[3] Start from the images the author has decoded before you decode any others.

:::checkpoint Read Revelation 1:12-16, John's first vision of Christ: hair white as wool, eyes like flame, feet like bronze, a voice like many waters, a sword from his mouth. Name three Old Testament passages the picture is built from.
Fee's answer is that the picture "is based primarily on Daniel 10:6 but is a superb collage of that passage with Daniel 7:9, 13; Isaiah 49:2; Ezekiel 1:24".[3] Daniel 7:9 gives the hair "white like wool"; Daniel 7:13 gives "one like a son of man"; Daniel 10:6 gives the eyes like flame, the bronze limbs, and the voice like a multitude; Ezekiel 1:24 gives the sound of many waters; Isaiah 49:2 gives the mouth like a sharp sword. Not one detail was invented for the occasion. Every one carries its Old Testament context with it: the Ancient of Days, the coming Son of Man, the servant whose word is a weapon. That is what the picture is doing. It is not a description of what Jesus looks like; it is a claim about who he is, made in the only language that could make it.
:::

## Worked example: the number of the beast

> This calls for wisdom: let the one who has understanding calculate the number of the beast, for it is the number of a man, and his number is 666. (Revelation 13:18, ESV)[5]

Read badly, this is an invitation to identify a modern figure. Every generation has done it, with popes, emperors, presidents, and prime ministers, and every generation's candidate has died without the world ending.

Read well, it is an invitation to the first readers to do some arithmetic they were equipped for. Greek and Hebrew both used letters as numerals, so any name had a numerical value; the practice is called gematria. The verse says "calculate", and it says the number is "of a man". John expected his readers to be able to do it.

:::predict Your ESV prints a footnote on this verse: "Some manuscripts 616." Before reading on: how old do you think the oldest manuscript with 616 is, and what would a different number do to the "modern figure" reading?
The oldest surviving witness to the 616 reading is Papyrus 115, a set of Oxyrhynchus fragments now in Oxford, dated by its editor to the late third or early fourth century.[7] It is one of the earliest manuscripts of this part of Revelation at all. Codex Ephraemi, from the fifth century, also reads 616.[7] If the number can be either 666 or 616 in the early copies, then whatever it points to must be something that works either way, which is a serious constraint on any reading, and a fatal one for readings that depend on the exact digits of a modern name.
:::

:::figure https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/5/58/Oxford%2C_Sackler_Library_Ms_LXIV_4499_%28Papyrus_115%29_Plate_3_verso_crop_Number_of_the_Beast_%CF%87%CE%B9%CF%9B_616.jpg | Fragment of Papyrus 115 showing Revelation 13:18 with the number of the beast written as the Greek letters chi, iota, digamma, which is 616
Papyrus 115 (P.Oxy. LXIV 4499), Sackler Library, Oxford: the oldest surviving copy of Revelation 13:18, reading 616 rather than 666. The number is written as three Greek letters with a line above them, the way numerals were written. Image via Wikimedia Commons, public domain.[16]
:::

Look at the fragment. That is what a verse looks like in the third century: three letters with a bar over them, no verse number, no chapter break, no footnote.

Now the common scholarly reading. Write "Nero Caesar" in Hebrew letters as the Greek form of the name (Neron Qesar) and add up the letter values, and the total is 666. Write it as the Latin form (Nero Qesar), dropping the final letter *nun*, worth 50, and the total is 616.[7] One name explains both numbers. The New Testament textual scholar P. J. Williams, surveying the evidence in the *Tyndale Bulletin* in 2007, says this identification has been popular since the nineteenth century and that the 616 variant has been taken to support it.[7] History also establishes that Nero was the emperor who, after the fire of Rome in AD 64, "fastened the guilt and inflicted the most exquisite tortures" on the Christians of the city, in the Roman historian Tacitus's words,[17] and the interpretation Williams discusses assumes the belief, attested in antiquity, that Nero would return.[7] A Roman emperor who demanded honour and killed Christians is exactly what chapter 13 describes.

Here is where the sorting matters. **History establishes** that the early copies read 666 and 616, that both numbers can be produced from Nero's name, and that Irenaeus, writing about 180, knew the 616 variant and rejected it, saying that 666 was "found in all the most approved and ancient copies".[6] **Scholars argue** about whether Nero is the intended referent. Williams himself urges caution: the Hebrew spelling of the Greek word for "beast" also comes to 666, and its shorter form to 616, so the 616 reading "should not therefore be taken as giving exclusive support to the interpretation Nero Caesar".[7] Nero is the most widely held reading; it is not the only one, and the lesson gives it as argued, not settled.

And Irenaeus, who was closer to John than we are by eighteen centuries, refused to name anyone. He tried out three Greek names that add to 666, thought one of them slightly more likely than the others, and then declined: it is "more certain, and less hazardous, to await the fulfilment of the prophecy, than to be making surmises".[6] That is the earliest Christian commentator on the verse telling you not to do what the modern pamphlets do.

## Worked example: the woman, the child, and the dragon

Chapter 12 is, in Fee's words, "the theological key to the book".[3] It is also the clearest case of a vision that reads as a timeline if you don't know the Old Testament and as a picture if you do.

"And a great sign appeared in heaven: a woman clothed with the sun, with the moon under her feet, and on her head a crown of twelve stars. She was pregnant and was crying out in birth pains and the agony of giving birth." (12:1-2)[5] A dragon waits to devour the child. The child is born, "one who is to rule all the nations with a rod of iron", and is "caught up to God and to his throne" (12:5).[5] The dragon is thrown down; John decodes him for us as "that ancient serpent, who is called the devil and Satan" (12:9).[5] The woman flees into the wilderness, carried on eagle's wings, and is kept there for a time.

Read as a timeline, this is baffling. Who is the woman, when is the birth, why does the child go straight from birth to throne with nothing in between?

Read as a picture, every piece has an address. The serpent is Genesis 3, and the enmity between the serpent and the woman's offspring is Genesis 3:15. The child who rules the nations with a rod of iron is Psalm 2:9. The woman with twelve stars is Israel, Joseph's dream of sun, moon, and stars in Genesis 37:9. Eagle's wings and a wilderness where God feeds his people are the exodus: "I bore you on eagles' wings", Exodus 19:4, and forty years of manna. Birth pains are the prophets' image for Israel's labour to bring forth salvation (Isaiah 26:17 and 66:7). The child's leap from birth to throne is not a gap in the story; it is a picture of the whole career of Jesus compressed to its two ends, born and enthroned, with the cross implied in the dragon's failure.

So what is the picture doing? Fee's answer: it tells of Satan's attempt to destroy Christ and of his own defeat instead, so that within the New Testament's already-and-not-yet, Satan is a defeated foe whose end has not yet come, which is why there is rejoicing in heaven and woe on earth at once.[3] The church in Asia Minor, watching Rome sharpen its knives, is being told who is actually losing.

Notice the spaced review. In lesson 4 you learned that prophecy has a near horizon and a far one. Revelation has both too: Rome's judgment and the last judgment are pictured in overlapping language, and Fee says plainly that "there seems to be no way one can deny the reality of this".[3] What the genre adds is that both horizons are painted, not charted. In lesson 6 you learned that a parable works by trapping the hearer with a story. An apocalyptic vision is a cousin: it works by showing the hearer a picture whose Old Testament address they already know, so that the recognition does the arguing.

## The four schools, in their own terms

So far the rules have been the ones nearly every serious commentator accepts. Now the part where they part company. Fee counts "at least five major schools of interpretation, not to mention significant variations within each",[3] and the four names every introduction uses are preterist, historicist, futurist, and idealist. They differ over one question: when do the events pictured in Revelation 4-19 happen?

Look at the bars. The preterist bar sits at the left end; the futurist bar sits at the right; the historicist bar runs the whole length as a sequence; the idealist bar runs the whole length as a pattern. The four are not four levels of seriousness. They are four answers to one question, and each has scholars behind it.

**Preterist.** From the Latin for "past". The preterist says that Revelation, like every other New Testament letter, was written to its own generation about events "that must soon take place" (1:1)[5], and that "soon" meant soon. The beast was Rome; the harlot city was either Jerusalem, judged in AD 70, or Rome; most of chapters 4-19 was fulfilled in the first century. What the book says to later readers, it says the way Amos does: by what it said to its own. Its best-known current defender is Kenneth Gentry, who wrote the preterist chapter in Zondervan's *Four Views on the Book of Revelation* (1998)[8] and whose 1989 book *Before Jerusalem Fell* argues that Revelation was written before 70, under Nero, which the reading needs.[12] Preterists are quick to say what their view is not. Gentry calls his own position "orthodox preterism" and has written a whole book against what he calls "hyper-preterism", the view that Christ's return, the resurrection, and the final judgment all happened in AD 70; his preterism keeps all three in the future.[18] And it does not mean the book "has nothing to say" now. It means the book says it the way the rest of the Bible does, by speaking first to its first readers.

**Historicist.** The historicist reads Revelation 4-19 as a continuous prophecy of the whole course of church history from John's day to the end, so that the seals, trumpets, and bowls are successive eras and the beast and harlot are institutions that rose and fell along the way. This was the reading of Luther, Calvin, and most Protestants for three centuries after the Reformation, who identified the papacy as the antichrist of the book, and its fullest statement is Edward Bishop Elliott's four-volume *Horae Apocalypticae* (1844).[13] It is the view of the Seventh-day Adventist tradition today, and it is otherwise now rare among commentators, which is why the *Four Views* volume gave its fourth chair to a second futurist position rather than to a historicist.[8] Its strength, in its own terms, is that it takes the book's claim to be prophecy of what follows John's day with full seriousness and refuses to leave eighteen centuries of the church's suffering outside the picture. Its difficulty, which its holders acknowledge, is that each generation's map has had to be redrawn as history went on.

**Futurist.** The futurist reads chapters 4-19 as describing a period still ahead of us, immediately before Christ returns: a tribulation, an antichrist, the final battle. This is the reading behind most popular prophecy books, but it is also held with great care by serious scholars, and it comes in kinds. Classical dispensationalists, represented in *Four Views* by Robert Thomas, read the book in sequence and as literally as the language allows, keep Israel and the church distinct, and place the church's removal before the tribulation.[8] Progressive dispensationalists, represented by C. Marvin Pate, hold that the kingdom has already begun in Christ and will be completed in a future tribulation and return.[8] Historic premillennialists, whose great modern representative was George Eldon Ladd, blend preterist and futurist readings: the book spoke of Rome to its first readers and also foretells the return of Jesus, and Ladd's 1972 commentary is the standard statement.[11] What all futurists share, in their own terms, is that the book's central promise is a real future event and that reading it only as first-century politics or as timeless pattern drains the promise.

**Idealist.** The idealist (sometimes "spiritual" or "timeless") reads Revelation as a symbolic presentation of the conflict between God and evil that recurs in every age, so that the beast is every state that demands worship, Babylon is every economy built on exploitation, and the book's comfort is available to every persecuted church without needing a date. Sam Hamstra wrote the idealist chapter in *Four Views*.[8] Idealists say in their own terms that this is simply reading apocalyptic as the genre it is: pictures of realities, not a calendar; and that the church has in fact used the book this way in every century, which is why it has never gone out of date.

**Mixed readings, which most commentators now hold.** The two most-used scholarly commentaries in English are both mixtures. G. K. Beale (1999) calls his approach "eclecticism, or a redemptive-historical form of modified idealism": largely idealist, but modified, because Christ's actual return and the last judgment are in the book.[9] Grant Osborne (2002) is also eclectic, with the preterist, idealist, and futurist readings allowed to interact, and the futurist element given the leading place.[10] Ladd, as noted, mixed preterist and futurist.[11] What these readings share is the recognition that the book spoke to Rome and its churches (preterist), pictures a pattern that recurs (idealist), and promises a real end (futurist), and that the genre's own conventions, near and far horizons painted in one picture, are why all three are there at once.

We prefer a mixed reading in the Beale and Osborne family, because it is the one that takes all three of Revelation's genres seriously: as a letter it spoke to its first readers, as prophecy it speaks to every age, and as apocalypse its pictures reach to the end. We say that once, and we say plainly that a Christian who reads as a futurist or a preterist is reading the same book with the same seriousness, and that the disagreement is contested within the faith, not a mark of who is faithful.

One more place the divergence shows, closing a loop from lesson 4. Ezekiel 40-48 describes a restored temple in detail. A dispensational reader, keeping Israel's promises for Israel, expects a literal temple in a future kingdom, and reads Revelation's thousand years (20:1-6) as that kingdom. A covenant reader takes the temple as fulfilled in Christ and his people, and reads the thousand years as either the present age or a picture. The same framework that sorted the law in lesson 4 sorts the end here. Neither reader is ignoring the text; they are reading it through different accounts of how the Old Testament's promises reach us.

### When was it written?

The question underneath the preterist reading is the date. Irenaeus, about 180, wrote that the vision was seen "no very long time since, but almost in our day, towards the end of Domitian's reign".[6] Domitian died in 96, so the traditional date is about 95, and it remains the majority view. Gentry and other preterists argue for a date before 70, under Nero, on internal grounds (the temple still standing in chapter 11, the sixth king of chapter 17).[12] History establishes what Irenaeus said; scholars argue over whether he was right and how much weight his testimony carries. The lesson doesn't need to settle it. Whichever date you take, the first readers were churches in the Roman province of Asia under pressure from the state.

## What people get wrong

**"A verse means what it says on its own."** Philippians 4:13 is the lesson's opening for this reason. A verse is a numbered slice of a paragraph, and a paragraph is a move in an argument. The unit of meaning in a letter is the paragraph.

**"Everything Paul told the Corinthians is for us as written."** Then the holy kiss, the covering, and the wine for Timothy's stomach are for us as written, and almost nobody who says this does them. The honest version is: everything Paul wrote is God's word to us, and the comparable-particulars test is how we find out what it asks.

**"Everything cultural can be dismissed."** Its mirror. The test does not dismiss; it distinguishes principle from application, and the principle stays. And Fee's second guideline stands in the way of anyone who wants to move the sin lists into the "cultural" column: the New Testament's sin lists never contain cultural items.[2]

**"Revelation is a coded timeline of the modern world."** Fee's fifth exegetical point is that apocalypses "seldom intend to give a detailed chronological account of the future", and his hermeneutical warning is that the book "was not intended to prophesy the existence of Communist China, for example, or to give us literal details of the conclusion of history".[3] The first readers had to be able to understand it (the basic rule again), and they had never heard of any modern state.

**"Revelation was written about events already past, so it has nothing to say."** This is not what preterists say, and it is not what the lesson says either. The preterist reads Revelation the way lesson 4 taught you to read Amos: a word to its own generation that speaks to ours because the God who judged Rome has not changed. Even a strict preterist keeps the return of Christ, and Fee, who is not one, says the book's central word, that discipleship goes the way of the cross and God is in control, "needs to be heard again and again in the church" in every age.[3]

**"Apocalyptic is prophecy, so lesson 4's rules apply unchanged."** They overlap and they differ. Both speak God's word into a present crisis with judgment and salvation as the content; both have a near and a far horizon. But prophets spoke and their oracles were collected, while apocalypses were written as books with a structure, so Revelation reads more like an epistle than like Amos: each vision is a building block, and you have to "think paragraphs" here too.[3] And the images differ in kind: the prophets mostly used real things (salt, vultures, half-baked bread); apocalyptic uses fantasy, beasts with seven heads.[3] Same God, same purpose, different contract with the reader.

**Part 1: a whole letter.** Read Philemon in one sitting (twenty-five verses). Then write, in your own words:

1. The situation: who, to whom, about whom, from where. Four lines.
