> "and that all this assembly may know that the LORD saves not with sword and spear. For the battle is the LORD's, and he will give you into our hand." (1 Samuel 17:47, ESV)[3]

Who is the hero of that chapter?

If you grew up anywhere near a church, you already have an answer, and it's David. Small boy, big giant, five smooth stones. The story gets preached as "face your giants": name the Goliath in your life and go at it with the faith of a shepherd. It's a good sermon. It's also a reading the chapter itself argues against, in the very verse you just read, and once you see why, you'll read every story in the Bible differently.

This lesson is about the first decision a reader makes, usually without noticing: what kind of writing is this? Everything downstream depends on it. Get it right and the text starts making sense on its own terms. Get it wrong and you can read every sentence with perfect attention and still misunderstand all of it, while feeling completely clear.

## The decision you're already making

Think about a recipe. "Take two eggs." You don't read that as a claim about the world, or a moral instruction, or a poem. You know it's a recipe, so you know "take" means "use", the eggs are yours, and the sentence is complete when the cake is baked. Nobody taught you that. You learned it by handling recipes.

Now a novel: "She took two eggs." Same words nearly, different kind of writing, and you read them differently without effort. A headline. A love letter. A contract. Each comes with rules, and you apply them before you get to the end of the first line.

Gordon Fee and Douglas Stuart built the most-used introduction to reading the Bible around this one observation. Their method has three steps: work out what kind of writing you are holding, learn the rules of that kind, then read.[1] The Bible is a library, not a book, and the library holds stories, laws, poems, proverbs, sermons of prophets, biographies, letters, and visions. Each was written to be read by its own rules.

Here's the problem. The people who first read a psalm, or a letter from Paul, or Daniel's visions, knew the rules the way you know a recipe's. They'd grown up handling that kind of writing. We haven't. So the reflex they had for free, we have to learn on purpose, as a set of rules. That's what the next five lessons are for, and this one begins with the kind of writing you'll meet most.

## The basic rule

Fee and Stuart state one rule that sits underneath every genre, and they call it the basic rule: "a text cannot mean what it never could have meant to its author or readers".[1]

Read that twice, because it's doing a lot of work. It doesn't say a text can only mean one thing. It doesn't say a text has nothing to say to you. It sets a boundary: whatever you take from a passage, it must be something the writer and the first readers could have recognised. If Paul could not have meant it and the Corinthians could not have heard it, it isn't there.

Most bad readings of the Bible fail this rule. They don't fail by being too careful. They fail by starting from "what does this mean to me?", which is a real question, but it's the third question, not the first. Grant Osborne describes interpretation as a spiral that moves between the text's world and the reader's, with three stages in order: what it meant, what it means for me, and how to share it.[6] Kevin Vanhoozer spends a whole book defending the idea that meaning is what an author did with words, a communicative act, and that recovering it is possible and worth the trouble.[7] Scott Duvall and Daniel Hays, in the other standard textbook, teach the same order as a walk: grasp the text in "their town", measure the river between their situation and ours, then cross to "our town".[5] Three books, one order. The first question is always what it meant then.

Genre is where that question starts. You can't ask what a text meant to its first readers until you know what kind of text they thought they were reading.

## Why narrative comes first

Fee and Stuart give two figures. Over 40 percent of the Old Testament is narrative, and the Old Testament is three-quarters of the bulk of the Bible.[1] Put those together and story is the single largest kind of writing in the book. It's also the kind most new readers open first, and the kind they most often misread, because story feels like the one genre that needs no rules. You just follow along.

## What a biblical story is doing

Lesson 2 gave you the three levels of narrative, and this is where they go to work. Fee and Stuart say every Old Testament story runs on three levels at once: the top level is the whole plan of God, from creation to new creation; the middle level is the story of Israel, the people God chose; the bottom level is the individual story on the page, the hundreds of episodes that make up the other two.[1][2] Most readers only ever see the bottom level, and the bottom level, read alone, is where "face your giants" comes from.

Now the rules. Fee and Stuart give ten principles for reading Old Testament narrative. Here they are, condensed, with the ones that matter most for this lesson in bold.[2]

1. An Old Testament narrative usually does not directly teach a doctrine.
2. It usually illustrates a doctrine taught propositionally somewhere else.
3. **Narratives record what happened, not necessarily what should have happened or what ought to happen every time.** So not every story has a moral you can lift out.
4. **What people do in narratives is not necessarily a good example for us. Frequently, it is just the opposite.**
5. Most of the characters are far from perfect, and so are their actions.
6. **We are not always told at the end whether what happened was good or bad.** We are expected to judge from what God has taught plainly elsewhere in Scripture.
7. All narratives are selective and incomplete; what is there is what the writer thought we needed.
8. Narratives are not written to answer all our theological questions.
9. They teach either explicitly (by saying it) or implicitly (by clearly implying it), never secretly.
10. **In the final analysis, God is the hero of all biblical narratives.**

Principles 3, 4, and 6 add up to one habit that will save you from most of the misreadings in this lesson: a story reports, and reporting is not recommending. When the text does want to pass judgement, it has a way of doing it, and you need to learn to hear it. The narrator will step forward and say so.

The clearest case in the Old Testament is the end of 2 Samuel 11. David has taken Bathsheba, arranged her husband's death, and married her. For twenty-six verses the narrator reports it all without a word of comment. Then: "But the thing that David had done displeased the LORD" (2 Samuel 11:27, ESV).[3] One sentence. That's the narrator's voice, and it's the difference between a story that describes and a story that evaluates. When you read a Bible story, mark every sentence like that one. They're rarer than you'd think, and everything in between them is report.

:::callout Description and prescription
A passage *describes* when it tells you what happened. It *prescribes* when it tells you what to do. Narrative is nearly all description. The prescriptions in the Bible live mostly in the law, the prophets, the teaching of Jesus, and the letters, and the sixth principle sends you there to judge what the stories show. A reader who turns every description into a prescription ends up with a Bible that commands polygamy, trickery, and the casting of lots, because all of those are described.
:::

:::checkpoint Judges 11: Jephthah vows that if God gives him victory he will sacrifice whatever first comes out of his door to meet him, and it's his daughter. Which principle stops a reader from treating the vow as a model of devotion, and what does the narrator actually evaluate?
Principles 3, 4, and 6 together. The story records what happened, not what should have happened; Jephthah's act is reported, not recommended; and the narrator gives no verdict at the end of the chapter, so the verdict has to come from elsewhere, where the law forbids child sacrifice outright (Deuteronomy 12:31 and 18:10). The narrator of Judges evaluates the whole period, not this act, with the refrain that in those days there was no king and everyone did what was right in his own eyes (Judges 17:6, 21:25), which tells you how to read Jephthah's vow: as part of the mess, not as an example of faith. Be honest about one wrinkle: some readers, ancient and modern, argue the daughter was dedicated to lifelong service rather than killed, because the text speaks of her weeping for her virginity rather than her life. That reading is a minority one; on either reading the vow was rash, and neither reading makes it a model.
:::

## Worked example 1: David and Goliath, read three times

Open 1 Samuel 17. Read the whole chapter; it takes about ten minutes.

:::figure https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/4/48/Osmar_Schindler_David_und_Goliath.jpg | A colour lithograph: a small, bare-chested David with a sling faces a huge armoured Goliath holding a spear, who bends to look down at him
Osmar Schindler, *David und Goliath*, 1888, colour lithograph. This is the popular reading in a single picture: a small figure, a huge one, and courage as the whole point. Wikimedia Commons, public domain.[9]
:::

Look at Schindler's picture. Everything the popular reading needs is there: David is small and Goliath is enormous, and the story is about nerve. Now let's read the chapter on all three levels and see how much the picture leaves out.

:::predict Before reading on, say who the hero is on each of the three levels. Bottom level, the story of the chapter itself. Middle level, the story of Israel. Top level, the plan of God. Write three names, or three phrases.
Bottom level: David, the unlikely shepherd who beats the champion. Middle level: the question is not David against Goliath but David against Saul, Israel's true king against the king who has failed. Top level: God, who rescues his people through the king he chose, so that "all the earth may know that there is a God in Israel". If you wrote "David" three times, you've read the bottom level three times. Keep going.
:::

**Bottom level.** A shepherd boy, sent with bread and cheese for his brothers, hears a Philistine champion taunt the army of Israel and asks why nobody has answered. He is mocked by his brother, brought to Saul, offered the king's armour, and refuses it. He goes down with a sling and five stones and kills Goliath. On this level the story is about an unlikely hero, and that's real. The narrator means you to feel the size of the giant and the smallness of the boy.

**Middle level.** Now do the next step yourself. Go back to 1 Samuel 9:2 and 10:23, and read what the narrator tells you about Saul's height. Then read 8:20, where Israel says what it wants a king for. Then 1 Samuel 16:7 and 16:13. Then 17:11. Write one sentence on what the chapter is saying about Saul before David ever arrives.

Here's mine. Israel asked for a king to "go out before us and fight our battles" (paraphrasing 8:20), and got a man taller than anyone in the nation; now the tallest man in Israel is hiding from the tallest man in Philistia, "dismayed and greatly afraid" (17:11, ESV).[3] The narrator has already told us, one chapter earlier, that God has rejected Saul and that "man looks on the outward appearance, but the LORD looks on the heart" (16:7, ESV).[3] The Spirit has come on David at his anointing (16:13). So when the boy walks into the valley, the chapter is showing Israel who its real king is. Read the armour scene again with that in mind:

> "Then Saul clothed David with his armor. He put a helmet of bronze on his head and clothed him with a coat of mail, and David strapped his sword over his armor. And he tried in vain to go, for he had not tested them. Then David said to Saul, 'I cannot go with these, for I have not tested them.' So David put them off." (1 Samuel 17:38-39, ESV)[3]

The king's armour doesn't fit the king God chose. That's a narrator's detail, and it's not there by accident. On the middle level the chapter is a scene in the long story of Israel's kingship: the man of outward appearance failing, the man after God's heart stepping into his place.

**Top level.** Now read David's speech, which is the longest thing anyone says in the chapter:

> "You come to me with a sword and with a spear and with a javelin, but I come to you in the name of the LORD of hosts, the God of the armies of Israel, whom you have defied. This day the LORD will deliver you into my hand, and I will strike you down and cut off your head. And I will give the dead bodies of the host of the Philistines this day to the birds of the air and to the wild beasts of the earth, that all the earth may know that there is a God in Israel, and that all this assembly may know that the LORD saves not with sword and spear. For the battle is the LORD's, and he will give you into our hand." (1 Samuel 17:45-47, ESV)[3]

David tells you the point of his own story, in the story. The battle belongs to God. The purpose of the fight is that "all the earth may know that there is a God in Israel". The tenth principle isn't a pious gloss we add afterwards; the narrator built it into the climax. God is the hero of 1 Samuel 17, and David says so before he throws.

So what happens to "face your giants"? It doesn't become false. Courage in the face of something too big for you is on the page. But it's the bottom level, and it was never the reason the chapter was written. A reader who stops there makes David the hero of a story in which David explicitly hands the credit to someone else, and then tries to become David. The chapter would rather you noticed who saves.

## Worked example 2: Gideon's fleece, and the silence that gets read as a yes

The first example was a story most people read on the wrong level. This one is a story most people turn into a method.

Open Judges 6. God has called Gideon to rescue Israel from Midian. Read the call first, because everything depends on it:

> "And the LORD turned to him and said, 'Go in this might of yours and save Israel from the hand of Midian; do not I send you?' And he said to him, 'Please, Lord, how can I save Israel? Behold, my clan is the weakest in Manasseh, and I am the least in my father's house.' And the LORD said to him, 'But I will be with you, and you shall strike the Midianites as one man.'" (Judges 6:14-16, ESV)[3]

That's a direct promise, with a direct commission. Gideon then asks for a sign and gets one (fire from the rock, 6:17-24). He tears down his father's altar to Baal at night, because he's afraid of his family and the town (6:27). The Spirit comes on him, he summons an army, and then, with the army gathered and the promise given twice, we get the fleece:

> "Then Gideon said to God, 'If you will save Israel by my hand, as you have said, behold, I am laying a fleece of wool on the threshing floor. If there is dew on the fleece alone, and it is dry on all the ground, then I shall know that you will save Israel by my hand, as you have said.' And it was so. When he rose early next morning and squeezed the fleece, he wrung enough dew from the fleece to fill a bowl with water. Then Gideon said to God, 'Let not your anger burn against me; let me speak just once more. Please let me test just once more with the fleece. Please let it be dry on the fleece only, and on all the ground let there be dew.' And God did so that night; and it was dry on the fleece only, and on all the ground there was dew." (Judges 6:36-40, ESV)[3]

The popular reading turns this into a method for guidance. "Putting out a fleece" is a phrase Christians use for setting God a test: if the job offer comes by Friday, I'll take it as a yes. The reasoning is that Gideon did it, God answered, and the text doesn't tell him off. So it must be allowed, maybe even wise.

:::predict What does the text say about the fleece afterwards? Does the narrator, or God, or Gideon, ever refer back to it, approve it, or condemn it? Guess before you check.
Nothing. The story moves straight on to the army in chapter 7. No verdict, no comment, no later reference. The silence is total, and that silence is the whole problem: the popular reading has filled it with approval.
:::

Now read it by the rules.

Ask what Gideon is actually doing. He's not seeking guidance. He has it, twice, in God's own words: "do not I send you?" and "you shall strike the Midianites as one man". Look at his own phrase, repeated in both requests: "as you have said". Gideon knows what God has said. He's testing whether God meant it. And notice his own nervousness in verse 39, "Let not your anger burn against me". Gideon knows this is a risky thing to ask. He's a frightened man asking a patient God for reassurance he's already been given.

Now the sixth principle. The narrator gives no verdict, so we can't read one off the page in either direction. What we can do is what the principle says: judge from what God has taught elsewhere. Deuteronomy 6:16 forbids putting the LORD to the test, and Jesus quotes it to Satan in Matthew 4:7. Meanwhile, the story itself shows God's patience: in chapter 7 God gives Gideon a sign he never asked for, sending him down to the enemy camp to overhear a dream (7:9-15). So the story reports a real man's doubt and God's kindness to him. It does not offer a technique.

Here's the wrinkle, and it's the point of choosing this example. The story really doesn't condemn Gideon. If you're expecting a verdict, you'll be tempted to supply one, and readers supply the one they came in with. Some read the silence as approval (so fleeces are fine). Others, wanting to correct that, read it as condemnation (so Gideon was faithless). Both are adding a sentence the narrator didn't write. The honest reading holds the silence as silence: the story describes doubt met with patience, and leaves you to weigh the practice against the rest of Scripture, which is exactly what principle 6 tells you to do.

So may a Christian ask God for a sign? That's a question the story doesn't settle, and I won't pretend it does. What the story does settle is that Gideon is not a model for how to get guidance you don't yet have. He's a picture of a man who already had it and struggled to trust it, and of a God who bore with him. That is a story worth having, and it's a different story from the one on the bookmark.

:::checkpoint In one sentence each: what does Judges 6:36-40 describe, and what does it prescribe?
It describes a frightened man asking God twice to confirm a promise God had already made, and God granting both requests without comment. It prescribes nothing; the passage contains no command and no evaluation, and any judgement about the practice has to come from elsewhere in Scripture.
:::

## What people get wrong

**"A literal reading is the faithful reading."** This is the biggest one, and it's held by people on both sides of the argument: some who think they're being faithful by ignoring genre, and some who think genre-first reading is a liberal invention. Neither is right. The Chicago Statement on Biblical Inerrancy (1978), the reference statement for the strictest evangelical view of Scripture, affirms in Article XVIII that the text is to be interpreted "taking account of its literary forms and devices", and its exposition says outright: "So history must be treated as history, poetry as poetry, hyperbole and metaphor as hyperbole and metaphor, generalization and approximation as what they are, and so forth."[4] Reading a text by its genre is what the most conservative statement of the doctrine requires. The question is never literal or not. It's what kind of writing, and therefore what "literal" means here.

**"Everyone in the story is a role model."** Principle 4: frequently the opposite. Abraham lies about his wife, twice. Jacob cheats his brother and his father. David commits adultery and murder. Samson breaks every vow he's under. The stories don't hide any of this, and they rarely stop to say "don't do this". They expect you to know. If you go into an Old Testament story looking for a hero to copy, you'll either be disappointed or, worse, you'll copy the wrong thing.

**"If the Bible records it, the Bible recommends it."** This is the Gideon error in general form, and once you can see it you'll see it everywhere: polygamy in Genesis, casting lots in Acts, Jephthah's vow. Fee and Stuart list it among the standard misreadings of narrative, alongside allegorising (looking for the meaning behind the plain one), decontextualising (ignoring the setting), moralising (assuming every story has a lesson to lift), and personalising (reading the story as if it were written about you).[2] The test is simple: is there a command anywhere, or a verdict from the narrator? If not, you're reading a report.

**"Genesis 1 must be read as one particular genre."** You'll hear that Genesis 1 is obviously plain history, and you'll hear that it's obviously a poem. Both statements skip the question this lesson is about. Christians who hold the chapter to be entirely true, and who hold to the Chicago Statement, read its genre differently: as a straightforward chronological account, as a structured literary account of a real creation, as a temple text, and in other ways. Which it is remains a live argument among Christians, and this course points you to *Faith and Science* for it. All we say here is that the genre question comes first, it's real, and it's not settled by saying "literal".

## Where the course stands

Let me say plainly which kind of claim each part of this lesson is.

*Scholars establish* the genre facts: that the Old Testament is mostly narrative, that Hebrew narrators evaluate rarely and explicitly, and that stories like Gideon's report without judging. Jewish and critical scholars read 1 Samuel 17 and Judges 6 with the same tools; Robert Alter, whose *The Art of Biblical Narrative* taught a generation of readers to see the narrator's reticence and the meaning in small repeated details, is a Hebrew scholar writing from outside the Christian faith, and much of what this lesson teaches about narrative craft he would recognise.[10] Christine Hayes's Yale lectures on the books from Joshua to Kings teach the same narrative art without asking anyone to believe it.[11]

*Scholars argue about* the historicity of the events in these stories, and lesson 10 and *Hard Questions* take that up. Nothing in this lesson depends on it: the reading rules are the same whether you take 1 Samuel 17 as a record of a real fight, which we do, or as a literary composition, which a critical reader might.

*We hold by faith* that God is the hero of all these stories in more than a literary sense: that the LORD who says "the battle is the LORD's" was, and is, real, and that the plan of God on the top level is what the whole Bible is about. A Jewish reader reads 1 Samuel 17 as a story in the Tanakh about the God of Israel and Israel's king, and would say much of what we've said about the middle level and the narrator's craft; where we differ is in where we think the top level ends, and lesson 2 said why.

## Practice

:::exercise Do it now: Jacob and Esau, three ways
Read Genesis 25:19-34 and Genesis 27. About fifteen minutes.

1. Write the story in three lines, one for each level. Bottom level: what happens between two brothers and their parents. Middle level: what this means for the line of Abraham and the promise. Top level: what God is doing (start from the oracle in 25:23, which is the one place God speaks).
2. Go through both passages and mark two things. Put a tick beside every sentence where the narrator, or God, evaluates a character or an action in his own voice. Put a dash beside every sentence that merely reports. Count both. You'll find the dashes outnumber the ticks by a wide margin, and you may find the ticks are hard to locate at all.
3. Now write a paragraph on what a reader gets wrong by treating Jacob as a model. Be specific: name the act they'd end up copying, and say which principle stops them.
4. Last question, the hard one: God's promise in 25:23 comes true through Jacob's deception. Does the story therefore approve the deception? Answer in two sentences, using principle 6, and say where else in Scripture you would look for the verdict.
:::

When you're done, close the page and, on paper, write out the basic rule from memory, the three levels, and as many of the ten principles as you can. Then check. That's the retrieval practice that will make this stick; rereading the list won't.

## Connections

Lesson 2 gave you the top level of narrative as a map of the whole Bible. This lesson has taken one chapter and read it on all three levels at once, which is what the map is for. Lesson 1's warning about chapter divisions is worth remembering too: 1 Samuel 17 only makes full sense with chapter 16 read first, because the narrator set up Saul's rejection and David's anointing before the giant ever appeared.

Every lesson from here to lesson 7 uses the method you've just learned: identify the kind of writing, learn its rules, then read. Lesson 4 turns to law and prophecy, where the rules are very different, and where the basic rule does even harder work. Lesson 6 applies today's narrative principles to the Gospels and Acts, where the temptation to turn description into prescription is strongest of all, because it's the early church being described.

## Go deeper

- **Fee and Stuart, *How to Read the Bible for All Its Worth*, 4th ed., chapter 5, "The Old Testament Narratives: Their Proper Use".** The full statement of the ten principles and the list of common errors, with their own worked examples, including Ruth and Joseph. About twenty pages.[2]
- **Robert Alter, *The Art of Biblical Narrative*, revised edition (Basic Books, 2011).** The book that taught many readers to see how Hebrew narrative works: the narrator's reticence, the meaning carried by repeated words, the "type scene". Written by a Jewish literary scholar; you'll disagree with him in places and read the stories better for it.[10]
- **BibleProject, "Character in Biblical Narrative" and "Setting in Biblical Narrative".** The two companion videos to the plot video above, each about five minutes, on why the Bible's characters are so rarely explained and why place names carry weight.[8]
- **Christine Hayes, Yale RLST 145, the lectures on the Deuteronomistic History (Joshua through Kings).** The critical reading of the same books, taught by a scholar who wants you to see the narrative art and does not ask you to believe. Free, with transcripts.[11]
