Two verses, one after the other, in Leviticus 19. Here they are in the ESV.[1]

> **18** You shall not take vengeance or bear a grudge against the sons of your own people, but you shall love your neighbor as yourself: I am the LORD.
>
> **19** You shall keep my statutes. You shall not let your cattle breed with a different kind. You shall not sow your field with two kinds of seed, nor shall you wear a garment of cloth made of two kinds of material.

Jesus called the first of these the second greatest commandment. Paul said the whole law is summed up in it. You probably own a cotton-polyester shirt that breaks the second.

Most people handle this by instinct. They keep verse 18 because it sounds like God and drop verse 19 because it sounds like an ancient farming manual, and they don't ask what rule they just used. That instinct is the problem this lesson is about, because the same instinct, applied to a different pair of verses, is how people end up quoting Leviticus at their neighbours while eating shrimp, and how their neighbours end up saying the whole book is nonsense.

There is a better way to read these verses, and it starts with a question that sounds too simple: what kind of document is the law in?

The answer to that question turns out to answer a second one that looks unrelated. Why do the prophets, who take up so much of the Old Testament, sound the way they do? Once you can see law and prophets as two halves of one thing, both become readable. That is the whole lesson.

## What the law is

Open Exodus 19. Israel has just come out of Egypt and is camped at Sinai. Before a single law is given, God says what the relationship is: he carried them out, and if they keep his covenant they will be his treasured people among the nations (19:4 to 6). Then the people say yes. Then, and only then, come the commandments.

That order matters. The commandments of Exodus 20 to 23, and the much longer body of law in Leviticus, Numbers, and Deuteronomy, are not a free-floating code of ethics dropped from the sky. They are the terms of an agreement between two named parties. Fee and Stuart's chapter on the law is titled "Covenant Stipulations for Israel", and that phrase is the most useful thing you can carry into any page of Leviticus.[2]

:::predict Before we count them: on the traditional Jewish count, how many commandments are in the Torah? Guess a number.
The traditional count is 613. The number comes from a saying of Rabbi Simlai preserved in the Talmud (Makkot 23b): 365 prohibitions, one for each day of the solar year, and 248 positive commands, one for each of the limbs of the body as they were then counted.[3] Maimonides gave the classic list in his *Sefer HaMitzvot*, the Book of Commandments.[4] It is a Jewish count, made by people who loved the law, and the arithmetic itself is a small sermon: the whole year and the whole body, every day and every part of you, belong inside the covenant.
:::

### The shape of a treaty

Here is where the ancient world helps. Scholars in the 1950s, George Mendenhall first among them, noticed that the Sinai covenant is laid out like the treaties Hittite kings made with their vassals between about 1500 and 1200 BC.[5][6] Christine Hayes, teaching the Hebrew Bible at Yale, walks through the form in her lecture on Exodus. The treaties have six parts, and her wording is worth keeping.[6]

1. A preamble: "The suzerain identifies himself."
2. "Some kind of historical prologue": what the great king has done for the vassal.
3. "Some sort of set of stipulations and requirements, upon the vassal generally."
4. Arrangements "for the publication of the treaty, or its deposition, its safe-keeping in some sort of shrine."
5. "A concluding invocation of witnesses, usually the gods."
6. "A list of blessings for the party who obeys, and curses for the party that violates the pact."

Now watch the Bible do the same things. Exodus 20 opens with the LORD naming himself and what he did: he brought them out of Egypt (preamble and prologue in one sentence). The commandments follow (stipulations). Deuteronomy 31 has Moses write the law down and hand it to the priests to be read aloud to all Israel every seven years (deposit and publication). Deuteronomy 30:19, in the ESV: "I call heaven and earth to witness against you today, that I have set before you life and death, blessing and curse. Therefore choose life, that you and your offspring may live."[7] There are the witnesses, and there is the blessing and curse. Deuteronomy 28 spells that last part out at length, and we'll come back to it, because the prophets never stopped reading it.

Hayes is careful, and so should we be: "no one passage contains all of the elements of the Hittite treaty form", but "there are enough of them scattered around to suggest it as a model."[6] That is the honest strength of the claim. The form is not exact. It is recognisable.

The Hittite version of the treaty between the Hittite king Hattusili III and the Egyptian pharaoh Ramesses II, concluded around 1259 BC and found at the Hittite capital, Hattusa. This one is a treaty between equals, not a suzerain and a vassal, but it shows the kind of document Israel's neighbours drew up: a written agreement, sworn before the gods, with terms and sanctions. Museum of the Ancient Orient, Istanbul Archaeology Museums. Photo: Iocanus, Wikimedia Commons, CC BY 3.0.[8]
:::

Why does the form matter for reading? Because a stipulation only means something inside its treaty. Take the parapet law in Deuteronomy 22:8, or the two-kinds-of-cloth law in Leviticus 19:19, out of the covenant and you have a strange rule. Put it back in and you have a term in a relationship: this is how the people the LORD carried out of Egypt were to live in his land, so that they would look like his people and not like the nations around them. Whether it applies to you is a separate question, and we're about to reach it. But you can't ask that question well until you know that the law is not a list of timeless rules. It is the terms of a covenant made with Israel at Sinai.

:::checkpoint Judges 2:1 to 3: the angel of the LORD reminds Israel that he brought them up from Egypt and swore to give them the land, then charges them with breaking the covenant, then announces that the nations will remain as a snare. Which parts of the treaty form can you see in those three verses?
Prologue (what the LORD did: brought them up, swore the land), stipulation (the command not to make a covenant with the land's inhabitants, which they broke), and sanction (the nations left as thorns). No witnesses named, no deposit clause; as Hayes says, the parts are scattered, not all present in every passage. What you should notice is that the speaker treats the Sinai terms as still in force and Israel as still bound. That is the frame every prophet will use.
:::

## How a Christian reads it now

So the law is the terms of a covenant made with Israel. You are (probably) not an Israelite at Sinai. What, then, is the law to you?

Christians have never had one answer to that, and I'd rather show you the real range than hand you one view as if the others didn't exist. What every serious answer agrees on is that the question is real: Jesus said "Do not think that I have come to abolish the Law or the Prophets; I have not come to abolish them but to fulfill them" (Matthew 5:17, ESV),[9] and the same New Testament says, in a parenthesis in Mark 7:19, that "he declared all foods clean".[9] Any account of the law has to hold both sentences.

### Proposal 1: three kinds of law

The oldest and most widespread Christian tool is to sort the law into three kinds. Thomas Aquinas set it out in the thirteenth century: "We must therefore distinguish three kinds of precept in the Old Law; viz. 'moral' precepts... 'ceremonial' precepts... and 'judicial' precepts, which are determinations of the justice to be maintained among men."[10] The Westminster Confession of 1646, the standard of Presbyterian churches, uses the same three drawers. The moral law (the Ten Commandments) binds everyone always. The ceremonial laws, which prefigured Christ, are, in the Confession's words, "now abrogated under the New Testament". The judicial laws given to Israel "as a body politic" expired with that state, and bind others only so far as their "general equity" still applies.[11]

This is a strong tool and most Christians use some version of it without knowing its name. Its weakness is one its defenders admit: the text of Leviticus doesn't come with those labels. Leviticus 19 mixes "love your neighbour" with "don't mix your cloth" in adjacent verses and calls both "my statutes". The sorting is something the reader brings, on principle, and the principle has to be argued.

### Proposal 2: what the new covenant renews

Fee and Stuart take a different route.[2] The old covenant, they say, was made with Israel, and it is not the covenant Christians are under; Jeremiah 31 promised a new one, and the New Testament says it has come. So the working test is: has this stipulation been renewed in the new covenant? Where it has (love of neighbour: Galatians 5:14 quotes Leviticus 19:18 and calls it the whole law fulfilled in one word),[9] it binds as command. Where it has not (the food laws: Mark 7:19), it does not bind as command. And then the half people forget: all of it is still God's word to us. Nothing in the Old Testament is deleted. It is all Scripture, all teaching, all evidence of who God is and what he cares about; it is just not all commandment to us.

This proposal is cleaner than the first, and its weakness is the mirror image. To run the test you need the whole New Testament in your head, and there are laws the New Testament never mentions at all. What does "renewed" mean for the parapet in Deuteronomy 22:8? Fee and Stuart would say: the command isn't renewed, the concern behind it is.

### Behind both: two frameworks and the space between

Under the two proposals sit two larger ways of relating the whole Old Testament to the whole New, and they produce different reading habits. I'll give each in its own terms, because a person who holds it should recognise it.[12]

Covenant theology reads the Bible as one covenant of grace unfolding through history. The New Testament interprets the Old; promises to Israel find their fulfilment in Christ and in the church, which is the Israel of God; the law is one administration of that single covenant, and its moral core continues. The Westminster Confession is this tradition's classic statement.

Dispensationalism reads Israel and the church as distinct peoples in God's plan. Charles Ryrie's *Dispensationalism* (revised 1995) names its three essentials: a consistent distinction between Israel and the church, a consistently plain or literal reading of Scripture, and the glory of God as the purpose of history.[13] On this reading, Old Testament promises made to Israel keep a literal future referent for Israel; the New Testament adds, but does not change what the Old Testament texts meant.

Between them sit two newer positions. Progressive covenantalism (Peter Gentry and Stephen Wellum, *Kingdom through Covenant*, 2012) argues that each covenant builds on the last and all find their end in Christ, without either a single covenant of grace or a permanent Israel-church distinction.[14] Progressive dispensationalism (Craig Blaising and Darrell Bock, *Progressive Dispensationalism*, 1993) keeps a future for national Israel while allowing that the church already shares in the new covenant blessings now.[15]

Where does this bite? Lesson 7 will show you the standing example, the temple of Ezekiel 40 to 48: a dispensational reader expects that building to stand in a future age; a covenant reader sees it fulfilled in Christ and his people. Both readers are working from the same chapters with the same seriousness.

We hold a view, and the course says so once here. We read the law the way Fee and Stuart do, as the terms of a covenant that is not ours, all of it God's word and none of it deleted, with the threefold division as an older tool we respect and sometimes use. Within the larger frameworks we sit nearer covenant theology than dispensationalism. A Christian may reasonably hold any of the four.

### The Jewish reading: the law is the gift

One more reading, and it is not a Christian one. For a Jewish reader the law is not a problem to be sorted. It is the heart of the covenant and the gift at the centre of it. That is what the 613 are about: Rabbi Simlai's count is not a burden tallied but a treasure inventoried, and Maimonides' list is an act of devotion.[3][4] Psalm 19 and Psalm 119 are Israel's own voice on the matter, and they sound like someone in love. When a Jewish reader hears a Christian ask "which of these do we still have to keep?", they hear the wrong question being asked about the right book. This course says plainly: the Tanakh is a living Scripture read on its own terms by Jews today, and on those terms the Torah is not a stage to be passed through but the way of life the covenant gives. We do not share that reading. We want you to be able to state it so that a Jewish reader would say, yes, that is what we believe.

## Worked example 1: Leviticus 19:18 and 19:19

Back to the two verses. Let me run each proposal, honestly, including where it strains.

**The threefold division.** Verse 18 is moral: it is the second table of the Ten Commandments summed up, and Jesus repeats it, so it binds always. Verse 19 is ceremonial. The separation of kinds (animals, seeds, cloth) belongs with the laws that marked Israel off as holy, and those laws pointed to a holiness Christ has brought, so they are abrogated. Where it strains: the text gives no label. You have to decide that "don't mix cloth" is ceremonial, and the reasoning is that it looks like the purity laws rather than like the moral ones. The reasoning is good. It is still reasoning, not reading.

**Fee and Stuart's covenant test.** Verse 18: renewed. Galatians 5:14, Romans 13:9, Matthew 22:39, James 2:8 all repeat it as command. It binds.

Now do verse 19 yourself before reading on. Is the law of mixed kinds renewed anywhere in the New Testament? And if it isn't, what does Fee and Stuart's second half say about it?

Here's my answer. It isn't renewed; no New Testament writer restates it, and the general move in Mark 7 and Acts 10 and 15 is away from the boundary-marking laws. So it does not bind as a command. But it remains God's word: it tells you that Israel's God cared about his people being visibly distinct, down to their clothes and their fields, and the New Testament renews that concern in a different form (1 Peter 2:9 calls the church a holy nation). So the second verse still teaches, even though it no longer commands.

**The Jewish reading.** Both verses are commandments; both are kept. The question of sorting does not arise. What arises instead is study of how to keep them, which is what the Talmud's tractates are.

Notice what happened. Three readings, three different answers, and none of them was "keep the one that sounds nice". Every one had a stated principle you could argue with. That is the difference between reading the law and rummaging in it.

## What a prophet was

Now the other half.

The prophetic books take up a large part of the Old Testament, and for most readers they are the hardest part, because they arrive with an expectation attached: a prophet is someone who predicts the future. Hold that expectation and Amos is baffling. Almost none of it looks like prediction, and what does look like prediction seems to be about Assyria.

Drop the expectation and start from the covenant. The prophets are the people God sent to enforce it. Hayes puts the basic distinction well: a prophet is someone "sent by God with a message, not someone who is consulted by a client to find out what God thinks."[16] The message, over and over, is the covenant's own terms read back to the people who agreed to them. Fee and Stuart's chapter title says it in three words: "Enforcing the Covenant in Israel".[2]

Three things follow that change how you read.

**The prophets spoke; the books are collections.** Amos did not sit down and write the book of Amos. He delivered oracles, spoken messages, over a period of years, and the book gathers them. Hayes calls the prophetic books "anthologies of oracles",[16] and Fee and Stuart's instruction for the prophets is two words in capitals: "THINK ORACLES".[2] Read a prophetic book as a continuous argument and you'll be lost by chapter 3. Read it as a collection of separate speeches, each with its own occasion, and it opens.

**They spoke to their own generation.** Amos 1:1 dates him: in the reign of Uzziah in Judah and Jeroboam in Israel, which puts him in the eighth century BC, speaking to the northern kingdom.[9] His hearers were alive. The charges concern their markets, their courts, their worship. Whatever else an oracle does, it did something first for the people standing in front of the prophet.

**The sanctions were already written.** Deuteronomy 28 is a long list of what happens to a covenant-keeping Israel (verses 1 to 14) and a much longer list of what happens to a covenant-breaking one (verses 15 to 68). Every prophet had it, or the tradition behind it, in his bones. So when a prophet says drought is coming, he is not looking into a crystal ball. He is reading the treaty's penalty clause aloud.

:::predict Fee and Stuart give an estimate of how much Old Testament prophecy is about the Messiah, how much is about the age the New Testament describes, and how much is about events still future to us. Before the figures: what would you guess for each?
Their estimate, in their own words: "Less than 2 percent of Old Testament prophecy is messianic. Less than 5 percent specifically describes the new-covenant age. Less than 1 percent concerns events yet to come in our time."[2] They give no source for the figures and neither will I; treat them as an experienced reader's rough count rather than a measurement. But even if the real numbers were double, the picture would stand. More than nine-tenths of what the prophets said concerned their own hearers and their own century.
:::

## Worked example 2: Amos with Deuteronomy 28 open

Here is the mechanism, and it's the part I most want you to see for yourself.

:::predict Deuteronomy 28:15 to 24 lists what will happen to an Israel that breaks the covenant. Before reading the list, write down four things you'd expect an ancient treaty's curse section to threaten a farming nation with.
Most people get drought and defeat in war. Fewer think of crop disease, and almost nobody thinks of the specific pairing of blight and mildew, or of locusts. The list below has all of them.
:::

Deuteronomy 28:15, in the ESV: "But if you will not obey the voice of the LORD your God or be careful to do all his commandments and his statutes that I command you today, then all these curses shall come upon you and overtake you."[7] What follows, quoted here from the NET Bible, includes these:[17]

- 28:21: "The Lord will plague you with deadly diseases until he has completely removed you from the land"
- 28:22: "He will afflict you with weakness, fever, inflammation, infection, sword, blight, and mildew"
- 28:23 to 24: "The sky above your heads will be bronze and the earth beneath you iron. The Lord will make the rain of your land powder and dust"
- 28:38: "You will take much seed to the field but gather little harvest, because locusts will consume it"
- 28:41: "You will bear sons and daughters but not keep them, because they will be taken into captivity"
- 28:42: "Whirring locusts will take over every tree and all the produce of your soil"

Now Amos 4:6 to 11. The oracle is a list of things that have already happened to Israel, each closed with the same refrain. In the ESV, verse 6 begins: "I gave you cleanness of teeth in all your cities, and lack of bread in all your places, yet you did not return to me," declares the LORD.[7] (Cleanness of teeth means nothing to chew. Lesson 11 uses this verse for a different reason.) Then, from the NET:[17]

- 4:7: "I withheld rain from you three months before the harvest"
- 4:9: "I destroyed your crops with blight and disease. Locusts kept devouring your orchards, vineyards, fig trees, and olive trees"
- 4:10: "I sent against you a plague like one of the Egyptian plagues. I killed your young men with the sword"
- 4:11: "I overthrew some of you the way God overthrew Sodom and Gomorrah"

And after each: "Still you did not come back to me."

Lay them side by side.

Every item in Amos's list is in Deuteronomy's. And the one item in Deuteronomy's list that is not yet in Amos's, captivity, is exactly what Amos 4:12 turns to: "Therefore this is what I will do to you, Israel... prepare to meet your God".[17] The northern kingdom went into Assyrian exile a few decades later. That is what people mean when they say Amos predicted it. What he was doing, in his own terms, was pointing at the last line of a contract his hearers had signed.

Now the oracle everyone quotes. Amos 5:21 to 24, ESV:[1]

> **21** "I hate, I despise your feasts, and I take no delight in your solemn assemblies.
> **22** Even though you offer me your burnt offerings and grain offerings, I will not accept them; and the peace offerings of your fattened animals, I will not look upon them.
> **23** Take away from me the noise of your songs; to the melody of your harps I will not listen.
> **24** But let justice roll down like waters, and righteousness like an ever-flowing stream."

Read badly, this is a prediction: God is announcing that one day sacrifice and festival will be abolished and replaced by ethics, and the reader who wants to may hear Christianity or modern secular justice in it. But Amos's hearers were not being told about a future. They were being sued. The feasts and offerings are the feasts and offerings the covenant itself commanded (Leviticus 23 lists them), and the charge is that Israel is keeping the ritual clauses while breaking the ones about the poor, the courts, and honest weights (Amos 2:6 to 8; 5:10 to 12; 8:4 to 6). Hayes reads it as "an attack on empty piety" offered "in accompaniment to social injustice".[16] The form is a lawsuit, what scholars call the *riv* (the Hebrew word for a legal dispute), and Hayes flags the form in Hosea and Isaiah as well: "he's now the first to couch his charge in the form of this formal *riv*, or lawsuit."[18] Summons, charge, evidence, verdict. Verse 24 is the verdict's demand, not a forecast.

Does the oracle say anything beyond its century? Yes, and the New Testament takes up the pattern (Jesus quotes Hosea 6:6, "I desire mercy, and not sacrifice", twice in Matthew). But you can only see what carries forward once you have seen what it said first. Reading order: near horizon, then far.

:::checkpoint Hosea 4:1 to 3, ESV: "Hear the word of the LORD, O children of Israel, for the LORD has a controversy with the inhabitants of the land. There is no faithfulness or steadfast love, and no knowledge of God in the land; there is swearing, lying, murder, stealing, and committing adultery; they break all bounds, and bloodshed follows bloodshed. Therefore the land mourns, and all who dwell in it languish, and also the beasts of the field and the birds of the heavens, and even the fish of the sea are taken away."[1] What is the charge, which covenant is being enforced, and what is the sanction?
The word the ESV renders "controversy" is the *riv*, the lawsuit. The charge is a list of broken stipulations, and you can hear the Ten Commandments in it: swearing (the name), lying, murder, stealing, adultery. The covenant is Sinai's. The sanction is the land itself failing, which is Deuteronomy 28's language of a cursed field and a sky of bronze, extended to the animals. Note the shape: summons ("Hear"), charge, sanction. That is the lawsuit form, and once you can see it you will find it in most of the prophets.

## Two horizons

Here is the rule we've been using, stated plainly. Every prophetic oracle has a near horizon: what it meant to the people who first heard it, in their own decade, with their own kings and enemies and harvests. Some oracles also have a far horizon: a fulfilment beyond that generation, which the New Testament writers find in Christ and his people. The near horizon is always there and is always read first. The far horizon is sometimes there, is never the only meaning, and the New Testament is the Christian's guide to where it is.

The whole art of reading the prophets is keeping both horizons in view and not letting either swallow the other. Let the far horizon swallow the near and you get "prophecy means prediction", with the prophet reduced to a man who did not know what he was saying. Let the near swallow the far and you get a Bible in which Matthew's Gospel is simply wrong to quote Isaiah. The next example is the hardest case in the book, and it's where a reader in either tradition should be able to recognise their own view.

## Worked example 3: Isaiah 7:14

First the near horizon, and you'll be surprised how much of it everyone agrees on.

**The situation.** Isaiah 7:1 dates the oracle: Ahaz is king of Judah; Rezin of Syria and Pekah of Israel have marched on Jerusalem.[19] The year is about 734 BC. Assyria under Tiglath-Pileser is pressing on the whole region, Syria and Israel have allied against it, and, in Hayes's summary, "Judah refused to join the alliance... So in anger, Aram and Israel moved south and lay siege to Jerusalem."[18] Ahaz is terrified (7:2). Isaiah is sent to him with his own son, whose name Shear-jashub means "a remnant will return", and the message, in Hayes's paraphrase of 7:4, is "be quiet and do not fear."[18] The two kings, God says, will fail (7:7). Then the line that turns the chapter: "If you are not firm in faith, you will not be firm at all" (7:9, ESV).[19]

Ahaz is offered a sign and refuses one. So God gives one anyway. ESV:[1]

> **14** Therefore the Lord himself will give you a sign. Behold, the virgin shall conceive and bear a son, and shall call his name Immanuel.
>
> **16** For before the boy knows how to refuse the evil and choose the good, the land whose two kings you dread will be deserted.

(The ESV's footnote: *Immanuel* means *God is with us*.)

**What the sign was for Ahaz.** Read verse 16 slowly, because it fixes the near horizon. Before a child born now is old enough to know right from wrong, the two kingdoms Ahaz fears will be empty. That is a sign for Ahaz, in Ahaz's lifetime, about Ahaz's enemies. Isaiah 8 continues in the same key: the prophet fathers a son with another portentous name, Maher-shalal-hash-baz, and before that child can say "my father", Damascus and Samaria will be plundered (8:3 to 4).[19] The name Immanuel comes back at 8:8 and 8:10 as the reason Judah will survive, and 8:18 has Isaiah say, "Behold, I and the children whom the LORD has given me are signs and portents in Israel."[19] History establishes this much: the oracle was spoken into a specific crisis, it promised something Ahaz would see, and the children with the strange names were the signs.

**The word.** The Hebrew word for the mother is *almah*. The Jewish Publication Society's Tanakh translates the verse: "Look, the young woman is with child and about to give birth to a son. Let her name him Immanuel."[20] Note the present tense: she is already pregnant. The Greek translation of Isaiah made by Jewish scholars in the centuries before Jesus, the Septuagint, has *parthenos*, which in Greek normally means a virgin, and a future tense: "behold, the virgin shall conceive in the womb, and shall bring forth a son."[21] Matthew 1:23 quotes the Greek: "Behold, the virgin shall conceive and bear a son, and they shall call his name Immanuel" (which means, God with us).[1]

Now the two readings, each in its own words.

### The Jewish reading

I'll let the classic Jewish commentators speak, since they are on Sefaria for anyone to check. Rashi (eleventh century, France), on "the young woman": "My wife will conceive this year. This was the fourth year of Ahaz."[22] The mother is Isaiah's own wife; the child is born in Ahaz's reign; the sign is that a girl who has never prophesied will be moved to give the child a prophetic name.

Ibn Ezra (twelfth century, Spain) is blunter: "It is to me a matter of surprise that there are those who say the prophet here refers to Jesus, since the sign was given to Ahaz, and Jesus was born many years afterwards."[23] He too takes the child to be Isaiah's son, one of the children of 8:18, and takes the sign to be what the child would live through.

Radak (David Kimhi, twelfth to thirteenth century, Provence) reads *almah* as a word for a young woman who may or may not be a virgin, and takes the mother to be Ahaz's own wife, so that the child is a royal son.[24]

Three medieval Jewish readers, three candidate children, one shared point: the sign is for Ahaz, in Ahaz's day, and the Hebrew word does not say "virgin". This is not a modern objection. Justin Martyr's *Dialogue with Trypho*, written in the second century, has his Jewish interlocutor say it: "The Scripture has not, 'Behold, the virgin shall conceive, and bear a son,' but, 'Behold, the young woman shall conceive, and bear a son.'"[25] The Jewish reading has been stated, in those terms, for as long as there have been Christians to state it to. Hayes, teaching from the critical side, calls the Greek "a mistranslation of the word 'young woman' as 'virgin'."[18]

A Jewish reader would add: Immanuel, "God is with us", is fulfilled in the survival of Judah in 8:10, and the chapter needs no second fulfilment to make sense.

### The Christian reading

We hold, with Matthew, that the verse also speaks of Jesus. Here is the case as we would make it.

First, the near horizon is granted. Ahaz got a sign in his own lifetime; the apostles never denied it, and a Christian reading that denies it is reading badly. Second, the Septuagint's *parthenos* is not a Christian invention; it is what Jewish translators chose before there were Christians, and Matthew quotes what he had. Third, the name and the chapter reach beyond one child: Immanuel is invoked at 8:8 over the whole land, and Isaiah 9 promises a child on David's throne whose names are titles no eighth-century prince bore. Fourth, and this is the heart of it, the apostles read the Old Testament with a pattern: what God did once, in part, he does again, in full. They found the fuller sense in Isaiah 7 because they had met Jesus and read backwards. That is a faith reading. It is not what the Hebrew obviously means, and we should stop saying it is.

So here is the sort. History establishes the crisis of 734, the near-horizon sign, the Hebrew *almah* and the Greek *parthenos*, and the fact that both readings are ancient. Scholars argue over which child Isaiah meant and whether the Septuagint's word choice reflects an older Jewish reading or a looser one. We hold by faith that Matthew read the verse truly, that the sign to Ahaz was the near horizon of a promise whose far horizon is the virgin birth of the one who is God with us. A Jewish reader should be able to read this section and say, yes, that is our reading and our reasons. A Christian should be able to say the same. If either can't, tell us.

:::checkpoint A friend says, "The Septuagint proves that Jews before Jesus already read Isaiah 7:14 as a virgin birth." Sort that sentence. Which part is established, which part is argued, and what word is doing too much work?
Established: the Greek translators, who were Jewish and worked before Jesus was born, wrote *parthenos*. Argued: what they meant by it. *Parthenos* usually means a virgin, but scholars disagree about whether the translators were making a claim about the mother or simply choosing the nearest Greek word for a young unmarried woman; Hayes calls it a mistranslation, and some Christian scholars think it preserves an older reading. "Proves" is the word doing too much work. The Greek is real evidence for the Christian reading and we use it; it is not a proof that settles what a Jewish reader in 150 BC believed.
:::

:::callout Isaiah 53, briefly
The same discipline applies to the servant song of Isaiah 52:13 to 53:12, the passage Christians most often call "obviously" about Jesus. Rashi reads the servant as Israel, and he gives a textual reason: the prophet "characterizes all Israel as a single entity", as in 44:2 ("Fear not, My servant Jacob") and 52:13, which he takes to refer to the house of Jacob.[26] Isaiah does call Israel "my servant" repeatedly in chapters 41 to 49, so this is a reading with roots in the text, not a dodge. And Jewish tradition is not one voice: the Aramaic Targum Jonathan renders 52:13 "Behold, my servant the messiah shall prosper".[27] We hold that the servant is Christ, as the apostles read it. We do not hold that only a hostile reader could miss it.
:::

## What people get wrong

**"Prophecy means prediction."** Fee and Stuart's estimate is that under two percent of it is messianic and under one percent is about events still ahead of us.[2] The prophets announced what was coming, often and specifically. But they announced it as the covenant's sanction on the people in front of them. Prediction is the tool; enforcement is the job.

**"The law is abolished, so the Old Testament is irrelevant."** Marcion said something like this in the second century and the church said no; lesson 2 covered it. Jesus said he had not come to abolish the Law.[9] Fee and Stuart's second half is the corrective: all of it remains God's word even where none of it remains our command.

**"Christians are inconsistent for eating shellfish while quoting Leviticus 18."** The honest answer is that every Christian tradition has a principled way of sorting, and you have now seen three. You can disagree with the principle. You cannot say there isn't one. The sharper form of the objection, that the sorting is done to keep the laws people already wanted to keep, is a fair challenge to any particular reader, and the answer is to show your principle and apply it where it costs you.

**"The prophets wrote books."** They spoke oracles. The books are collections, and reading them as continuous treatises is why Isaiah 7 seems to change subject every ten verses. Think oracles.

**"Isaiah 53 is obviously about Jesus."** We hold that it is about Jesus. "Obviously" is a claim about the Hebrew that the Hebrew doesn't support on its own; Jewish tradition reads the servant as Israel with reasons from the text, and part of that same tradition reads him as the Messiah.[26][27] Say what you hold and why, not that everyone who differs is reading in bad faith.

**"The near horizon is the only horizon."** This is the mirror error, and a Christian should be able to see it in a critical scholar's reading. If the apostles' fuller sense is ruled out in advance, then Matthew is not interpreting Isaiah but misquoting him, and the whole New Testament use of the Old is an error. We think it is a reading, held by faith, with a pattern behind it. Say that, and you have said what history can establish and what it can't.
