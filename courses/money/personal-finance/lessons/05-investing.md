Investing sounds like picking winning stocks. For almost everyone, it isn't. It's owning a small slice of the whole economy, for a long time, at low cost, and not panicking.

## Stocks, bonds, funds

-   A **stock** is a share of ownership in one company. High potential return, high risk for any single company.
-   A **bond** is a loan to a company or government that pays you interest. Lower return, more stable.
-   A **fund** pools money from many people to buy many investments at once. One purchase, instant diversification.

## Index funds

An **index fund** simply buys every company in an index, such as the largest 500 in a country, in proportion. No manager picks stocks. Because of that, fees are tiny, often under 0.1% a year.

Decades of evidence show that most professional fund managers fail to beat the index over long periods, especially after their fees. Buying the index means you get the market's return minus almost nothing.

> "Don't look for the needle in the haystack. Just buy the haystack." — John Bogle, founder of Vanguard

## Why fees matter so much

A 1% annual fee sounds small. Over 30 years at 7% growth it consumes roughly a quarter of your final balance. Fees compound too, against you.

## Risk and time

Markets drop 10% most years and 30% or more every decade or so. That's normal, not a sign to sell. Historically, the longer you hold a broad index, the smaller the chance of a loss. Over 20-year periods, broad markets have essentially always been up. Money you need soon should not be in stocks.

## Tax-advantaged accounts

Most countries offer accounts where investments grow tax-free or tax-deferred: 401(k) and IRA in the US, ISA and pension in the UK, and similar elsewhere. If your employer matches contributions, that is an instant return of 50 to 100%. Take all of it before anything else.

## A simple plan

1.  Emergency fund in place, high-interest debt gone.
2.  Capture any employer match.
3.  Open a tax-advantaged account with a low-cost provider.
4.  Buy a broad, low-fee index fund (a global or total-market fund is a common choice).
5.  Automate a monthly contribution. Ignore the news. Check once or twice a year.

## Big decisions: a framework

Rent or buy? New car or used? Pay off the mortgage early or invest? Use the same three questions each time: What does each option cost in total, over its whole life? What could that money do instead? What happens in the bad case? Write the numbers down. Big decisions made on feel are where most financial damage happens.

:::exercise Do it now
Find out whether your employer offers a retirement match and whether you're taking the full amount. Look up the expense ratio (annual fee) of any fund you already own. If it's above 0.5%, note it.
:::
