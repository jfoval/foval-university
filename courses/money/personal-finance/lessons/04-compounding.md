Compound interest is interest on interest. When you owe money it works against you. When you save and invest, it works for you, and over long periods it does most of the work.

## The maths, gently

If you have $1,000 growing at 7% a year:

| Year | Value |
| --- | --- |
| 0 | $1,000 |
| 1 | $1,070 |
| 10 | $1,967 |
| 20 | $3,870 |
| 30 | $7,612 |
| 40 | $14,974 |

Notice the last decade adds more than the first three combined. Growth is slow, then sudden. That's why *time* matters more than *amount*.

## The rule of 72

Divide 72 by the annual rate to estimate how many years it takes money to double. At 7%, about 10 years. At 3%, 24 years. At 24% (a credit card, against you), just 3 years.

## Regular contributions

Saving $200 a month at 7% for 30 years gives roughly $245,000. You contributed $72,000. The other $173,000 is compounding. Start ten years later and the total falls to about $104,000. The cost of waiting is enormous.

## Inflation: the silent tax

Prices rise, typically 2 to 3% a year. Money in a drawer loses buying power. A savings account paying 1% when inflation is 3% is losing 2% a year in real terms. That's why long-term money needs to be invested, not just saved.

## Where to keep what

-   **Money you need within 1 to 2 years:** savings account. Safety over return.
-   **Money you won't touch for 5+ years:** invested, so it can outpace inflation.
