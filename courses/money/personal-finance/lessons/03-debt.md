Debt is borrowing tomorrow's money to spend today, and paying a fee for the privilege. The fee is **interest**. Understanding how it is calculated tells you which debts are fine and which are emergencies.

## APR and how interest compounds against you

**APR** (annual percentage rate) is the yearly cost of borrowing. A card with 24% APR charges roughly 2% per month on whatever you owe. If you owe $2,000 and only pay the minimum, you pay about $40 a month in interest alone before reducing the balance by a cent.

| Debt | Typical APR | Character |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Payday loan | 300%+ | Emergency. Get out at any cost. |
| Credit card | 18–30% | Expensive. Pay off before saving beyond the starter fund. |
| Car loan | 5–12% | Moderate. Depends on terms. |
| Student loan | 3–8% | Often manageable; check the terms. |
| Mortgage | 3–7% | Usually the cheapest debt you'll ever have. |

## The minimum payment trap

Minimum payments are designed to keep you in debt. On a $5,000 card balance at 22% APR, paying only the minimum can take over 20 years and cost more in interest than the original balance. Paying even $50 above the minimum cuts that dramatically.

## Two ways to pay off multiple debts

-   **Avalanche:** pay minimums on everything, throw every spare dollar at the highest-APR debt first. Mathematically cheapest.
-   **Snowball:** same, but attack the smallest balance first. Costs a bit more but gives quick wins, which keeps many people going.

Either works. The best method is the one you will actually stick to.

## Good debt, bad debt

Debt that buys an asset likely to grow or earn (a home, an education with clear job prospects, a tool for your business) can be sensible. Debt that buys things that lose value or disappear (restaurant meals, gadgets, holidays) is almost always a bad trade.

:::callout Credit score basics
Pay every bill on time, keep card balances well below their limits, and don't open lots of accounts at once. That's most of what a good score requires.
:::
