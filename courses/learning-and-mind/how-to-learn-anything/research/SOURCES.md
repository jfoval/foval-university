# Sources: How to Learn Anything

*Stage 1 research. Written before any lesson is drafted. This is a working document: for each source, say what it is good for and what it establishes.*

Research date: September 2026. Where a figure below is quoted, it was read from the paper itself (full text or abstract) unless marked "via secondary source"; those should be verified in the fact-check pass.

## Canonical textbooks

- **Brown, Roediger & McDaniel, *Make It Stick: The Science of Successful Learning* (Harvard UP, 2014)** — the book cognitive psychologists most often hand to students; Roediger and McDaniel are two of the field's central figures. Best for: narrative framing, the "illusions of knowing" theme, and its eight-chapter structure, which is a sound lesson backbone. Chapters: 1 Learning Is Misunderstood; 2 To Learn, Retrieve; 3 Mix Up Your Practice; 4 Embrace Difficulties; 5 Avoid Illusions of Knowing; 6 Get Beyond Learning Styles; 7 Increase Your Abilities; 8 Make It Stick (tips for students, teachers, trainers). We draw on 1, 2, 4, 5 and 6 most. Caution: chapter 7 leans on growth-mindset claims that have since weakened (see Contested questions).
- **Dunlosky, Rawson, Marsh, Nathan & Willingham, "Improving Students' Learning With Effective Learning Techniques", *Psychological Science in the Public Interest* 14(1), 4–58 (2013)** — the single most important source for this course; the peer-reviewed monograph that rated ten techniques against four generalisability criteria (learning conditions, student characteristics, materials, criterion tasks) plus implementation issues. Full text read. Table 4 ratings: **High utility**: practice testing, distributed practice. **Moderate**: elaborative interrogation, self-explanation, interleaved practice (promising but under-tested in classrooms; interleaving evidence "just begun to be systematically explored"). **Low**: summarization, highlighting, keyword mnemonic, imagery for text, rereading. Key facts inside: rereading is the most common student strategy (84% of Karpicke et al.'s elite-university sample listed it; top-ranked by 55%); highlighting rated Negative for criterion tasks (can hurt inference); self-explanation and elaborative interrogation need prior knowledge to work; keyword mnemonic benefits are short-lived. Dunlosky's plain-English version, "Strengthening the Student Toolbox", *American Educator* Fall 2013, is free (ERIC EJ1021069) and should be the learner-facing link.
- **Willingham, *Why Don't Students Like School?* (Jossey-Bass, 2009; 2nd ed. 2021)** — nine chapters, each a cognitive principle with its evidence. Most useful principles for us: "memory is the residue of thought" (ch. 3: you remember what you think about, so shallow processing produces shallow memory); factual knowledge precedes skill (ch. 2); abstractions are hard because we understand new things via what we already know (ch. 4); "proficiency requires practice" (ch. 5); experts think differently from novices and novices cannot shortcut to expert habits (ch. 6); learning styles do not exist as commonly claimed (ch. 7). Second edition adds technology.
- **Ambrose, Bridges, DiPietro, Lovett & Norman, *How Learning Works: Seven Research-Based Principles for Smart Teaching* (Jossey-Bass, 2010; 2nd ed. 2023 with Mayer)** — the faculty-development standard. Seven principles: prior knowledge helps or hinders; knowledge organisation shapes use; motivation directs effort; goal-directed practice plus targeted feedback; mastery = component skills + integration + knowing when to apply; climate matters; self-directed learners monitor and adjust. Best for: the practice-and-feedback lesson and the self-monitoring lesson.
- **Oakley, *A Mind for Numbers* (Tarcher, 2014)** and **Oakley, Sejnowski & McConville, *Learning How to Learn* (2018, for ages 10–17)** — the most widely read popular treatments (the Coursera course has 4.18 million enrolments). Fifteen chapters; the ones that matter: 2 (focused vs diffuse modes), 4 (chunking and illusions of competence), 5–6 (procrastination and habit), 7 (chunking vs choking). Best for: metaphors learners already know, and the procrastination material, which the academic sources barely cover. Caution: "focused/diffuse mode" is Oakley's gloss on attention and default-mode network research, not a standard cognitive-psychology construct; present it as a useful metaphor, cite the underlying research on incubation separately, and do not treat "chunking" the way she does as equivalent to Chase & Simon's technical sense.
- **Weinstein, Sumeracki & Caviglioli, *Understanding How We Learn: A Visual Guide* (Routledge, 2019)** — the Learning Scientists' book; four parts (evidence-based education; cognitive basics; six strategies; tips for students, teachers, parents). Good for a clean six-strategy framing and for phone-friendly visual explanations to imitate.
- **Sweller, Ayres & Kalyuga, *Cognitive Load Theory* (Springer, 2011)** and **Sweller, van Merriënboer & Paas, "Cognitive Architecture and Instructional Design: 20 Years Later", *Educational Psychology Review* 31, 261–292 (2019)** — the standard statement of CLT: novel information passes through a capacity- and duration-limited working memory into an effectively unlimited long-term memory; instruction should manage intrinsic load (element interactivity) and cut extraneous load. Effects we use: worked-example effect (Sweller & Cooper 1985: algebra students who studied worked examples solved later problems in about half the time with a fifth of the errors, but the advantage did not extend to problems that varied from the studied ones), expertise-reversal effect (Kalyuga et al. 2003: guidance that helps novices becomes redundant or harmful for experts), and the 2010 redefinition of germane load as working-memory resources devoted to intrinsic load.

## University syllabi consulted

- **UC San Diego / Coursera, "Learning How to Learn" (Oakley & Sejnowski)** — four modules: (1) What is learning? focused/diffuse modes, memory basics, sleep, procrastination tools; (2) Chunking: compact information packages, illusions of competence, overlearning, interleaving; (3) Procrastination and memory: long-term memory, memory palace; (4) "Renaissance learning": metaphors, teamwork, test-preparation checklist, imposter syndrome. Notable choice: motivation and procrastination get a full quarter of the course; the academic sources give them almost nothing. We should include one lesson on it, sourced to the self-regulation literature rather than to Oakley's anecdotes.
- **Minnesota State University, Mankato, PSYC 150 "Science of Learning for College Student Success" (Lassonde)** — a credit-bearing undergraduate course built on Agarwal & Bain *Powerful Teaching*, Weinstein & Sumeracki, retrievalpractice.org and learningscientists.org; students apply strategies to their other courses with peer tutors. Notable choice: the assessment is applying the method to a live course, which is exactly the project we should set.
- **Deans for Impact, *The Science of Learning* (2015; 2nd ed. read in full)** — written with Willingham as the consensus summary for teacher-training deans. Six questions: How do students understand new ideas? How do students learn and retain new information? How do students solve problems and transfer learning? What motivates students to learn? How do students self-regulate their learning? What are common misconceptions about how students think and learn? Its misconceptions table (10% of the brain; learning styles / left-right brain; novices think like experts) is a ready checklist for our myths lesson.
- **Weinstein, Madan & Sumeracki, "Teaching the Science of Learning", *Cognitive Research: Principles and Implications* 3:2 (2018, open access)** — the peer-reviewed version of the Learning Scientists' six-strategy sequence: spaced practice, interleaving, retrieval practice, elaboration, concrete examples, dual coding. Notes that concrete examples and dual coding have far less classroom evidence than the first four, and that dual coding must not be confused with learning styles. This is the sequence most practitioner courses follow.

## Primary sources

**Retrieval practice**
- **Roediger & Karpicke, "Test-Enhanced Learning", *Psychological Science* 17, 249–255 (2006)** — the modern founding study (full text read). Experiment 1: after 5 minutes, restudying beat testing (81% vs 75%); after 2 days testing won (68% vs 54%, d = 0.95); after 1 week 56% vs 42% (d = 0.83). Experiment 2: study-test-test-test recalled 61% at one week vs 40% for study-study-study-study, despite the repeated studiers being more confident. The worked example for the retrieval lesson.
- **Karpicke & Blunt, "Retrieval Practice Produces More Learning than Elaborative Studying with Concept Mapping", *Science* 331, 772–775 (2011)** — full text read. Four conditions, 20 per cell, 276-word science text; one-week short-answer test with verbatim and inference questions. Retrieval practice M = 0.67 vs concept mapping 0.45, d = 1.50, and retrieval won even when the final test was itself a concept map. Students predicted the opposite ordering. Experiment 2 (within-subjects, N = 120): 84% did better after retrieval; 75% believed concept mapping would work better.
- **Karpicke, Butler & Roediger, "Metacognitive strategies in student learning", *Memory* 17, 471–479 (2009)** — 177 undergraduates: 84% list rereading; only 11% self-test. Kornell & Bjork (2007, *Psychonomic Bulletin & Review*): of 472 UCLA students who self-tested, only 18% did so because it teaches more; about 70% did it to check what they knew.
- **Adesope, Trevisan & Sundararajan, "Rethinking the Use of Tests", *Review of Educational Research* 87, 659–701 (2017)** — meta-analysis, overall g = 0.61 for practice testing vs all comparisons (via secondary source; verify); multiple-choice practice tests g = 0.70, short-answer 0.48.
- **Yang, Luo, Vadillo, Yu & Shanks, "Testing (quizzing) boosts classroom learning", *Psychological Bulletin* 147, 399–435 (2021)** — 222 classroom studies, 48,478 students, g = 0.499; moderated by feedback, test-format match and repetition.
- **Agarwal, Nunes & Blunt, "Retrieval Practice Consistently Benefits Student Learning", *Educational Psychology Review* 33, 1409–1453 (2021)** — full text read. 50 classroom experiments, 49 effect sizes, n = 5,374; 57% medium or large; only 6% from non-WEIRD countries. Eight implementation recommendations. Good for the "does it work in real classrooms" question.
- **Rawson & Dunlosky, "Optimizing schedules of retrieval practice", *JEP: General* 140, 283–302 (2011)** — 533 students; prescription: recall each item correctly 3 times initially, then relearn to 1 correct recall in 3 spaced sessions ("successive relearning").

**Spacing**
- **Cepeda, Pashler, Vul, Wixted & Rohrer, "Distributed practice in verbal recall tasks", *Psychological Bulletin* 132, 354–380 (2006)** — 839 assessments, 317 experiments, 184 articles; spacing reliably beats massing and the optimal gap grows with the retention interval.
- **Cepeda, Vul, Rohrer, Wixted & Pashler, "Spacing Effects in Learning: A Temporal Ridgeline of Optimal Retention", *Psychological Science* 19, 1095–1102 (2008)** — 1,350+ participants, gaps to 3.5 months, tests to 1 year. Optimal gap ≈ 20–40% of a one-week test delay, falling to 5–10% of a one-year delay; a rule of thumb of roughly 10–20% of the time until you need it. Exact numbers for the spacing lesson's worked example.
- **Bahrick, Bahrick, Bahrick & Bahrick, "Maintenance of Foreign Language Vocabulary and the Spacing Effect", *Psychological Science* 4, 316–321 (1993)** — 9-year study: 13 relearning sessions at 56-day intervals matched 26 sessions at 14 days. Bahrick (1984): Spanish learned in school by 733 people decays for 3–5 years then holds for 25–30 ("permastore").
- **Latimier, Peyre & Ramus, *Educational Psychology Review* 33, 959–987 (2021)** — spaced vs massed retrieval practice g = 0.74 across 29 studies; expanding vs uniform schedules roughly equal. Kang (2016, *Policy Insights from the Behavioral and Brain Sciences* 3, 12–19) is the accessible policy summary.

**Interleaving**
- **Rohrer & Taylor, "The shuffling of mathematics problems improves learning", *Instructional Science* 35, 481–498 (2007)** — four solids' volume formulae; blocked practice looked better during practice (about 89% vs 60% correct) but at one week interleaved scored 63% vs 20%.
- **Rohrer, Dedrick & Stershic, "Interleaved Practice Improves Mathematics Learning", *JEP* 107, 900–908 (2015)** — full text read. 126 seventh-graders, 3 months; 1-day test 80% vs 64% (d = 0.42), 30-day test 74% vs 42% (d = 0.79).
- **Rohrer, Dedrick, Hartwig & Cheung, "A Randomized Controlled Trial of Interleaved Mathematics Practice", *JEP* 112, 40–52 (2020)** — full text read. Preregistered cluster RCT, 54 seventh-grade classes, 4 months; one-month test 61% vs 38%, d = 0.83; teachers implemented without training.
- **Kornell & Bjork, "Learning Concepts and Categories: Is Spacing the 'Enemy of Induction'?", *Psychological Science* 19, 585–592 (2008)** — 12 painters, 6 paintings each; interleaving improved identifying new paintings, yet of 72 participants with a preference, 64 said massing worked better even after seeing their results.
- **Brunmair & Richter, "Similarity matters", *Psychological Bulletin* 145, 1029–1052 (2019)** — 59 studies, overall g = 0.42; paintings g = 0.67, maths g = 0.34, expository text nonsignificant, word lists favour blocking (g = −0.39). Interleaving helps when categories are confusable; it is not a universal good.

**Desirable difficulties, metacognition, learning vs performance**
- **Bjork, "Memory and metamemory considerations in the training of human beings", in Metcalfe & Shimamura (eds), *Metacognition* (MIT Press, 1994), 185–205** — coined "desirable difficulties".
- **Bjork & Bjork, "Making things hard on yourself, but in a good way", in *Psychology and the Real World* (Worth, 2011), 56–64** — full text read; free PDF on the Bjork Lab site. Storage strength (how entrenched) vs retrieval strength (how accessible now); learners mistake the second for the first. Named difficulties: varying conditions of practice, interleaving, spacing, using tests rather than presentations, plus the generation effect. Explicit warning that a difficulty is only desirable if the learner can meet it.
- **Soderstrom & Bjork, "Learning Versus Performance: An Integrative Review", *Perspectives on Psychological Science* 10, 176–199 (2015)** — performance during practice is an unreliable index of learning; some manipulations move them in opposite directions.
- **Bjork, Dunlosky & Kornell, "Self-Regulated Learning: Beliefs, Techniques, and Illusions", *Annual Review of Psychology* 64, 417–444 (2013)** — the fluency illusion: 90% of students did better after spacing, 72% still preferred massing. Koriat & Bjork (2005) "foresight bias"; Dunlosky & Rawson (2012, *Learning and Instruction* 22, 271–280): overconfident self-judgements led students to stop practising early and retain less.

**Elaboration, self-explanation, worked examples**
- **Chi, Bassok, Lewis, Reimann & Glaser, "Self-Explanations", *Cognitive Science* 13, 145–182 (1989)** — good physics students explain worked examples to themselves and monitor accurately; poor ones reread and copy. **Chi, de Leeuw, Chiu & LaVancher (1994)**: eighth-graders prompted to self-explain a circulatory-system text learned more than those who read it twice. Bisra et al. (2018, *Educational Psychology Review*) is the meta-analysis. Pressley et al. (1987) is the elaborative-interrogation origin.
- **Kirschner, Sweller & Clark, "Why Minimal Guidance During Instruction Does Not Work", *Educational Psychologist* 41, 75–86 (2006)** and **Hmelo-Silver, Duncan & Chinn (2007) response** — the guided-vs-discovery debate; the reply argues problem-based learning is heavily scaffolded, not minimal guidance.

**Expertise and deliberate practice**
- **Ericsson, Krampe & Tesch-Römer, "The role of deliberate practice in the acquisition of expert performance", *Psychological Review* 100, 363–406 (1993)** — 30 Berlin violinists in three groups; the best had accumulated about 7,410 hours of solitary practice by 18 vs 3,420 for future teachers, and over 10,000 by 20. Deliberate practice defined as effortful, teacher-designed activity with feedback, distinct from play and paid performance. **Ericsson & Pool, *Peak* (2016)**: naive vs purposeful vs deliberate practice; mental representations as the mechanism.
- **Macnamara, Hambrick & Oswald, *Psychological Science* 25, 1608–1618 (2014)** — meta-analysis: deliberate practice explains 26% of performance variance in games, 21% music, 18% sports, 4% education, <1% professions. **Hambrick et al. (2014, *Intelligence* 45, 34–45)**: about a third in chess, 30% in music. **Ericsson (2016)** reply: the meta-analysis counted almost any practice, not deliberate practice as defined; **Macnamara, Moreau & Hambrick (2016)** rejoinder. **Macnamara & Maitra, *Royal Society Open Science* 6:190327 (2019)**: preregistered replication with 39 Cleveland violinists; the best had practised *less* than the good (8,224 vs 9,844 hours by 18); practice explained 26% of variance, not the original 48%.

**Learning styles and other myths**
- **Pashler, McDaniel, Rohrer & Bjork, "Learning Styles: Concepts and Evidence", *Psychological Science in the Public Interest* 9, 105–119 (2008)** — sets the test (a crossover interaction: the best method for one style must not be the best for another) and finds "virtually no evidence" for it. **Willingham, Hughes & Dobolyi (2015, *Teaching of Psychology*)** update. **Dekker et al. (2012, *Frontiers in Psychology*)**: over 90% of UK and Dutch teachers endorsed learning styles. **Nancekivell, Shah & Gelman (2020, *JEP*)**: belief persists because people essentialise it.
- Perceptual disfluency ("hard fonts help"): Rummer, Schweppe & Schwede (2016) null across four experiments; Xie, Zhou & Liu (2018) meta-analysis near zero (d ≈ 0.01, via secondary source); five Sans Forgetica studies in 2020–22 found no benefit. The canonical example of an *undesirable* difficulty.
- Laptop vs longhand notes: Mueller & Oppenheimer (2014) did not replicate in Morehead, Dunlosky & Rawson (2019) or Urry et al. (2021, n = 142 plus mini-meta-analysis of 8 studies). Media multitasking: May & Elder (2018) review finds consistent negative associations with GPA, recall and comprehension.

**Memory basics**
- Miller (1956) "seven plus or minus two"; Cowan (2001) revises to about four chunks. Chase & Simon (1973) chess chunks; Gobet & Simon (1998) on the "50,000 chunks" figure. Working-memory limits are the mechanism behind cognitive load and chunking.

**Sleep**
- **Rasch & Born, "About Sleep's Role in Memory", *Physiological Reviews* 93, 681–766 (2013)** — the standard review: active systems consolidation during slow-wave sleep. **Cordi & Rasch, "How robust are sleep-mediated memory benefits?", *Current Opinion in Neurobiology* 67, 1–7 (2021)** — from the same lab: effects are "smaller, more task-dependent, less SWS-related, less robust and less long-lasting than previously assumed". **Cordi & Rasch (2021, *SLEEP*)**: 159 participants, no within-person correlation between slow-wave sleep and consolidation. Dewald et al. (2010) meta-analysis: sleep duration and school performance r ≈ 0.07 across 15,199 children. Walker's *Why We Sleep* has documented factual errors (Guzey 2019) and should not be cited.

## What the field considers essential

The order below is the order the ideas depend on each other; it becomes the outline backbone.

1. **Learning is not performance.** Memory has storage strength and retrieval strength; what you can do right now is a poor guide to what you will be able to do next month (Bjork & Bjork 1992/2011; Soderstrom & Bjork 2015). Everything else follows from this.
2. **Your sense of knowing is unreliable in a predictable direction.** Fluency, familiarity and immediate performance inflate judgements of learning (Koriat & Bjork 2005; Bjork, Dunlosky & Kornell 2013). This is why rereading and highlighting feel good and work badly (Dunlosky et al. 2013; Karpicke et al. 2009).
3. **Working memory is small; long-term memory is large and organised in chunks.** Cowan's four; chunking; the reason novices and experts see different things (Chase & Simon 1973; Willingham chs. 2–4). Cognitive load theory turns this into rules: cut extraneous load, use worked examples early, fade them as expertise grows (Sweller; Kalyuga).
4. **Memory is the residue of thought.** You remember what you think about, so meaning-making beats exposure (Willingham ch. 3; levels of processing).
5. **Retrieval practice.** The most robust effect in the literature: lab (Roediger & Karpicke 2006), meaningful learning (Karpicke & Blunt 2011), classrooms (Agarwal et al. 2021; Yang et al. 2021). Mechanism: retrieval strengthens and reorganises the memory trace; errors with feedback help.
6. **Spacing.** Gap sizing from Cepeda 2008; successive relearning from Rawson & Dunlosky 2011; Bahrick for decades-scale.
7. **Interleaving and variation.** Forces discrimination and strategy choice; strongest for confusable categories and maths problem types, weak or negative for word lists and prose (Rohrer; Brunmair & Richter 2019).
8. **Elaboration, self-explanation, and worked examples.** How to understand hard material: explain why, connect to prior knowledge, study solutions before attempting problems, then attempt (Chi 1989/1994; Sweller & Cooper 1985; expertise reversal).
9. **Deliberate practice and feedback for skills.** Ericsson's definition (specific goals, at the edge of ability, immediate feedback, designed by someone who knows the domain); Ambrose's goal-directed practice principle; the honest state of the variance-explained debate.
10. **Managing yourself.** Attention, media multitasking, procrastination, sleep, and building a schedule that spaces and interleaves within the hours you actually have (Oakley; May & Elder 2018; Rasch & Born with Cordi & Rasch caveats).
11. **The myths, named.** Learning styles, 10% of the brain, left/right brain, cramming, "10,000 hours", hard fonts, handwriting-beats-typing, multitasking.
12. **Building a personal system.** Apply 5–9 to one real subject as the course project; test whether it worked with a delayed self-test.

## Common misconceptions

- **"If it feels easy and fluent, I've learned it."** Fluency reflects retrieval strength now, not storage. Kornell & Bjork's painters: 64 of 72 preferred the method that worked worse.
- **"Rereading and highlighting are study."** They are the most-used techniques (84% and 55% top-ranked for rereading) and rated low utility; highlighting can impair inference. Replace with self-testing and then rereading only what you could not retrieve.
- **"Cramming works."** It does for tomorrow (restudy beat testing at 5 minutes, 81% vs 75%) and fails for next week (42% vs 56%). The learner must decide which they want.
- **"Testing is for measuring, not learning."** Most students who self-test do it to check, not to learn (Kornell & Bjork 2007). Testing is one of the two high-utility techniques.
- **"Practise one thing until it's perfect, then move on."** Blocked practice looks better during practice (89% vs 60%) and worse at a week (20% vs 63%).
- **"Everyone has a learning style, and matching it helps."** Preferences exist; the crossover interaction that would justify matching has virtually never been found (Pashler et al. 2008). Yet over 90% of surveyed teachers believed it (Dekker 2012).
- **"10,000 hours makes anyone an expert."** Gladwell's reading of an average as a threshold; Ericsson himself rejected it; practice explains a minority of variance in most domains (Macnamara 2014) and the founding study did not replicate cleanly (Macnamara & Maitra 2019).
- **"Any difficulty is desirable."** Only difficulties the learner can overcome, and that engage the target processing, help. Hard fonts do not.
- **"I can multitask."** Media multitasking in class is consistently associated with lower recall and grades (May & Elder 2018).
- **"Sleep is optional for learning" / "sleep is everything."** Sleep after learning helps consolidation, but the effect is smaller and less robust than popular books claim; the correlation between sleep duration and school grades is r ≈ 0.07.
- **"Understanding it once is enough."** Bahrick: decay is steepest in the first 3–5 years; what survives is what was relearned on spaced schedules.

## Contested questions and the positions on each

1. **How much of expertise does deliberate practice explain?** *Position A (Ericsson):* deliberate practice, properly defined, is the dominant modifiable cause; meta-analyses diluted the construct by counting any practice. *Position B (Macnamara, Hambrick):* even generously counted, practice explains roughly a fifth to a third of variance in music, chess and sport, 4% in education, under 1% in professions; other factors (starting age, working memory, genetics) matter and the original violin result did not replicate. *What would settle it:* preregistered longitudinal studies measuring practice quality prospectively rather than by retrospective estimate. *Status:* **contested empirical**. Teach as: deliberate practice is the best-supported way to improve; how far it goes is unresolved; "10,000 hours" is not a finding.
2. **Does growth mindset improve achievement?** *Position A (Yeager & Dweck 2020; Yeager et al. 2019, Nature):* a preregistered national RCT of 12,000+ ninth-graders in 65 schools found a sub-hour intervention raised grades among lower-achieving students and advanced-maths enrolment, when peer norms supported it; effects are real, small and heterogeneous. *Position B (Sisk et al. 2018; Macnamara & Burgoyne 2023):* across 43 intervention studies d = 0.08; across 63 studies (N = 97,672) d = 0.05, falling to 0.02 in the six best-designed; apparent effects likely reflect design flaws and bias. *What would settle it:* more preregistered, third-party, adequately powered trials with prespecified moderators. *Status:* **contested empirical**. Course.yaml's outcomes do not require mindset content; mention it only in the myths or motivation lesson with both positions.
3. **Do "desirable difficulties" transfer outside the lab?** Retrieval and spacing: classroom evidence is strong (Agarwal 2021; Yang 2021; Rohrer 2020 RCT). Generation, variability, and interleaving beyond maths: much thinner. Van Gog & Sweller (2015) argued the testing effect shrinks with material complexity; Karpicke & Aue (2015) replied that the null studies used massed or trivial retrieval. *Status:* **established** for retrieval and spacing in classrooms; **contested** for complexity limits and for the other difficulties.
4. **How general is interleaving?** Strong for maths problem types and visual categories; weak or reversed for word lists and prose (Brunmair & Richter 2019). *Status:* **established with boundary conditions**; teach the boundary, not a slogan.
5. **How strong is sleep-dependent consolidation?** Mechanistic story is well developed (Rasch & Born 2013) but the same lab now reports smaller, less robust behavioural effects and no within-person SWS correlation (Cordi & Rasch 2021). *Status:* **established that sleep matters; contested how much and by what mechanism**. Advise adequate sleep; do not promise specific gains.
6. **Guided instruction vs problem-based/inquiry learning** (Kirschner et al. 2006 vs Hmelo-Silver et al. 2007). Relevant to our worked-examples lesson. *Status:* **contested**, partly definitional; for novices, the worked-example and expertise-reversal evidence is solid.
7. **Learning styles.** Not contested among researchers; the meshing hypothesis is **established false** in the sense of no supporting evidence despite extensive search. Do not both-sides it.

## Safety-critical guidance (practical subjects)

- Not a safety domain. One note per course: sleep and attention advice is general education, not medical advice; learners with suspected ADHD, sleep disorders or learning disabilities should see a clinician, since some findings (e.g. optimal difficulty, note-taking) may not apply unchanged.

## Open concerns about scope

- **Six hours is tight for twelve essential ideas.** Suggest 8–9 lessons: (1) learning vs performance and the fluency illusion; (2) memory basics, chunking, cognitive load; (3) retrieval practice; (4) spacing; (5) interleaving and variation; (6) understanding hard material: worked examples, self-explanation, elaboration; (7) deliberate practice and feedback for skills; (8) attention, procrastination, sleep, and the study system; (9) the myths, or fold them into each lesson and use the final test to gather them. The project: apply the system to one real subject for two weeks and report a delayed self-test.
- **Oakley's framing vs the academic literature.** Learners will arrive knowing "focused/diffuse" and "chunking" from Coursera. Use her vocabulary as a bridge but source claims to the primary literature; do not repeat neuroscience glosses we cannot cite.
- **Effect sizes to verify in fact-check:** Adesope g = 0.61 and Xie d ≈ 0.01 came via secondary sources; all Rohrer, Roediger & Karpicke, Karpicke & Blunt, Cepeda 2008, Macnamara, Sisk, and Macnamara & Burgoyne figures were read from the papers or their abstracts.
- **WEIRD sample caveat.** 94% of classroom retrieval studies are from Western countries (Agarwal 2021). Say so once.
- **Course.yaml outcome 3 ("design deliberate practice for a skill") is the least supported by classroom research** and rests on a contested literature; the lesson must be explicit about that while still giving a usable method.

## Self-regulation

*Appended 2026-09-04 at the lesson 8 fact-check. Citation metadata verified via CrossRef; figures from published abstracts unless noted.*

- **Gollwitzer, "Implementation Intentions: Strong Effects of Simple Plans", *American Psychologist* 54(7), 493–503 (1999)**, DOI 10.1037/0003-066X.54.7.493 — distinguishes goal intentions ("I intend to reach X") from implementation intentions ("when situation Y arises, I will do Z"); the if-then plan delegates control of action to a situational cue, so it fires without a fresh decision.
- **Gollwitzer & Sheeran, "Implementation Intentions and Goal Achievement: A Meta-analysis of Effects and Processes", *Advances in Experimental Social Psychology* 38, 69–119 (2006)**, DOI 10.1016/S0065-2601(06)38002-1 — 94 independent tests; adding an if-then plan to a goal intention had a medium-to-large effect on goal attainment, d = 0.65 (abstract). Participant total is not in the abstract; "over 8,000" appears only in secondary sources and should not be quoted.
- **Steel, "The Nature of Procrastination: A Meta-Analytic and Theoretical Review of Quintessential Self-Regulatory Failure", *Psychological Bulletin* 133(1), 65–94 (2007)**, DOI 10.1037/0033-2909.133.1.65 — meta-analysis of 691 correlations; strong, consistent predictors of procrastination are task aversiveness, task delay, low self-efficacy and impulsiveness; framed by temporal motivation theory.
- **Duckworth, Gendler & Gross, "Situational Strategies for Self-Control", *Perspectives on Psychological Science* 11(1), 35–55 (2016)**, DOI 10.1177/1745691615623247 — theoretical paper using the process model of self-control: because impulses strengthen over time, strategies that select or modify the situation before temptation are argued to be more effective than effortful resistance in the moment, and are underused because they feel effortless. Cite as an argument, not a trial result.
- **Lim & Dinges, "A Meta-Analysis of the Impact of Short-Term Sleep Deprivation on Cognitive Variables", *Psychological Bulletin* 136(3), 375–389 (2010)**, DOI 10.1037/a0018883 — 70 articles, 147 tests; under short-term total sleep deprivation, lapses in simple attention g = −0.78, reasoning accuracy g = −0.13 (ns). Supports "a tired session is a worse one" for attention specifically. For partial restriction see Lowe, Safati & Hall, *Neuroscience & Biobehavioral Reviews* 80, 586–604 (2017).

### Perceptual disfluency and Sans Forgetica: verified citations (lesson 8 fact-check)

- Rummer, Schweppe & Schwede 2016 (*Metacognition and Learning* 11, 57–70, DOI 10.1007/s11409-015-9151-5) ran **three** experiments, not four as stated under "Learning styles and other myths" above.
- Xie, Zhou & Liu 2018 (*Educational Psychology Review* 30, 745–771, DOI 10.1007/s10648-018-9442-x): 25 articles, 3,135 participants; recall d = −0.01, transfer d = 0.03, judgments of learning d = −0.43, learning time d = 0.52. **Weissgerber, Brunmair & Rummer 2021** (*Educational Psychology Review* 33, 1221–1247, DOI 10.1007/s10648-020-09579-1) found coding and effect-size errors; transfer result robust, recall result and moderators doubtful. Quote "near zero" or the transfer figure, not a precise recall d. This supersedes the "d ≈ 0.01, via secondary source" note above.
- Sans Forgetica, no memory benefit: **Taylor, Sanson, Burnell, Wade & Garry**, *Memory* 28(7), 850–857 (2020), DOI 10.1080/09658211.2020.1758726 (four experiments); **Wetzler, Pyke & Werner**, *SAGE Open* 11(4) (2021), DOI 10.1177/21582440211056624 (N = 120, one-week delay); **Huff, Maxwell & Mitchell**, *Cognitive Research: Principles and Implications* 7:102 (2022), DOI 10.1186/s41235-022-00448-9 (four experiments).
- Sans Forgetica, benefit only under conditions unlike study: **Geller, Davis & Peterson**, *Memory* 28(8), 957–967 (2020), DOI 10.1080/09658211.2020.1797096 (benefit only with no test expected); **Geller & Peterson**, *JEP: LMC* 47(12), 1924–1938 (2021), DOI 10.1037/xlm0001042 (same boundary); **Eskenazi & Nix**, *JEP: LMC* 47(1), 45–52 (2021), DOI 10.1037/xlm0000809 (benefit only for high-skill spellers). Also **Cui & Liu**, *Vision* 6(3):52 (2022) (small recognition advantage the authors hold reservations about) and **Cushing & Bodner**, *JARMAC* 11(3), 427–436 (2022) (no proofreading benefit).
- Net: say "no usable benefit", not "five null studies".

### Other corrections found at the lesson 8 fact-check (append-only; fold into the entries above when editing)

- **Dewald et al. 2010** (*Sleep Medicine Reviews* 14, 179–189, DOI 10.1016/j.smrv.2009.10.004): sample is children **and adolescents**; sleep duration r = 0.069 (17 studies, N = 15,199); sleep quality r = 0.096 (N = 13,631); sleepiness r = −0.133 (N = 19,530). Quote all three, not only the smallest.
- **Cordi & Rasch 2021 in *SLEEP*** is 44(8), zsab034, DOI 10.1093/sleep/zsab034. Cordi & Rasch is Rasch's group (Fribourg), not Born's; say "Rasch, a co-author of the 2013 review", not "the same lab".
- **Cepeda et al. 2008**: the paper gives about 20–40% of a one-week delay and 5–10% of a one-year delay; optimal gaps of 1/11/21/21 days at 7/35/70/350-day retention intervals; too-short gaps cost more than too-long ones. The course's 10–20% rule of thumb is a simplification, not a figure from the paper.
- **Deans for Impact, *The Science of Learning***: 2nd edition released May 2026; the misconceptions table is one page (p. 13), five rows, with learning styles and left/right brain in a single row.
- **Nancekivell, Shah & Gelman 2020** (*J Educ Psychol* 112(2), 221–235, DOI 10.1037/edu0000366): shows that many believers hold an essentialist (innate, brain-based) view of styles, not that essentialism is *why* the belief persists.
- **The Pomodoro technique** is Francesco Cirillo's (late 1980s); Oakley recommends it and credits him.
- **Nielsen, Zielinski, Ferguson, Lainhart & Anderson**, "An Evaluation of the Left-Brain vs. Right-Brain Hypothesis with Resting State Functional Connectivity Magnetic Resonance Imaging", *PLOS ONE* 8(8), e71275 (2013) — 1,011 individuals aged 7–29; lateralisation is a local property of particular connections, not a whole-person trait. Use for the left/right-brain myth.
- **May & Elder 2018** abstract (verified): effects "demonstrated during in-class activities (largely lectures) and while students are studying"; "students struggle to accurately assess the impact media multitasking will have on their academic performance". It is a narrative review with no pooled effect size.
