# Outline: How to Learn Anything

*Stage 2. Built from SOURCES.md. Eight lessons, a final test, a two-week project. Sequence follows the dependency order in "What the field considers essential": first the two ideas that explain why intuition misleads (learning vs performance; the fluency illusion), then the machinery (memory, load), then the four techniques in descending strength of evidence, then how to understand hard things, then skills, then running yourself. Myths are named inside the lesson they belong to rather than quarantined in a "myths" lesson, and gathered in the final test.*

**Conventions for every lesson:** open with the learner's own experience (a study habit they have), then the finding that contradicts it, then the mechanism, then the method. Every lesson ends with a "Do it now" exercise that uses the technique on this course's own content, so the course is its own worked example. Every lesson names its sources by author and year in the text and lists them in `## Sources`. Cite figures exactly as SOURCES.md records them; two are flagged "via secondary source" (Adesope 2017 g = 0.61; Xie 2018 d ≈ 0.01) and must be marked as such or omitted until fact-checked.

## Lesson sequence

### 1. Learning is not performance (~35 min)
- **Objectives:** explain the difference between storage strength and retrieval strength; identify the fluency illusion in a described study habit; predict which of two study conditions will win at one week versus five minutes.
- **Core ideas:** what you can do right now is a poor guide to what you'll be able to do next month. Memory has two strengths (Bjork & Bjork 2011): storage (how entrenched) and retrieval (how accessible now). Practice conditions that boost retrieval strength today can leave storage strength untouched. Judgements of learning track fluency and current performance, so they are biased in a predictable direction (Koriat & Bjork 2005; Bjork, Dunlosky & Kornell 2013).
- **Mechanism:** rereading raises fluency (the text feels familiar) without forcing the memory to be reconstructed; familiarity is read as knowledge.
- **Worked examples:** (a) Roediger & Karpicke 2006 Exp. 1 laid out as a prediction game: 5 min restudy 81% vs test 75%; one week 42% vs 56%. Have the learner predict before revealing. (b) With a wrinkle: Kornell & Bjork 2008 painters, where 64 of 72 preferred the method that worked worse *even after seeing their own results*, to show the illusion survives evidence.
- **Misconceptions:** "if it feels easy I've learned it"; "rereading and highlighting are studying" (84% list rereading; Dunlosky rates it low; highlighting can hurt inference); "cramming works" (it does for tomorrow, and the learner should decide which they want).
- **Practice:** the learner writes down their three main study habits and, for each, whether it boosts retrieval strength, storage strength, or both. Then a 5-item self-test on this lesson without looking back.
- **Sources:** Bjork & Bjork 2011; Soderstrom & Bjork 2015; Roediger & Karpicke 2006; Kornell & Bjork 2008; Karpicke, Butler & Roediger 2009; Dunlosky et al. 2013; Bjork, Dunlosky & Kornell 2013.
- **Connects:** foundation for everything; lesson 3 shows the fix.

### 2. How memory actually works (~35 min)
- **Objectives:** explain why working memory limits make novices and experts see different things; apply the idea of chunking to a subject you're learning; identify extraneous cognitive load in a piece of instruction and remove it.
- **Core ideas:** working memory holds about four chunks (Cowan 2001, revising Miller 1956); long-term memory is effectively unlimited and organised in chunks; expertise is having bigger chunks (Chase & Simon 1973 chess). "Memory is the residue of thought" (Willingham ch. 3): you remember what you thought about, so exposure without processing leaves little. Cognitive load theory (Sweller): intrinsic load from the material's element interactivity, extraneous load from bad presentation; cut the second, manage the first.
- **Mechanism:** new material that exceeds working memory can't be assembled into a chunk; prior knowledge is what makes big chunks possible, which is why factual knowledge precedes skill (Willingham ch. 2) and why abstractions are understood via what you already know (ch. 4).
- **Worked examples:** (a) reading a chess position as a novice vs a master: same board, different number of chunks; the same thing happens reading a paragraph of law, code, or Scripture. (b) With a wrinkle: a learner "understands" a lecture in the moment because the lecturer is holding the chunks for them; alone the next day, working memory overflows. That's why notes that copy slides don't help and notes that reconstruct do.
- **Misconceptions:** "smart people have bigger working memory" (differences are small; chunk size is the lever); "I can multitask" (May & Elder 2018: consistently negative associations); "10% of the brain".
- **Practice:** take one page of anything you're learning and list its chunks; mark which you already own and which are new. Rewrite one confusing explanation to cut extraneous load.
- **Sources:** Cowan 2001; Miller 1956; Chase & Simon 1973; Willingham 2009 chs. 2–4; Sweller, van Merriënboer & Paas 2019; May & Elder 2018; Deans for Impact misconceptions table.
- **Connects:** explains why worked examples (lesson 6) and prior knowledge matter; sets up lesson 3's "reconstruct, don't recognise".

### 3. Retrieval practice: the strongest tool there is (~40 min)
- **Objectives:** explain why retrieving beats restudying and what "the testing effect" means; design a retrieval routine for a real subject; distinguish self-testing to check from self-testing to learn.
- **Core ideas:** pulling information out of memory strengthens and reorganises it more than putting it in again. Dunlosky's top rating. Works for meaningful learning, not just facts (Karpicke & Blunt 2011: retrieval beat concept mapping, d = 1.50, even on an inference test, even when the final test was a concept map). Works in real classrooms (Agarwal, Nunes & Blunt 2021: 50 experiments; Yang et al. 2021: g = 0.499 across 222 studies). Errors with feedback help. Successive relearning (Rawson & Dunlosky 2011): recall correctly three times, then relearn to one correct recall in three spaced sessions.
- **Mechanism:** retrieval is an act of reconstruction; each reconstruction lays down more routes to the memory. Recognition (rereading) doesn't exercise the route.
- **Worked examples:** (a) Karpicke & Blunt walked through: four conditions, one-week test, 0.67 vs 0.45, and the students who predicted the opposite. (b) With a wrinkle: Kornell & Bjork 2007, where only 18% of students who self-test do it to learn; the rest do it to check and then stop. The difference in behaviour: after checking, they reread everything; after learning, they retest only what they missed.
- **Misconceptions:** "testing is for measuring"; "flashcards are for kids/vocabulary"; "I'll get it wrong and learn the wrong thing" (errors followed by feedback improve later recall).
- **Practice:** build a ten-question self-test for lesson 1 and 2 of this course from memory, then check. Then set up one retrieval routine for a subject you're actually studying (free recall on a blank page after each session; questions in the Review page of this site).
- **Sources:** Roediger & Karpicke 2006; Karpicke & Blunt 2011; Karpicke, Butler & Roediger 2009; Kornell & Bjork 2007; Agarwal, Nunes & Blunt 2021; Yang et al. 2021; Rawson & Dunlosky 2011; Dunlosky et al. 2013. Adesope 2017 only if marked "via secondary source".
- **Connects:** the site's Review feature is this lesson in software; lesson 4 says when to retrieve.

### 4. Spacing: when to come back (~35 min)
- **Objectives:** calculate a sensible review gap from how long you need to remember something; explain why spacing beats massing; set up a spaced schedule for a real goal.
- **Core ideas:** the same total study time spread over gaps beats the same time in one block (Cepeda et al. 2006: 317 experiments). The best gap grows with how long you need to remember: about 20–40% of a one-week horizon, 5–10% of a one-year horizon, roughly 10–20% as a rule of thumb (Cepeda et al. 2008). Spaced retrieval combines the two strongest techniques (Latimier et al. 2021, g = 0.74). Decades-scale: Bahrick's Spanish "permastore" and his 9-year vocabulary study, where 13 sessions at 56 days matched 26 at 14 days.
- **Mechanism:** a partly forgotten memory has to be reconstructed on review, which is retrieval practice; a fresh memory is just recognised. Forgetting a little is the price of learning a lot.
- **Worked examples:** (a) an exam in 30 days: gap ≈ 3–6 days; lay out the actual calendar. (b) With a wrinkle: learning a language you want for life: Bahrick's numbers imply fewer, wider-spaced sessions over years beat many close ones, and that decay is steepest in years 1–5.
- **Misconceptions:** "cramming is efficient"; "I forgot it so the studying was wasted" (the forgetting is what made the next review count); "expanding schedules are essential" (expanding and uniform are roughly equal; Latimier 2021).
- **Practice:** pick one thing you must know in 3 months; write the review dates. Then note how the site's Review page schedules questions and why the gaps lengthen.
- **Sources:** Cepeda et al. 2006, 2008; Bahrick et al. 1993; Bahrick 1984; Latimier, Peyre & Ramus 2021; Kang 2016; Dunlosky et al. 2013.
- **Spaced review of:** lesson 1's five-minute-vs-one-week result, now explained by spacing.

### 5. Interleaving and variation: mix it up, within limits (~30 min)
- **Objectives:** explain what interleaving forces the learner to do that blocking doesn't; decide, for a given subject, whether interleaving will help; design a mixed practice set.
- **Core ideas:** practising mixed problem types beats practising one type at a time, at a delay (Rohrer & Taylor 2007: 89% vs 60% during practice, then 63% vs 20% at a week; Rohrer et al. 2015: d = 0.79 at 30 days; the 2020 preregistered RCT across 54 classes: 61% vs 38%, d = 0.83, no teacher training needed). Boundary: it helps when categories are confusable (paintings g = 0.67, maths 0.34) and is neutral or harmful for prose and word lists (word lists g = −0.39; Brunmair & Richter 2019).
- **Mechanism:** blocked practice lets you skip the hardest step, choosing which method applies; interleaving forces the choice on every problem and forces discrimination between similar categories.
- **Worked examples:** (a) Rohrer's volume formulae: blocked looks better in the session and collapses at a week. (b) With a wrinkle: Kornell & Bjork's painters, where interleaving improved recognising *new* paintings by each artist, and learners still believed blocking worked better.
- **Misconceptions:** "practise until perfect then move on"; "interleaving is always better" (not for vocabulary lists or reading prose).
- **Practice:** take two or three confusable things from a subject (three tenses, three chart types, three doctrines, three formulas) and build a shuffled ten-item practice set. Do it.
- **Sources:** Rohrer & Taylor 2007; Rohrer, Dedrick & Stershic 2015; Rohrer, Dedrick, Hartwig & Cheung 2020; Kornell & Bjork 2008; Brunmair & Richter 2019; Dunlosky et al. 2013 (interleaving rated moderate; say why).
- **Spaced review of:** learning vs performance (lesson 1) as the reason blocked practice fools you.

### 6. Understanding hard material (~40 min)
- **Objectives:** use worked examples correctly (study, then attempt, then fade); apply self-explanation and elaborative interrogation to a passage; recognise when guidance should be reduced as you improve.
- **Core ideas:** for a novice, studying worked solutions beats attempting problems (Sweller & Cooper 1985: about half the time, a fifth of the errors, though the advantage didn't extend to problems that varied from the studied ones). As expertise grows, that guidance becomes redundant or harmful: the expertise-reversal effect (Kalyuga et al. 2003). Self-explanation: good physics students explain worked examples to themselves and monitor accurately; poor ones reread and copy (Chi et al. 1989); prompting self-explanation raised learning from a biology text over rereading (Chi et al. 1994). Elaborative interrogation ("why would that be true?") works when you have prior knowledge to connect to (Dunlosky: moderate). Contested: guided vs discovery learning (Kirschner, Sweller & Clark 2006 vs Hmelo-Silver et al. 2007); for novices the worked-example evidence is solid.
- **Mechanism:** cognitive load (lesson 2). A novice attempting a problem spends working memory on search rather than on learning the pattern; a worked example hands over the pattern. Self-explanation forces you to fill the gaps the author skipped, which is exactly where understanding fails.
- **Worked examples:** (a) an algebra problem studied as a worked example, then a near-transfer attempt, then a varied one, showing where the benefit stops. (b) With a wrinkle: a paragraph of dense text (from any course on this site) taken through self-explanation prompts: "what does this sentence add? why is that true? how does it connect to the last one?"
- **Misconceptions:** "struggling first is always best" (not for novices); "if I can follow the solution I could produce it" (lesson 1's illusion again); "summarising and highlighting are elaboration" (they're rated low).
- **Practice:** pick a hard passage or problem from a course you're taking; do one worked-example cycle and one self-explanation pass; write down the two gaps you found.
- **Sources:** Sweller & Cooper 1985; Kalyuga et al. 2003; Sweller, van Merriënboer & Paas 2019; Chi et al. 1989, 1994; Bisra et al. 2018; Pressley et al. 1987; Kirschner, Sweller & Clark 2006; Hmelo-Silver et al. 2007; Dunlosky et al. 2013.
- **Connects:** this is how Foval lessons are built (worked example, then wrinkle, then practice); the learner should now see the design.

### 7. Getting good at a skill: deliberate practice and feedback (~35 min)
- **Objectives:** define deliberate practice and distinguish it from naive practice; design a practice session for a real skill with a specific goal and a feedback loop; state honestly what the research does and doesn't show about practice and expertise.
- **Core ideas:** Ericsson's definition (1993): effortful, at the edge of current ability, with specific goals, immediate feedback, and structure designed by someone who knows the domain; distinct from play and from performing. Ambrose's principle: goal-directed practice plus targeted feedback. The mechanism Ericsson proposed: better mental representations. **The honest state of the debate, classified as contested (standards 3.1):** Macnamara, Hambrick & Oswald 2014 found practice explains 26% of variance in games, 21% music, 18% sports, 4% education, under 1% in professions; Ericsson replied the meta-analysis counted any practice; the preregistered replication (Macnamara & Maitra 2019) found the best violinists had practised *less* than the good ones, with practice explaining 26% of variance rather than 48%. "10,000 hours" was Gladwell's reading of an average, not a threshold, and Ericsson rejected it.
- **Mechanism:** feedback closes the loop between what you did and what you intended; without it, repetition entrenches whatever you're already doing. Working at the edge (not beyond) is the practical form of "desirable difficulty".
- **Worked examples:** (a) two people each practising guitar an hour a day for a year: one plays songs they know, one drills the transitions they can't do with a metronome and records themselves. (b) With a wrinkle: a professional (nurse, lawyer, salesperson) with twenty years' experience who isn't better than at year five, because daily work isn't designed practice, which is what the <1% figure in professions is about.
- **Misconceptions:** "10,000 hours"; "experience equals expertise"; "talent doesn't matter / only talent matters" (both positions overstated); "harder is always better" (difficulties must be surmountable).
- **Practice:** choose one skill. Write: the specific sub-skill you can't yet do, the drill, the feedback source, the session length. Do one session and log it.
- **Sources:** Ericsson, Krampe & Tesch-Römer 1993; Ericsson & Pool 2016; Ambrose et al. 2010; Macnamara, Hambrick & Oswald 2014; Hambrick et al. 2014; Ericsson 2016; Macnamara, Moreau & Hambrick 2016; Macnamara & Maitra 2019; Bjork & Bjork 2011 on surmountable difficulty.
- **Neutrality note:** present both positions in their own terms; do not resolve.

### 8. Running yourself: attention, procrastination, sleep, and your system (~40 min)
- **Objectives:** build a weekly study plan that spaces and interleaves within the hours you actually have; apply one evidence-based procrastination tactic; state what the evidence does and doesn't show about sleep and learning; name and refute the common learning myths.
- **Core ideas:** attention: media multitasking is consistently associated with worse recall and grades (May & Elder 2018); single-task in short blocks. Procrastination: the self-regulation literature's tactics (implementation intentions, shrinking the first step, environment design; Oakley's framing as a bridge, cite the underlying research). Sleep: consolidation during sleep is real (Rasch & Born 2013), but the same lab reports the effects are smaller and less robust than assumed (Cordi & Rasch 2021), and sleep duration correlates with school performance at only r ≈ 0.07 (Dewald et al. 2010): sleep enough, don't expect magic, and don't cite *Why We Sleep*. Myths gathered: learning styles (Pashler et al. 2008: virtually no evidence for the crossover; yet over 90% of teachers believed it, Dekker 2012), hard fonts (Rummer 2016 null; Sans Forgetica nulls), handwriting beats typing (Mueller & Oppenheimer 2014 did not replicate: Morehead 2019, Urry 2021), growth mindset (contested: Yeager 2019 vs Sisk 2018 / Macnamara & Burgoyne 2023, presented both ways). The system: a week's plan template combining lessons 3–7 in the hours available.
- **Mechanism:** every technique in the course costs effort now for payoff later, which is exactly the trade procrastination and multitasking undo. Self-management is the delivery mechanism for everything else.
- **Worked examples:** (a) a learner with 5 hours a week and three subjects: build the actual timetable with spacing, interleaving, retrieval, and a weekly free-recall session. (b) With a wrinkle: a learner with 45 minutes a day on a phone commute: what fits (retrieval, spaced review, audio) and what doesn't (worked examples needing paper).
- **Misconceptions:** all the above, plus "I need long uninterrupted blocks or it's pointless".
- **Practice:** write your own plan for next week using the template; commit to it; that plan is the start of the course project.
- **Sources:** May & Elder 2018; Rasch & Born 2013; Cordi & Rasch 2021 (both); Dewald et al. 2010; Pashler et al. 2008; Dekker et al. 2012; Nancekivell 2020; Rummer et al. 2016; Morehead, Dunlosky & Rawson 2019; Urry et al. 2021; Yeager et al. 2019; Sisk et al. 2018; Macnamara & Burgoyne 2023; Gollwitzer on implementation intentions; Oakley 2014 chs. 5–6 (bridge only).
- **Safety note (once, here):** general education, not medical advice; suspected ADHD, sleep disorders, or learning disabilities warrant a clinician.
- **Spaced review of:** lesson 1 (fluency), lesson 3 (retrieval), lesson 4 (gaps), lesson 7 (surmountable difficulty).

## Assessments

- **Course-end test** (`assessments/final-test.md`): 20 items. Blueprint: 3 on learning vs performance, 2 on memory/load, 4 on retrieval, 3 on spacing, 2 on interleaving with boundary conditions, 2 on worked examples/self-explanation, 2 on deliberate practice including the contested status, 2 on myths. At least 12 items are application cases ("A student does X; what will happen at one week and why?"). Pass mark 80%.
- **Project** (`assessments/project-two-weeks.md`): "Apply the method for two weeks." The learner picks one real subject, writes a plan (lesson 8), runs it for 14 days with a log, and finishes with a delayed self-test they wrote on day 1 and take on day 14 without looking. Deliverable: the plan, the log, the test with scores, and a one-page reflection naming which technique helped most and one thing they'd change. Rubric: plan uses at least three of retrieval, spacing, interleaving, worked examples, deliberate practice (3 pts); log shows the plan was actually followed or honestly adjusted (3 pts); delayed self-test exists and was written before studying (2 pts); reflection cites specific evidence from the log, not impressions (2 pts). 7/10 to pass.

## Neutrality notes
Not a sensitive domain. Two contested empirical questions must be presented with both positions: deliberate practice's share of expertise (lesson 7) and growth mindset (lesson 8). Learning styles is not contested and should not be both-sided. Cite figures exactly; flag the two secondary-sourced effect sizes.
