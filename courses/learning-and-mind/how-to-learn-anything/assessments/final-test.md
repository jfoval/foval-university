  # --- Learning vs performance (3) ---
  - q: >-
      Priya reads her anatomy chapter four times in one evening. By the fourth pass the text feels
      completely familiar and she can predict what comes next. She rates her knowledge as 9/10 and
      stops. What is the most likely state of her memory a week later?
    options:
      - She will remember most of it; four passes is thorough, and the familiarity she feels is a reliable sign the material went in
      - She will remember far less than she predicts; rereading raised fluency, not storage strength
      - She will remember it well, but only if she also sleeps properly that night, since sleep is what consolidates a reread
      - She will remember about as much as someone who self-tested for the same time, since time on task is what matters
    answer: 1
    explain: >-
      Rereading makes text feel familiar, and familiarity gets read as knowledge. Bjork and Bjork
      (2011) separate storage strength (how entrenched a memory is) from retrieval strength (how
      accessible it is right now). Four passes in one evening push retrieval strength and fluency
      up, which is exactly what her 9/10 rating is tracking, while adding little to storage
      strength. So B. A trusts the feeling this course exists to warn you about. C makes sleep the
      deciding factor; sleep helps consolidation, but it doesn't rescue shallow encoding. D is
      wrong because equal time doesn't mean equal learning: Roediger and Karpicke (2006) found
      repeated study lost to repeated testing at one week (40% vs 61%) with the same time on task.
  - q: >-
      Two learners study the same word list for the same total time. Marco crams it the night before
      a test tomorrow. Dana spreads it across a week and tests herself. Which statement about the
      results is correct?
    options:
      - Marco does better tomorrow and Dana does better next month; each has to decide which they need
      - Dana does better both tomorrow and next month, because spacing and testing win in every condition
      - Marco does better both tomorrow and next month, because he encoded it all in one concentrated block
      - They score about the same at both times, since they spent the same total time on the list
    answer: 0
    explain: >-
      Performance and learning can move in opposite directions (Soderstrom and Bjork 2015). The
      same reversal shows up in Roediger and Karpicke (2006): restudy beat testing five minutes
      later (81% vs 75%) and lost a week later (42% vs 56%). Cramming works for tomorrow; it's
      later that it fails. So A is the honest answer: Marco has to decide which he wants. B
      overstates the case; spacing and testing usually look worse in the short term, which is
      exactly why people give them up. C confuses the short-term win with learning. D repeats the
      "time is what matters" error from the first question.
  - q: >-
      In Kornell and Bjork's painting study, learners who saw each artist's paintings interleaved
      with other artists' were better at identifying new paintings by those artists than learners
      who saw each artist's work in a block. Straight after the test, participants were asked which
      schedule had helped them learn more. What did most say?
    options:
      - Most said interleaving, because the test they had just taken made the difference obvious
      - Most said the two were about the same, since they couldn't tell which artists had been blocked
      - Most said blocking had helped more, even though their own test performance showed the opposite
      - Most said interleaving would help other people, but that blocking had worked for them personally
    answer: 2
    explain: >-
      In the first experiment (120 people, each seeing some artists blocked and some interleaved),
      interleaved artists were identified at .61 against .35 for blocked ones, and 78% of
      participants did better on the interleaved artists. Asked which had helped more, 78% said
      blocking was as good or better. In a second experiment (80 people), of the 72 who didn't say
      "about the same", 64 said blocking. So C. Nobody was shown scores by condition, and a
      follow-up found people couldn't even tell which artists had been blocked, which is why B is
      half right and still wrong: they couldn't tell the schedules apart, yet they had a firm
      preference, and it was for the one that worked worse. A assumes taking a test educates the
      judgement; it doesn't, because the judgement is built from how study felt while you were
      doing it (blocked feels smooth, interleaved feels hard). D invents a split the study never
      asked about. The practical lesson: don't use "which felt better" as your guide. Use a
      delayed test.
  # --- Memory and cognitive load (2) ---
  - q: >-
      Tom understands every step of a statistics lecture while the lecturer is talking. That night,
      alone with the slides he copied word for word, he cannot follow any of it. What best explains
      the gap?
    options:
      - He has a smaller working memory than most people, so he should copy the slides more carefully next time
      - He was multitasking during the lecture without noticing it, so very little was encoded in the first place
      - Understanding fades overnight for everyone, so he needed to reread the slides the same day
      - The lecturer was holding the chunks for him; alone, verbatim slides give him nothing to rebuild them from
    answer: 3
    explain: >-
      Working memory holds about four chunks (Cowan 2001, revising Miller's seven). A lecturer
      supplies the structure that groups elements into chunks, so the material fits while they
      talk. Alone, Tom has to build the chunks himself, and slides copied word for word record the
      lecturer's words without the connections that made them make sense. So D. Notes that
      reconstruct (your own words, with the reasons) help; notes that copy don't. A blames
      capacity; people do differ, but nobody's working memory holds a lecture's worth of unchunked
      detail, and chunk size is the lever you control. B invents a cause the question gives no
      sign of. C turns a chunking problem into a timing problem; rereading the same slides the
      same day would leave him equally stuck.
  - q: >-
      A tutorial on compound interest opens with a two-paragraph history of banking, uses a
      different symbol for the interest rate on each page, and puts the formula in an image the
      reader has to scroll away from the worked numbers to see. A student finds it exhausting.
      Which change would cut the most extraneous load?
    options:
      - Cut the history, use one symbol throughout, and put the formula next to the numbers
      - Simplify the mathematics so the formula has fewer terms and fewer steps to hold in mind
      - Ask the student to work faster, so the pieces are still in working memory together
      - Add a summary quiz at the end so the student retrieves what they read instead of just reading
    answer: 0
    explain: >-
      Cognitive load theory (Sweller, van Merriënboer and Paas 2019) separates intrinsic load,
      which comes from how many elements interact in the material itself, from extraneous load,
      which comes from how the material is presented. The history, the shifting notation and the
      split between formula and numbers are all presentation problems, so A removes extraneous
      load without touching the content. B reduces intrinsic load by teaching less, which is a
      different trade. C makes the overload worse. D is good practice in general (lesson 3) but
      does nothing about the exhaustion the presentation is causing.
  # --- Retrieval practice (4) ---
  - q: >-
      Aisha reads a passage once, then spends the rest of her study session closing the book and
      writing out everything she can remember, twice. Ben reads the same passage four times in the
      same total time. Tested five minutes later and again one week later, what happens?
    options:
      - Ben wins at five minutes, Aisha wins at a week, and Ben feels more confident throughout
      - Aisha wins at both times, because retrieval beats rereading at every delay ever tested
      - Ben wins at both times, because four exposures beat one exposure plus two attempts
      - They score the same at a week; retrieval's advantage only shows up at short delays
    answer: 0
    explain: >-
      This is the pattern from Roediger and Karpicke (2006). Experiment 1: at five minutes restudy
      led (81% vs 75%); at two days testing led (68% vs 54%); at one week testing led (56% vs
      42%). Experiment 2: a study-test-test-test group recalled 61% at one week against 40% for
      study-study-study-study, and the repeated studiers were the more confident group. So A: Ben
      gets the short-term edge and the confidence; Aisha gets the memory. B ignores the
      five-minute result, which is exactly what makes people distrust retrieval. C and D read the
      five-minute result as if it were the one-week result.
  - q: >-
      Leo has to understand a science text well enough to answer inference questions, rather than
      simply recall it. His friend says concept mapping is the better method for that because it "builds
      understanding", while retrieval is only for facts. Based on Karpicke and Blunt (2011), what
      should Leo expect?
    options:
      - They will perform about equally, since concept mapping and retrieval are both active methods
      - Concept mapping wins on inference questions and retrieval wins on verbatim ones
      - Concept mapping wins overall, because it takes longer and engages more thought about structure
      - Retrieval beats concept mapping on both kinds of question, even if the final test is a concept map
    answer: 3
    explain: >-
      Karpicke and Blunt (2011) tested this with a 276-word science text and a one-week
      short-answer test containing both verbatim and inference questions. Retrieval practice
      scored 0.67 against 0.45 for concept mapping (d = 1.50). In a second, within-subject
      experiment the final test was itself a concept map, and retrieval still won (d = 1.01). So
      D. The students expected none of this: asked during learning, they ranked repeated study
      best and retrieval worst, and 75% expected mapping to do as well as or better than retrieval.
      B is the intuition the study refuted. A treats "active" as the operative feature; what
      matters is that retrieval forces you to reconstruct. C assumes more time and more effort mean
      more learning, which lesson 1 showed is unreliable; the mappers had the text and an example
      map in front of them the whole time.
  - q: >-
      Sofia uses flashcards. She goes through the whole deck once, sees that she got about
      two-thirds right, decides she "knows where she stands", and then rereads the entire chapter.
      What is the problem, and what should she do instead?
    options:
      - There is no problem; she has combined testing with rereading, which covers both bases
      - She is testing to check, not to learn; she should retest what she missed and reread only what still won't come
      - Flashcards suit vocabulary and dates; for a chapter she should switch to concept mapping instead
      - She should reread first and test after, so she doesn't get answers wrong and learn the errors
    answer: 1
    explain: >-
      Kornell and Bjork (2007) surveyed 472 UCLA students. Only 18% self-tested because it teaches
      more; 68% did it to check what they knew, and 9% didn't self-test at all. Sofia is in the
      68%. Checking and then rereading everything throws away the benefit. So B: treat a miss as
      the start of practice. Retest what you missed, get feedback, retest, and reread only the
      residue. A rereads material she has already retrieved, which is the low-utility step. C
      underrates retrieval: it works for meaningful material as well as for facts (Karpicke and
      Blunt 2011). D worries about errors, but a failed retrieval followed by the answer beat
      simply reading the answer in five of six experiments (Kornell, Hays and Bjork 2009). The
      errors don't stick.
  - q: >-
      Rahul has 40 pharmacology terms to know for an exam in five weeks. He wants a retrieval
      schedule with the best evidence behind it. Which plan matches the "successive relearning"
      prescription from Rawson and Dunlosky (2011)?
    options:
      - Recall each term correctly once, then reread the list a few times in the week before the exam
      - Recall each term correctly ten times in one sitting, so each is firmly stored from the start
      - Recall each term correctly three times, then relearn each to one correct recall in three spaced sessions
      - Reread the list daily for five weeks and run one full self-test the night before the exam
    answer: 2
    explain: >-
      Rawson and Dunlosky (2011), with 533 students, found the efficient prescription was three
      correct recalls per item initially, then relearning each item to one correct recall in three
      spaced sessions. That combines retrieval with spacing, so C. A stops after a single success
      and switches to rereading. B piles all the retrievals into one session, which is massed
      practice wearing a retrieval costume; the later sessions are where the spacing effect comes
      from. D is the most common student plan and the one Dunlosky et al. (2013) rate low utility.
  # --- Spacing (3) ---
  - q: >-
      Nadia has an exam in 30 days and has just learned a topic. Using the course's rule of thumb
      and the Cepeda et al. (2008) data behind it, roughly how long should the gap be before her
      next review of that topic?
    options:
      - About one day, so that nothing has been forgotten by the time she comes back to it
      - About 4 to 10 days, which lets some forgetting happen before she returns to it
      - About 15 days, halfway to the exam, since a wider gap is always the safer choice
      - It doesn't matter, so long as the total number of reviews before the exam is the same
    answer: 1
    explain: >-
      The 10–20% rule of thumb comes from Carpenter, Cepeda, Rohrer, Kang and Pashler (2012);
      applied to 30 days it gives 3 to 6 days. Cepeda et al. (2008), the data behind it, measured
      optimal gaps of 1, 11, 21 and 21 days for horizons of 7, 35, 70 and 350 days, so the nearest
      measured horizon to Nadia's gave 11 days. Between the rule and the data, 4 to 10 days is the
      evidence-based band, so B. Cepeda also found that undershooting the gap cost far more than
      overshooting it, so if in doubt, go longer. A is the "never let yourself forget" instinct; a
      one-day gap was best only for a test one week away. C is beyond anything observed: no
      optimum was above about a third of the interval, and she'd fit in only one review. D is
      wrong because Cepeda et al. (2006), across 317 experiments, found that the size of the gap
      matters; the count alone doesn't settle it.
  - q: >-
      Jonas reviews his notes after a week's gap and finds he has forgotten about half. He concludes
      the first study session was wasted and decides to review daily from now on so it never happens
      again. What has he misunderstood?
    options:
      - The partial forgetting is what made the review valuable; he had to reconstruct rather than recognise
      - He should have reread the notes more times in the first session so they stuck the first time
      - Nothing; forgetting means the memory was never stored, and daily review will repair that
      - Weekly gaps only work for simple material; for lecture notes he needs a much shorter gap
    answer: 0
    explain: >-
      A partly forgotten memory has to be rebuilt on review, and rebuilding is retrieval practice,
      one of the two best-supported techniques in the field (spacing is the other). A fresh memory
      is recognised, and recognition doesn't exercise the route. That is the best-supported account
      of why spacing works, though researchers still argue about the mechanism. Either way, the
      data say the gap should be long enough to let some forgetting happen: in Cepeda et al.
      (2008) a one-day gap was best only for a test one week away, and too-short gaps cost more
      than too-long ones. So A. C treats forgetting as failure. B would have raised fluency in
      session one and changed little at a week. D invents a complexity rule; Cepeda's data come
      from verbal material in general, simple lists included but not limited to them.
  - q: >-
      Two friends both want to keep a language for life. Priya relearns her vocabulary in 26
      sessions two weeks apart. Sam relearns it in 13 sessions eight weeks apart. Based on Bahrick
      et al. (1993), what should they expect years later?
    options:
      - Priya will retain much more, because she did twice as many sessions and sessions are what count
      - Sam will retain much more, because a wider gap is always better when the horizon is long
      - Neither will retain much after a few years, since Bahrick found school languages decay to nothing
      - Their retention will be about the same, so Sam got a comparable result for half the sessions
    answer: 3
    explain: >-
      Bahrick, Bahrick, Bahrick and Bahrick (1993) followed four people (the authors themselves)
      for nine years: 13 relearning sessions at 56-day intervals gave retention comparable to 26
      sessions at 14-day intervals, though the wider gap slowed the relearning slightly. So D. Four
      people is a small study and you should hold the number loosely, but it points the same way
      as everything else about long horizons: a comparable result for half the sessions. A counts
      sessions instead of gaps. B overshoots; wider is not always better, and Bahrick's wide-gap
      condition matched rather than beat the closer one. C misreads Bahrick (1984): in a
      cross-sectional study of 733 people, school Spanish declined for the first three to six years
      and then held nearly unchanged for up to 30, the "permastore". What predicted how much
      survived was mainly the original level of training; later rehearsal had little effect.
  # --- Interleaving, with boundary conditions (2) ---
  - q: >-
      A maths teacher gives one class a worksheet of ten volume problems all using the same solid,
      then ten of the next solid, and so on (blocked). Another class gets the same forty problems
      shuffled. During the session the blocked class looks much stronger. What will the test a week
      later most likely show?
    options:
      - The blocked class keeps its lead, since it practised each formula to mastery before moving on
      - The interleaved class wins by a wide margin, because every problem forced a choice of formula
      - The classes score about the same, since they worked the same forty problems with the same answers
      - The interleaved class wins only on problems mixing two solids and loses on the single-solid ones
    answer: 1
    explain: >-
      This is the pattern from Rohrer and Taylor (2007), whose four-solids experiment had 18
      students: blocked practice looked better during the session (about 89% vs 60% correct) and
      collapsed a week later (20% vs 63% for interleaved, d = 1.34). That's a small study, but the
      preregistered trial across 54 seventh-grade classes found the same pattern at one month, 61%
      vs 38%, d = 0.83 (Rohrer et al. 2020). So B. The mechanism: blocked practice lets you skip
      the hardest step, deciding which method applies, because the worksheet tells you.
      Interleaving forces that choice every time. A mistakes in-session performance for learning
      (lesson 1 again). C assumes order doesn't matter. D imagines the benefit is confined to mixed
      problems; it shows up on ordinary problems because choosing the method is the skill being
      tested.
  - q: >-
      Having learned that interleaving beats blocking, Ana applies it to everything. She shuffles
      her French vocabulary lists with unrelated words, and reads her history textbook chapters in
      a mixed order, one section from each. For which of these does the evidence support her?
    options:
      - The vocabulary but not the textbook, since lists are discrete items that are easy to shuffle
      - Both; interleaving is the better default for any material, whatever its structure
      - Neither; interleaving helps when the things you mix are confusable, and neither of hers are
      - The textbook but not the vocabulary, since prose has more structure to mix across chapters
    answer: 2
    explain: >-
      Brunmair and Richter (2019) pooled 59 studies: g = 0.42 overall, but 0.67 for paintings,
      0.34 for maths, mixed and nonsignificant for expository text, and −0.39 for their "words"
      category (names in conceptual categories, pronunciation rules, translations), where blocking
      won. The closest thing to Ana's vocabulary plan, Hausman and Kornell (2014) mixing anatomy
      terms with Indonesian words, found no reliable effect either way. Interleaving works by
      forcing you to discriminate between confusable categories or choose between similar methods.
      Unrelated words and separate history chapters give you nothing to discriminate, so C. A, B
      and D each treat interleaving as a slogan rather than a mechanism with a boundary, which is
      why Dunlosky et al. (2013) rated it only moderate: a small literature "with enough null
      effects to raise concern". What Ana should interleave is confusable things: three French
      tenses, three chart types, three formulas.
  # --- Worked examples and self-explanation (2) ---
  - q: >-
      Wei is starting algebra and has been told that "struggling first is the best way to learn".
      He attempts twenty problems cold, getting most wrong, then checks the answers. His sister,
      also a beginner, studies ten fully worked solutions and then attempts ten similar problems.
      What does the worked-example research predict, and what is the caveat?
    options:
      - His sister learns more, faster, with fewer errors; the advantage fades as she becomes proficient
      - Wei learns more, because productive struggle is the core of what makes a difficulty desirable
      - They learn the same amount, since both end up with feedback on the same number of problems
      - His sister learns more, but only if she rereads each worked solution several times first
    answer: 0
    explain: >-
      Sweller and Cooper (1985): in their third experiment, algebra students who had studied worked
      examples then solved similar problems in about 44 seconds each, against 78 seconds for
      students who had only solved problems, with about a ninth of the errors. The advantage did
      not extend to problems that varied from the studied ones. So A. The mechanism is cognitive
      load: a novice attempting problems cold spends working memory searching rather than learning
      the pattern. The caveat is the expertise-reversal effect (Kalyuga et al. 2003): guidance that
      helps novices becomes redundant or harmful as they become proficient, so worked examples
      should fade as skill grows. B confuses desirable difficulty with any difficulty; Bjork and
      Bjork (2011) say a difficulty is desirable only if the learner can meet it. C assumes
      feedback is all that matters, but twenty wrong attempts is a lot of searching and very little
      pattern. D replaces studying with rereading, which is not what makes examples work;
      explaining each step to yourself is, as the next question shows.
  - q: >-
      Reading a dense passage on how the circulatory system works, Ella pauses after each sentence
      to ask "what does this add, why would that be true, and how does it connect to the last one?"
      Fatima reads the passage twice and highlights the key terms. Which comparison does the
      evidence support?
    options:
      - Fatima learns more, because two readings plus highlighting means more exposure to the key terms
      - They learn about the same, provided Fatima's highlighting is selective rather than wholesale
      - Ella learns more only if she already has a biology background; otherwise the questions are noise
      - Ella learns more, because self-explaining forces her to fill the gaps the author skipped
    answer: 3
    explain: >-
      Chi, de Leeuw, Chiu and LaVancher (1994) prompted eighth-graders to self-explain a
      circulatory-system text and found they learned more than students who read it twice. Chi et
      al. (1989) had earlier found, with eight university students, that the good physics students
      explained worked examples to themselves and monitored their understanding accurately, while
      the poor ones reread and searched the example for something to use. Dunlosky et al. (2013)
      rate self-explanation and elaborative interrogation moderate, and rereading and highlighting
      low, with highlighting scored negative on some criterion tasks because it can hurt inference.
      So D. A counts exposures; you remember what you think about, not what passed in front of
      you. B: selective highlighting is still recognition, not thought. C contains a real
      half-truth: elaborative interrogation does need some prior knowledge to connect to, but that
      limits how much it helps; it isn't a switch that turns it off, and the 1994 participants were
      schoolchildren, not specialists.
  # --- Deliberate practice, including its contested status (2) ---
  - q: >-
      Two guitarists each practise an hour a day for a year. Kim plays through songs she already
      knows. Jo picks the one chord transition she keeps fumbling, drills it slowly with a
      metronome, records herself, listens back, and speeds up only when it is clean. By Ericsson's
      definition, which is deliberate practice, and why?
    options:
      - Neither, because deliberate practice by definition needs a teacher present in the room
      - Both; an hour a day for a year is deliberate practice whatever you do with the hour
      - Jo's, because it targets a specific weakness at the edge of her ability with feedback
      - Kim's, because playing whole songs builds the mental representations Ericsson described
    answer: 2
    explain: >-
      Ericsson, Krampe and Tesch-Römer (1993) defined deliberate practice as effortful activity
      with specific goals, at the edge of current ability, with immediate feedback, structured by
      someone who knows the domain, and distinct from play and from paid performance. Jo's routine
      has the goal, the edge, and the feedback loop (the recording), so C. Kim's has none; without
      feedback, repetition entrenches whatever you already do. B measures hours, which is the
      "10,000 hours" mistake. D misreads the mechanism: representations are built by attacking what
      you can't do, not by rehearsing what you can. A overreads "designed by someone who knows the
      domain"; the design can come from a teacher, a method book, or a well-informed learner, and
      the feedback from a recording.
  - q: >-
      A friend tells you "the science is settled: 10,000 hours of practice makes anyone an expert".
      Which reply states the evidence honestly?
    options:
      - Agree; the Berlin violin study established a 10,000-hour threshold and it has replicated since
      - Say the opposite is settled; practice explains almost nothing, and talent is what really matters
      - Say it's contested; practice is the best-supported way to improve, but how much it explains is unresolved
      - Say practice explains everything in music and sport but nothing in professions, so it depends on the field
    answer: 2
    explain: >-
      Ericsson, Krampe and Tesch-Römer (1993) reported that the best violinists had accumulated
      about 7,410 hours of solitary practice by 18, against 3,420 for the future teachers, and
      over 10,000 by 20. Those are averages, and Ericsson himself rejected the threshold reading;
      "10,000 hours" is Gladwell's. Macnamara, Hambrick and Oswald (2014) found practice explains
      26% of performance variance in games, 21% in music, 18% in sports, 4% in education and under
      1% in professions (that last figure rests on only seven effect sizes and isn't significant).
      Ericsson and Harwell (2019) replied that the meta-analysis counted almost any practice, not
      deliberate practice as defined. The preregistered replication with 39 Cleveland violinists
      (Macnamara and Maitra 2019) found the best had practised somewhat less than the good (8,224
      vs 9,844 hours by 18), a difference that was not statistically significant, with practice
      explaining about 26% of variance against the 48% they computed from the original data. That
      is a live, contested question, so C. A and B each pick a side and call it settled. D
      overstates both ends.
  # --- Myths (2) ---
  - q: >-
      A tutoring company classifies each student as a visual, auditory or kinaesthetic learner and
      matches lessons to the label. A parent asks whether this is supported. What is the accurate
      answer?
    options:
      - No; preferences exist, but the crossover that would justify matching has virtually never been found
      - It's contested among researchers, so the company is entitled to its position until that's resolved
      - Yes for children but not for adults, since styles are fixed early and fade with experience
      - Yes; students clearly have preferences, and over 90% of teachers endorse matching to them
    answer: 0
    explain: >-
      Pashler, McDaniel, Rohrer and Bjork (2008) set the standard: to justify matching, you need a
      crossover interaction, where the best method for one style is not the best for another, and
      they found "virtually no evidence" for one. Preferences are real; the claim that teaching to
      them improves learning is not supported. So A. D cites Dekker et al. (2012), where over 90% of
      UK and Dutch teachers endorsed learning styles, but popularity among teachers is not evidence
      about learning. B is false balance: among researchers the meshing hypothesis is not
      contested, and this course doesn't both-sides it. C invents an age split. What does have
      evidence is matching the format to the content, not to the learner: diagrams for spatial
      material, sound for music.
  - q: >-
      Marta has adopted three tips from an article on "science-backed study hacks": print notes in
      a hard-to-read font because "difficulty helps memory", always take notes by hand because
      "typing is proven worse", and skip sleep before a test because "sleep barely matters, the
      correlation with grades is tiny". Which of these does the evidence actually support?
    options:
      - All three; each of them rests on a published study, which is what "science-backed" means
      - The hard font and the handwriting, but not the sleep claim, since consolidation is established
      - The handwriting and the sleep claim, but not the hard font, which has failed to replicate
      - None as stated; the font effect is null, the handwriting result didn't replicate, and sleep still matters
    answer: 3
    explain: >-
      Hard fonts: Rummer, Schweppe and Schwede (2016) found no benefit across three experiments,
      Xie, Zhou and Liu's (2018) meta-analysis puts the effect near zero, and the Sans Forgetica
      studies of 2020 to 2022 found no usable benefit (three clean nulls and three results that
      appeared only under conditions unlike real study). A difficulty is desirable only if it
      engages the processing you want, and a hard font doesn't. Handwriting: Mueller and
      Oppenheimer (2014) did not replicate in Morehead, Dunlosky and Rawson (2019) or Urry et al.
      (2021). Sleep: Dewald et al. (2010) did find sleep duration correlated with school
      performance at only about r = 0.07 across 15,199 children and adolescents, but that's the
      smallest of three correlations they report (sleep quality 0.10, sleepiness −0.13), and Cordi
      and Rasch (2021) say sleep's memory effects are smaller and less reliable than assumed, not
      absent; consolidation during sleep is established (Rasch and Born 2013). "Sleep enough,
      don't expect magic" is the position, not "skip it". So D. A, B and C each accept at least
      one claim on the strength of a single study, which is the habit this course tried to break.

This test covers the whole course. Twenty questions, one attempt, no time limit beyond the 30-minute guide.

## The rules

- **Closed book.** Don't look back at the lessons, your notes, or anything else while you're answering. The point is to find out what you can retrieve, not what you can recognise. If you open a lesson mid-test, you'll get a score that flatters you and tells you nothing.
- **No time limit.** The 30 minutes is a guide. Take longer if you need it; don't rush a case question because a clock is running.
- **Pass mark is 80%**, which is 16 of 20. That's higher than the lesson quizzes because this test comes after spacing has done its work, and because the whole course is about not mistaking familiarity for knowledge.
- **You can retake it after 24 hours.** The gap is deliberate. If you fail, go back to the lessons for the questions you missed, do the retrieval exercises rather than rereading, and come back the next day. A retake five minutes later would test your memory of the answer key, not your understanding.

## What a pass means

Passing means you can take a described learner, a study habit, a schedule, or a claim from a "study hacks" article and say what will actually happen and why, using the evidence rather than the feeling. That's the skill the course was built to give you. It doesn't mean you now study this way: the lesson quizzes and this test measure what you know, and the two-week project measures whether you can run it. Do the project.

## After the test

Whatever your score, read every explanation, including the ones for questions you got right. Several of the distractors are half-truths that a learner who did well on the quizzes still falls for, and the explanations name the specific studies you'll want when someone tells you rereading works fine.
