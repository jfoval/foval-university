  # --- Learning vs performance (3) ---
  - q: >-
      Priya reads her anatomy chapter four times in one evening. By the fourth pass the text feels
      completely familiar and she can predict what comes next. She rates her knowledge as 9/10 and
      stops. What is the most likely state of her memory a week later?
    options:
      - She will remember most of it; four passes is thorough study and familiarity is a good sign
      - She will remember far less than she predicts, because rereading raised retrieval strength and fluency without doing much for storage strength
      - She will remember it well but only if she also slept properly that night
      - She will have learned it about as well as someone who self-tested, since total time on the material was the same
    answer: 1
    explain: >-
      Rereading makes text feel familiar, and familiarity gets read as knowledge. Bjork and Bjork
      (2011) separate storage strength (how entrenched a memory is) from retrieval strength (how
      accessible it is right now). Four passes in one evening push retrieval strength and fluency
      up, which is exactly what her 9/10 rating is tracking, while adding little to storage
      strength. Option A trusts the feeling the course exists to warn you about. Option C makes
      sleep the deciding factor; sleep helps consolidation, but it does not rescue shallow
      encoding. Option D is wrong because equal time does not mean equal learning: Roediger and
      Karpicke (2006) found repeated study lost to repeated testing at one week (40% vs 61%) with
      the same time on task.
  - q: >-
      Two learners study the same word list for the same total time. Marco crams it the night before
      a test tomorrow. Dana spreads it across a week and tests herself. Which statement about the
      results is correct?
    options:
      - Marco will do better tomorrow and Dana will do better a month from now, so each should decide which they actually need
      - Dana will do better both tomorrow and next month, because spacing and testing are better in every condition
      - Marco will do better both tomorrow and next month, because he encoded the material in one concentrated block
      - They will score about the same at both times, since total study time is the same
    answer: 0
    explain: >-
      Performance and learning can move in opposite directions (Soderstrom and Bjork 2015). In
      Roediger and Karpicke (2006), restudy beat testing five minutes later (81% vs 75%) and lost a
      week later (42% vs 56%). Cramming genuinely works for tomorrow; it is next month it fails. So
      the honest answer is A: the learner has to decide which they want. Option B overstates the
      case; spacing and testing usually look worse in the short term, which is why people abandon
      them. Option C confuses the short-term win with learning. Option D repeats the "time is what
      matters" error.
  - q: >-
      In Kornell and Bjork's painting study, learners who saw artists' works interleaved were better
      at identifying new paintings by those artists than learners who saw them blocked. What did
      most of the participants believe afterwards, once they had seen their own results?
    options:
      - Most recognised that interleaving had worked better for them
      - Most had no preference, since the results were shown to them
      - Most still believed blocking had worked better, showing the fluency illusion survives contact with the evidence
      - Most believed interleaving worked better for others but not for themselves
    answer: 2
    explain: >-
      Of the 72 participants with a preference, 64 said massing (blocking) had worked better, even
      after seeing that interleaving had produced their own better scores (Kornell and Bjork 2008).
      That is the point of the example: the illusion is not corrected by results, because it is
      built from how study feels while you are doing it. Blocked study feels smooth; interleaved
      study feels harder. The other options assume people update their beliefs when shown data,
      which in this study they mostly did not. The practical lesson is that you should not use
      "which method felt better" as your guide; use a delayed test.
  # --- Memory and cognitive load (2) ---
  - q: >-
      Tom understands every step of a statistics lecture while the lecturer is talking. That night,
      alone with the slides he copied word for word, he cannot follow any of it. What best explains
      the gap?
    options:
      - He has a smaller working memory than most people and should copy the slides more carefully next time
      - He was multitasking during the lecture without noticing, so nothing was encoded
      - Understanding fades overnight for everyone; he needs to reread the slides the same day
      - The lecturer was holding the chunks for him; alone, the material exceeds his roughly four-chunk working memory, and copied slides give him nothing to reconstruct from
    answer: 3
    explain: >-
      Working memory holds about four chunks (Cowan 2001, revising Miller's seven). A lecturer
      supplies the structure that groups elements into chunks, so the material fits while they
      talk. Alone, Tom has to build the chunks himself, and slides copied verbatim record the
      lecturer's words without recording the connections that made them make sense. Notes that
      reconstruct (in your own words, with the reasons) help; notes that copy do not. Option A
      blames working-memory capacity, but individual differences are small and chunk size is the
      lever. Option B invents a cause. Option C turns a chunking problem into a timing problem;
      rereading the same slides the same day would leave him equally stuck.
  - q: >-
      A tutorial on compound interest opens with a two-paragraph history of banking, uses a
      different symbol for the interest rate on each page, and puts the formula in an image the
      reader has to scroll away from the worked numbers to see. A student finds it exhausting.
      Which change would cut the most extraneous load?
    options:
      - Cut the history, use one symbol throughout, and keep the formula next to the numbers it is applied to
      - Simplify the mathematics so the formula has fewer terms
      - Ask the student to work through the tutorial faster so the pieces stay in mind together
      - Add a summary quiz at the end so the student retrieves what they read
    answer: 0
    explain: >-
      Cognitive load theory (Sweller, van Merriënboer and Paas 2019) separates intrinsic load,
      which comes from how many elements interact in the material itself, from extraneous load,
      which comes from how the material is presented. The history, the shifting notation and the
      split between formula and numbers are all presentation problems, so A removes extraneous load
      without touching the content. Option B reduces intrinsic load by teaching less, which is a
      different trade. Option C makes the overload worse. Option D is good practice in general
      (lesson 3) but does nothing about the exhaustion caused by the presentation.
  # --- Retrieval practice (4) ---
  - q: >-
      Aisha reads a passage once, then spends the rest of her study session closing the book and
      writing out everything she can remember, twice. Ben reads the same passage four times in the
      same total time. Tested five minutes later and again one week later, what happens?
    options:
      - Ben wins at five minutes and Aisha wins at one week, and Ben will feel more confident throughout
      - Aisha wins at both times, because retrieval is always superior
      - Ben wins at both times, because he saw the material more often
      - They score the same at one week; retrieval only matters for short delays
    answer: 0
    explain: >-
      This is Roediger and Karpicke (2006). Experiment 1: at five minutes restudy led (81% vs 75%);
      at two days testing led (68% vs 54%); at one week testing led (56% vs 42%). Experiment 2: a
      study-test-test-test group recalled 61% at one week against 40% for study-study-study-study,
      and the repeated studiers were the more confident group. So Ben gets the short-term edge and
      the confidence; Aisha gets the memory. Option B ignores the short-delay result, which is what
      makes people distrust retrieval. Options C and D read the five-minute result as if it were the
      one-week result.
  - q: >-
      Leo has to understand a science text well enough to answer inference questions, not just
      recall it. His friend says concept mapping is the better method for that because it "builds
      understanding", while retrieval is just for facts. Based on Karpicke and Blunt (2011), what
      should Leo expect?
    options:
      - They will perform about equally, since both are active methods
      - Concept mapping will win on inference questions and retrieval will win on verbatim ones
      - Concept mapping will win overall because it takes longer and engages more thought
      - Retrieval practice will beat concept mapping on both verbatim and inference questions, and would win even if the final test were itself a concept map
    answer: 3
    explain: >-
      Karpicke and Blunt (2011) tested exactly this with a 276-word science text and a one-week
      short-answer test containing both verbatim and inference questions. Retrieval practice scored
      0.67 against 0.45 for concept mapping (d = 1.50), and retrieval won even when the final test
      was a concept map. Students predicted the opposite. Option B is the intuition the study
      refuted. Option A treats "active" as the operative feature; what matters is that retrieval
      forces reconstruction. Option C assumes more time and more effort equal more learning, which
      lesson 1 already showed is unreliable.
  - q: >-
      Sofia uses flashcards. She goes through the whole deck once, sees that she got about
      two-thirds right, decides she "knows where she stands", and then rereads the entire chapter.
      What is the problem, and what should she do instead?
    options:
      - There is no problem; she has combined testing and rereading, which covers both bases
      - She is self-testing to check rather than to learn, like most students who self-test; she should retest the cards she missed until she can recall them, and only reread what she still cannot retrieve
      - Flashcards are for vocabulary and dates; for a chapter she should switch to concept mapping
      - She should reread the chapter first and only then test, so she does not get answers wrong and learn them wrongly
    answer: 1
    explain: >-
      Kornell and Bjork (2007) asked 472 UCLA students who self-tested why they did it: only 18%
      said because it teaches more; about 70% did it to check what they knew. Checking then
      rereading everything throws away the benefit. The behaviour that learns is to treat a miss
      as the start of practice: retest what you missed, feed back, retest, and reread only the
      residue. Option A rereads material she already retrieved, which is the low-utility step.
      Option C repeats the "flashcards are for kids" myth; retrieval works for meaningful material
      (Karpicke and Blunt 2011). Option D worries about errors, but errors followed by feedback
      improve later recall; they are not contaminating.
  - q: >-
      Rahul has 40 pharmacology terms to know for an exam in five weeks. He wants a retrieval
      schedule with the best evidence behind it. Which plan matches the "successive relearning"
      prescription from Rawson and Dunlosky (2011)?
    options:
      - Recall each term correctly once, then reread the list before the exam
      - Recall each term correctly ten times in one sitting so it is firmly stored
      - Recall each term correctly three times in the first session, then in three later spaced sessions relearn each to one correct recall
      - Reread the list daily for five weeks and test once the night before
    answer: 2
    explain: >-
      Rawson and Dunlosky (2011), with 533 students, found the efficient prescription was three
      correct recalls per item initially, then relearning each item to one correct recall in three
      spaced sessions. That combines retrieval with spacing. Option A stops after a single success
      and switches to rereading. Option B piles all the retrievals into one session, which is
      massed practice wearing a retrieval costume; the later sessions are where the spacing effect
      comes from. Option D is the most common student plan and the one Dunlosky et al. (2013) rate
      low utility.
  # --- Spacing (3) ---
  - q: >-
      Nadia has an exam in 30 days and wants to space her reviews of a topic she has just learned.
      Using the Cepeda et al. (2008) rule of thumb, roughly how far apart should her review sessions
      be?
    options:
      - About one day, so nothing is forgotten between sessions
      - About 3 to 6 days
      - About 15 days, halfway to the exam
      - It does not matter as long as the total number of reviews is the same
    answer: 1
    explain: >-
      Cepeda et al. (2008) found the optimal gap is a fraction of the retention interval, and the
      fraction shrinks as the horizon grows: about 20–40% of a one-week delay, 5–10% of a one-year
      delay, roughly 10–20% as a working rule. Ten to twenty percent of 30 days is 3 to 6 days.
      Option A is the "never let yourself forget" instinct; without some forgetting, review is
      recognition rather than reconstruction. Option C is too wide for a one-month horizon; she
      would only get one review in. Option D is wrong because Cepeda et al. (2006), across 317
      experiments, found gap size matters, not just count.
  - q: >-
      Jonas reviews his notes after a week's gap and finds he has forgotten about half. He concludes
      the first study session was wasted and decides to review daily from now on so it never happens
      again. What has he misunderstood?
    options:
      - The partial forgetting is what made the review valuable, because he had to reconstruct rather than recognise; daily review would turn reviews back into recognition
      - He should have reread the notes more times in the first session to make them stick
      - Nothing; forgetting means the memory was never properly stored, and daily review will fix it
      - Weekly gaps only work for simple material; for notes he should use a shorter gap
    answer: 0
    explain: >-
      A partly forgotten memory has to be rebuilt on review, and rebuilding is retrieval practice,
      the strongest tool there is. A fresh memory is merely recognised, and recognition does not
      exercise the route. That is the mechanism behind the spacing effect, and why the best gap is
      long enough to allow some forgetting. Option C treats forgetting as failure. Option B would
      have raised fluency in session one and changed little at a week. Option D invents a
      complexity rule the evidence does not support; Cepeda's fractions apply across verbal
      material generally.
  - q: >-
      Two friends both want to keep a language for life. Priya relearns her vocabulary in 26
      sessions two weeks apart. Sam relearns it in 13 sessions eight weeks apart. Based on Bahrick
      et al. (1993), what should they expect years later?
    options:
      - Priya will retain much more, because she did twice as many sessions
      - Sam will retain much more, because wide gaps are always better
      - Neither will retain anything after a few years, since Bahrick found language decays to zero
      - Their retention will be about the same, so Sam got the same result for half the sessions
    answer: 3
    explain: >-
      Bahrick et al. (1993), a nine-year study, found 13 relearning sessions at 56-day intervals
      matched 26 sessions at 14-day intervals. Same result, half the work: that is what a long
      horizon does to the optimal gap. Option A counts sessions instead of gaps. Option B overshoots;
      wider is not always better, the gap should be a fraction of the horizon, and Bahrick's
      condition happened to match rather than beat the closer one. Option C misreads Bahrick (1984):
      Spanish learned in school decayed for three to five years and then held for 25 to 30, the
      "permastore". Decay is steepest early; what survives is what was relearned on a spaced
      schedule.
  # --- Interleaving, with boundary conditions (2) ---
  - q: >-
      A maths teacher gives one class a worksheet of ten volume problems all using the same solid,
      then ten of the next solid, and so on (blocked). Another class gets the same forty problems
      shuffled. During the session the blocked class looks much stronger. What will the test a week
      later most likely show?
    options:
      - The blocked class keeps its lead, since it practised each formula to mastery before moving on
      - The interleaved class wins by a wide margin, because shuffling forced them to choose which formula applied on every problem
      - The classes score about the same, since they did identical problems
      - The interleaved class wins only on problems that mix two solids, and loses on the rest
    answer: 1
    explain: >-
      This is Rohrer and Taylor (2007): blocked practice looked better during the session (about
      89% vs 60% correct) and collapsed a week later (20% vs 63% for interleaved). The preregistered
      RCT across 54 seventh-grade classes found the same pattern at one month, 61% vs 38%, d = 0.83
      (Rohrer et al. 2020). The mechanism: blocked practice lets you skip the hardest step,
      deciding which method applies, because the worksheet tells you. Interleaving forces that
      choice every time. Option A mistakes in-session performance for learning (lesson 1 again).
      Option C assumes order does not matter. Option D imagines the benefit is confined to mixed
      problems; it shows up on the ordinary problems because strategy choice is the skill being
      tested.
  - q: >-
      Having learned that interleaving beats blocking, Ana applies it to everything. She shuffles
      her French vocabulary lists with unrelated words, and reads her history textbook chapters in
      a mixed order, one section from each. For which of these does the evidence support her?
    options:
      - The vocabulary lists but not the textbook, since lists are discrete items
      - Both; interleaving is the better default for any material
      - Neither; the meta-analysis found interleaving neutral for expository text and actually favoured blocking for word lists, because interleaving helps when categories are confusable
      - The textbook but not the vocabulary, since prose has more structure to mix
    answer: 2
    explain: >-
      Brunmair and Richter (2019) pooled 59 studies: an overall g of 0.42, but g = 0.67 for
      paintings, 0.34 for maths, nonsignificant for expository text, and g = −0.39 for word lists,
      where blocking won. Interleaving works by forcing discrimination between confusable
      categories and choice between similar methods. Unrelated vocabulary and separate history
      chapters offer nothing to discriminate, so there is no gain and, for lists, a cost. Ana should
      interleave confusable things: three French tenses, three chart types, three formulas. Options
      A, C and D each treat interleaving as a slogan rather than a mechanism with a boundary, which
      is why Dunlosky et al. (2013) rated it only moderate.
  # --- Worked examples and self-explanation (2) ---
  - q: >-
      Wei is starting algebra and has been told that "struggling first is the best way to learn".
      He attempts twenty problems cold, getting most wrong, then checks the answers. His sister,
      also a beginner, studies ten fully worked solutions and then attempts ten similar problems.
      What does the worked-example research predict, and what is the one caveat?
    options:
      - His sister learns more, faster and with fewer errors, but the advantage shrinks on problems that vary from the studied ones and disappears or reverses once she becomes proficient
      - Wei learns more, because productive struggle is the core of desirable difficulty
      - They learn the same amount, since both end up with feedback on the same problems
      - His sister learns more only if she rereads each solution several times before attempting
    answer: 0
    explain: >-
      Sweller and Cooper (1985) found algebra students who studied worked examples solved later
      problems in about half the time with a fifth of the errors, but the advantage did not extend
      to problems that varied from the studied ones. The mechanism is cognitive load: a novice
      attempting problems spends working memory searching rather than learning the pattern. The
      caveat is the expertise-reversal effect (Kalyuga et al. 2003): guidance that helps novices
      becomes redundant or harmful for experts, so worked examples should fade as skill grows.
      Option B confuses desirable difficulty with any difficulty; Bjork and Bjork (2011) say a
      difficulty is only desirable if the learner can meet it. Option D would replace studying with
      rereading, which is not what makes examples work.
  - q: >-
      Reading a dense passage on how the circulatory system works, Ella pauses after each sentence
      to ask "what does this add, why would that be true, and how does it connect to the last one?"
      Fatima reads the passage twice and highlights the key terms. Which comparison does the
      evidence support?
    options:
      - Fatima learns more, because two readings plus highlighting means more exposure to the key terms
      - They learn the same, provided Fatima's highlighting is selective
      - Ella learns more only if she already has a biology background; otherwise the questions are just noise
      - Ella learns more, because self-explanation forces her to fill the gaps the author skipped; Chi's studies found exactly this, while rereading and highlighting are rated low utility
    answer: 3
    explain: >-
      Chi et al. (1994) prompted eighth-graders to self-explain a circulatory-system text and found
      they learned more than students who read it twice. Chi et al. (1989) had earlier found that
      good physics students spontaneously explain worked examples to themselves and monitor
      accurately, while poor ones reread and copy. Dunlosky et al. (2013) rate self-explanation and
      elaborative interrogation moderate and rate rereading and highlighting low, with highlighting
      scored negative for inference tasks. Option C contains a real half-truth: elaborative
      interrogation does need some prior knowledge to connect to, but that is a limit on its size,
      not a switch that turns it off; the 1994 participants were schoolchildren, not specialists.
  # --- Deliberate practice, including its contested status (2) ---
  - q: >-
      Two guitarists each practise an hour a day for a year. Kim plays through songs she already
      knows. Jo picks the one chord transition she keeps fumbling, drills it slowly with a
      metronome, records herself, listens back, and speeds up only when it is clean. By Ericsson's
      definition, which is deliberate practice, and why?
    options:
      - Neither, because deliberate practice requires a teacher present in the room
      - Both; an hour a day for a year is deliberate practice regardless of content
      - Jo's, because it targets a specific sub-skill at the edge of her ability with immediate feedback; Kim's is closer to play, which Ericsson explicitly separated from practice
      - Kim's, because playing whole songs builds the mental representations that Ericsson said were the mechanism
    answer: 2
    explain: >-
      Ericsson, Krampe and Tesch-Römer (1993) defined deliberate practice as effortful activity
      with specific goals, at the edge of current ability, with immediate feedback, structured by
      someone who knows the domain, and distinct from play and from paid performance. Jo's routine
      has the goal, the edge, and the feedback loop (the recording). Kim's has none; without
      feedback, repetition entrenches whatever you already do. Option B measures hours, which is
      the "10,000 hours" mistake. Option D misreads the mechanism: representations are built by
      attacking what you cannot do, not by rehearsing what you can. Option A overreads "designed by
      someone who knows the domain"; the design can come from a teacher, a method book, or a
      well-informed learner, and the feedback from a recording.
  - q: >-
      A friend tells you "the science is settled: 10,000 hours of practice makes anyone an expert".
      Which reply states the evidence honestly?
    options:
      - Agree; Ericsson's Berlin violin study established the 10,000-hour threshold and it has been replicated
      - Say the opposite is settled; practice explains almost nothing and talent is what matters
      - Say it is contested; deliberate practice is the best-supported way to improve, but meta-analyses find it explains a minority of the variance in most domains, the original violin result did not replicate cleanly, and "10,000 hours" was Gladwell's reading of an average, not a threshold Ericsson endorsed
      - Say practice explains everything in music and sport but nothing in professions, so it depends on the field
    answer: 2
    explain: >-
      The 1993 study reported the best violinists had accumulated about 7,410 hours of solitary
      practice by 18, versus 3,420 for future teachers, and over 10,000 by 20; those are averages,
      and Ericsson himself rejected the threshold reading. Macnamara, Hambrick and Oswald (2014)
      found practice explains 26% of performance variance in games, 21% in music, 18% in sports, 4%
      in education and under 1% in professions. Ericsson (2016) replied that the meta-analysis
      counted almost any practice, not deliberate practice as defined. The preregistered replication
      with 39 Cleveland violinists (Macnamara and Maitra 2019) found the best had practised less
      than the good (8,224 vs 9,844 hours by 18), with practice explaining 26% of variance rather
      than the original 48%. That is a live, contested empirical question. Options A and B each
      pick a side and call it settled. Option D overstates both ends.
  # --- Myths (2) ---
  - q: >-
      A tutoring company classifies each student as a visual, auditory or kinaesthetic learner and
      matches lessons to the label. A parent asks whether this is supported. What is the accurate
      answer?
    options:
      - No; preferences exist, but the test that would justify matching, a crossover where the best method for one style is not the best for another, has virtually never been found despite extensive search
      - It is contested among researchers, so the company is entitled to its position
      - Yes for children, no for adults, since styles are fixed early
      - Yes; students clearly have preferences, and over 90% of teachers endorse matching, so the practice is well founded
    answer: 0
    explain: >-
      Pashler, McDaniel, Rohrer and Bjork (2008) set the standard: to justify matching, you need a
      crossover interaction, and they found "virtually no evidence" for one. Preferences are real;
      the claim that teaching to them improves learning is not supported. Option D cites Dekker et
      al. (2012), where over 90% of UK and Dutch teachers endorsed learning styles, but popularity
      among teachers is not evidence about learning. Option B is false balance: among researchers the
      meshing hypothesis is not contested, and this course does not both-sides it. Option C
      invents an age split. What does have evidence is matching the format to the content, not to
      the learner: diagrams for spatial material, sound for music.
  - q: >-
      Marta has adopted three tips from an article on "science-backed study hacks": print notes in
      a hard-to-read font because "difficulty helps memory", always take notes by hand because
      "typing is proven worse", and skip sleep before a test because "sleep barely matters, the
      correlation with grades is tiny". Which of these does the evidence actually support?
    options:
      - All three; each is based on a published study
      - The hard font and the handwriting, but not the sleep claim
      - The handwriting and the sleep claim, but not the hard font
      - None as stated; hard fonts are the textbook example of an undesirable difficulty, the handwriting result failed to replicate, and while the sleep-grades correlation is indeed only about 0.07, consolidation during sleep is real and no one should skip it
    answer: 3
    explain: >-
      Hard fonts: Rummer, Schweppe and Schwede (2016) found no benefit across four experiments,
      and five Sans Forgetica studies in 2020–22 found none either; a difficulty is only desirable
      if it engages the processing you want, and a hard font does not. Handwriting: Mueller and
      Oppenheimer (2014) did not replicate in Morehead, Dunlosky and Rawson (2019) or Urry et al.
      (2021). Sleep: Dewald et al. (2010) did find sleep duration correlated with school performance
      at only r ≈ 0.07 across 15,199 children, and Cordi and Rasch (2021) report sleep's memory
      effects are smaller and less robust than assumed, but consolidation during sleep is
      established (Rasch and Born 2013). The honest position is "sleep enough, don't expect magic",
      not "skip it". Every option except D accepts at least one claim on the strength of a single
      study, which is the habit this course tried to break.

This test covers the whole course. Twenty questions, one attempt, no time limit beyond the 30-minute guide.

## The rules

- **Closed book.** Don't look back at the lessons, your notes, or anything else while you're answering. The point is to find out what you can retrieve, not what you can recognise. If you open a lesson mid-test, you'll get a score that flatters you and tells you nothing.
- **No time limit.** The 30 minutes is a guide. Take longer if you need it; don't rush a case question because a clock is running.
- **Pass mark is 80%**, which is 16 of 20. That's higher than the lesson quizzes because this test comes after spacing has done its work, and because the whole course is about not mistaking familiarity for knowledge.
- **You can retake it after 24 hours.** The gap is deliberate. If you fail, go back to the lessons for the questions you missed, do the retrieval exercises rather than rereading, and come back the next day. A retake five minutes later would test your memory of the answer key, not your understanding.

## What a pass means

Passing means you can take a described learner, a study habit, a schedule, or a claim from a "study hacks" article and say what will actually happen and why, using the evidence rather than the feeling. That's the skill the course was built to give you. It doesn't mean you now study this way: the lesson quizzes and this test measure what you know, and the two-week project measures whether you can run it. Do the project.

## After the test

Whatever your score, read every explanation, including the ones for questions you got right. Several of the distractors are half-truths that a learner who did well on the quizzes still falls for, and the explanations name the specific studies you'll want when someone tells you rereading works fine.
