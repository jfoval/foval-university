Everything in this course so far has measured what you know about learning. This project measures whether you can do it. You'll pick one real subject, run the method on it for fourteen days, and finish by taking a test you wrote on day 1 and haven't looked at since. The score on that test is the point. Not how the studying felt, not how many hours you logged: what you can retrieve two weeks later.

Budget about ten hours across the two weeks: roughly half an hour on day 1 to set up, twenty to forty minutes a day of study, and an hour at the end to score and write. If you can only manage fifteen minutes a day, that's fine. Log it honestly and the project still works.

## Why the test comes first

You write the delayed self-test before you study, and then you seal it. Two reasons.

First, it's the only way to get an honest measure. If you write the test on day 14, you'll write questions about the things you happen to remember, which is the fluency illusion (lesson 1) running your assessment. Writing it on day 1, when you know least, means the questions come from what the subject demands, not from what stuck.

Second, it forces you to state a goal. "Learn some Spanish" isn't testable. "Conjugate ten irregular verbs in the preterite without looking" is. Writing the test turns a vague intention into something you can pass or fail, which is what lesson 7 said a practice goal has to be.

## Day 1: set up (about 30 minutes)

### Choose a subject

Pick something you actually need or want to know, with a body of material you can study: a course on this site, a language, a certification syllabus, an instrument, a chapter of a textbook, a set of formulas. It must be something you can test yourself on. "Get better at writing" is too loose; "explain the four rules from lesson 2 of Clear Writing and apply them to a paragraph" is right.

Choose something you're near the start of. If you already know it well, the test will be easy and you'll learn nothing about the method.

### Write the plan

Your plan uses **at least three** of these five techniques. Name each one, and say concretely what it looks like in your subject:

- **Retrieval** (lesson 3): what you'll retrieve, how (free recall on a blank page, flashcards, self-set questions, the site's Review page), and when in each session.
- **Spacing** (lesson 4): the horizon you're aiming at, the gap you've calculated from it, and the actual dates. For a 14-day project with a test on day 14, a gap of 2 to 3 days is in the 10–20% range from Cepeda et al. (2008).
- **Interleaving** (lesson 5): which confusable things you'll mix, and a note that they are confusable. If your subject has nothing confusable in it, say so and don't force it; interleaving unrelated material doesn't help.
- **Worked examples and self-explanation** (lesson 6): which solutions or passages you'll study before attempting, and the self-explanation prompts you'll use.
- **Deliberate practice** (lesson 7): the specific sub-skill you can't yet do, the drill, and the feedback source.

Then write the weekly timetable from lesson 8: which days, how many minutes, what happens in each session. Fit it to the hours you actually have. A plan you can't keep is worse than a small one you can.

A plan that names three techniques but describes none of them ("I'll use retrieval, spacing, and interleaving") doesn't count. The rubric asks for what each one looks like on Tuesday.

### Write the 10-item test and seal it

Write ten questions you should be able to answer by day 14 if the plan works. Mix formats: some recall ("what is the formula for..."), some application ("given this sentence, which tense..."). Make at least three of them things you cannot do today, so the test can detect learning.

Write the answers on a separate sheet.

Then seal it: an envelope, a file you don't open, a note you hand to someone else. Do not look at it until day 14. If you catch yourself studying "for the test" by reconstructing what you wrote, that's fine; you're retrieving. Just don't open it.

Record the date you wrote it. The rubric checks this.

## Days 2 to 13: run the plan and keep the log

Every day you study, add a row. Every day you don't, add a row that says so. The log is the evidence for the reflection, so a missed day recorded honestly is worth more than a gap.

Copy this table into your notes and fill it in:

```markdown
| Day 12 |  |  |  |  |
| Day 13 |  |  |  |  |
```

What goes in each column:

- **Date.** The actual date, so the gaps can be checked against your plan.
- **Minutes.** Real minutes, not planned ones. Twelve is a valid entry.
- **Technique(s) used.** From the five above. If you did something else (reread, watched a video), write that too; it's not a failure, it's data.
- **What was retrieved / spaced / interleaved.** Specifics. Not "vocabulary" but "the 15 preterite forms from Monday, recalled on paper, got 11". Not "spacing" but "second pass on chapter 3, three days after the first". This column is what the reflection will be built from.
- **One honest note.** One sentence. What was hard, what you skipped, what surprised you, what felt like it wasn't working. "Felt fluent, then got 4/10 on recall" is the most useful kind of note you can write, because it's lesson 1 happening to you.

If the plan isn't working, change it and write down the change and why. An adjusted plan with a reason scores full marks on the log row. A plan silently abandoned scores one.

## Day 14: take the test and score it

Open the sealed test. Take it closed-book: no notes, no log, nothing. Write your answers before you look at the answer sheet.

Score it out of 10. Then, for each question, note whether you got it right, wrong, or partly right, and which technique in your plan covered that material (or whether none did). This is where you'll find out if the item you interleaved stuck and the item you only reread didn't.

Record the score and the date. If you couldn't resist opening the test early, say so; an honest 6/10 with a note beats a suspicious 10/10.

## The reflection (one page)

Write about a page. Answer these in order, and for every claim you make, point to a row in the log.

1. **What was your score, and which items did you miss?** Match the misses to the plan. Were they covered by a technique, and which one?
2. **Which technique helped most?** Say why, with evidence from the log, not from how it felt. "Retrieval helped most: the six items I free-recalled on days 3, 6 and 10 all scored on day 14; the three I only reread scored 1 of 3" is evidence. "Retrieval felt effective" is an impression.
3. **Where did the fluency illusion show up?** Find at least one log note where you felt you knew something and then didn't. If there isn't one, say whether that's because you avoided it or because you never tested the feeling.
4. **What did you adjust, and was the adjustment right?** If nothing, say what you would adjust now that you've seen the score.
5. **One thing you'd change** if you ran the next two weeks. Be specific: a different gap, a different feedback source, a smaller session, a subject with more confusable material.

## What to submit

Four things, in one document:

1. The plan (day 1), including the timetable.
2. The log (days 2 to 13).
3. The test, your answers, the answer sheet, and your score, with the date you wrote the test and the date you took it.
4. The reflection.

## Rubric

Ten points. Seven to pass.

| Criterion | Points | What 3 (or 2) looks like | What 1 looks like |
|-----------|--------|--------------------------|-------------------|
| **Plan uses at least three techniques** (retrieval, spacing, interleaving, worked examples, deliberate practice) | 3 | Three or more techniques named, each described concretely for this subject: what is retrieved and when, the gap and the dates it produces, which confusable items are mixed, the sub-skill and the feedback source. A timetable that fits the learner's real hours. Someone else could run this plan. | Techniques listed by name without saying what they mean for this subject, or fewer than three, or a timetable that's obviously aspirational. "I will use spacing and retrieval" with nothing else. |
| **Log shows the plan was followed or honestly adjusted** | 3 | A row for every day, including missed days. Specific entries in the "what was retrieved" column. Where the plan changed, the change and the reason are written down. The reader can tell what actually happened. | Rows missing or filled in retrospectively in identical language. "Studied" in the technique column. Days that don't match the plan with no explanation. A log that suggests either nothing happened or it was written on day 14. |
| **Delayed self-test exists and was written before studying** | 2 | Ten items, dated day 1, mixing recall and application, including some things the learner couldn't do on day 1. Taken closed-book on day 14 with answers written before checking. Score recorded. | A test written late, or so easy it couldn't detect learning, or taken open-book, or with no evidence of when it was written. A score with no answers attached. |
| **Reflection cites specific evidence from the log, not impressions** | 2 | Every claim about what worked points to dated log rows and test items. The fluency illusion is located in the learner's own notes. The "one change" is specific enough to act on. | "Retrieval was really useful and I'd recommend it." Claims about what helped with no log rows behind them. A reflection that could have been written without doing the project. |

A note on scoring yourself, if no one else is marking this: use the right-hand column honestly. The project has no value if you give yourself the points. If you're not sure whether the log row is a 2 or a 3, ask whether a stranger reading it could reconstruct your fortnight. If they could, it's a 3.

## What comes next

If the score was good, you've got a system. Keep the log going on the next subject; by week six you'll know your own gaps and your own illusions well enough not to need the template.

If the score was bad, that's also a result, and a more useful one than most people ever get. Read the reflection you wrote, pick the one change, and run another fortnight. The method is the same; the plan is what changes.
