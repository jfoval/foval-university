Think about the last time you practised anything with problem sets: a maths chapter, a grammar unit, a coding tutorial. Almost certainly the practice was arranged the way textbooks arrange it. Ten problems on one method, then ten on the next, then ten on the next. By the tenth problem you were fast and accurate, which felt like mastery. Then the exam mixed everything together and you stared at a question, knowing you could do it, unable to remember which of the three methods it wanted.

That gap is not a memory failure. It is a skill you never practised, because the arrangement of the problems did it for you. This lesson is about that arrangement: why mixing beats grouping, what mixing trains, and where the finding stops.

## The core idea

**Blocked practice** groups problems by type: AAAA BBBB CCCC. **Interleaved practice** mixes them: ABCBACCAB. Same problems, same total time, different order.

The finding, replicated across two decades, is that blocked practice looks better during the session and interleaved practice wins on a delayed test, often by a wide margin. Rohrer & Taylor (2007) had students practise volume formulae for four solids. The blocked group was about 89% correct during practice; the interleaved group only about 60%. A week later, the blocked group scored 20% and the interleaved group 63%.[1]

That was a lab study with a few dozen students. Rohrer, Dedrick & Stershic (2015) took it into a real school: 126 seventh-graders, over three months, in their ordinary maths classes. On a test one day after the last session the interleaved group scored 80% against 64% (d = 0.42). Thirty days later the gap had widened: 74% against 42%, d = 0.79.[2] Notice the direction. The advantage grew with time.

Then Rohrer, Dedrick, Hartwig & Cheung (2020) ran the study the field had been waiting for: a preregistered, cluster-randomised controlled trial across 54 seventh-grade classes over four months. On a test one month after the practice ended, interleaved classes scored 61% and blocked classes 38%, d = 0.83. The teachers implemented it from the worksheets alone, without any training.[3] An effect that survives preregistration, random assignment of whole classes, and untrained delivery is about as robust as classroom evidence gets.

:::callout Why "d" keeps appearing
The d values are effect sizes: the difference between groups in standard deviations. By convention 0.2 is small, 0.5 medium, 0.8 large. The 2020 RCT's 0.83 is unusually large for a change that costs nothing but the order of problems.
:::

## The mechanism: interleaving makes you choose

Why would the same problems in a different order matter so much? Because solving a problem has two steps, and blocked practice quietly does the first one for you.

Step one is deciding which method the problem needs. Step two is executing that method. In a blocked set, the heading tells you the method. When every problem on the page is about the volume of a wedge, you never ask "is this a wedge problem?" You just apply the wedge formula ten times. You get very fluent at step two and get no practice at all at step one.

Every real test, and every real use of knowledge outside a classroom, starts with step one. A patient does not arrive with the diagnosis written on their forehead. Interleaving puts step one back into practice. On every problem you have to look at the surface features, work out what kind of thing it is, and pick the right tool, which is exactly what the delayed test will ask.[1][2]

The second thing interleaving trains is **discrimination**. When problem types sit next to each other, you notice how they differ. Two solids can look similar in a diagram; seeing them back to back teaches you what to attend to. Blocked practice hides the contrast, because the contrasting case is always three pages away. Brunmair & Richter (2019) put this at the centre of their meta-analysis: interleaving works when the categories are confusable, because its job is to teach you to tell them apart.[5]

This is why Bjork & Bjork (2011) list interleaving alongside spacing and testing as a **desirable difficulty**.[6] It makes practice slower and less accurate now, and that slowness is the work of learning being done rather than skipped.

## Worked example: the volume formulae

Take the Rohrer & Taylor setup and run it as a prediction game, as you did with Roediger & Karpicke in lesson 1.

Two groups of students learn to compute the volume of four uncommon solids. Each solid has its own formula. Everyone gets a tutorial on each formula and then practises on the same set of problems.

The blocked group does all the problems for solid A, then all for solid B, then C, then D. The interleaved group does the identical problems shuffled, so a B problem might follow a D problem, which might follow an A.

Before reading on, predict two numbers: which group scores higher during the practice session, and which scores higher on a test a week later.

During practice, the blocked group was about 89% correct and the interleaved group about 60%. A week later, blocked scored 20%, interleaved 63%.[1]

Now read those numbers with lesson 1's vocabulary. The 89% was retrieval strength: the formula was sitting at the front of the mind because the last nine problems had used it. It said nothing about storage strength, and it said nothing about whether the student could pick that formula out from three others a week later. The test measured exactly the two things blocked practice had not trained: identifying the solid and retrieving the matching formula from a full set. The interleaved group had practised that on every single problem, which is why their practice score was lower and their test score three times higher.

## A harder example: the painters

The volume study is about choosing a procedure. Kornell & Bjork (2008) asked whether the same thing happens when there is no procedure at all, only a category to recognise, and they added a twist that exposes a second problem.[4]

Participants studied paintings by 12 artists, six paintings each. For some artists, the six paintings were shown in a block, one after another. For others, the paintings were interleaved with paintings by other artists. The test was not "which of these paintings have you seen?" It was recognition of *new* paintings: here is a picture you have never seen, which of the 12 artists painted it?

There is no formula to retrieve here, only a style to have picked up. The obvious prediction, and the one most participants made, was that seeing six of an artist's works together would give you a better feel for the style. Interleaving should make it harder to build that feel, since the styles would blur into each other.

The result went the other way. Interleaving improved the identification of new paintings. Seeing one artist next to a different one next to the first again made the differences between them salient in a way that six paintings in a row by one artist did not.[4]

Here is the wrinkle. After the test, participants were asked which schedule had helped them more. Of the 72 who expressed a preference, 64 said the blocked schedule, the one that had just performed worse for them, and they said it *after seeing their own results*.[4] The fluency of blocked study was so convincing that it overrode the evidence in front of them. This is the same illusion you met in lesson 1, and it is the reason interleaving is so rarely used by choice: it feels worse while it works better, and the feeling wins unless you have a reason to distrust it.

## Where interleaving stops working

"Mix everything up" is not the lesson. Brunmair & Richter (2019) pooled 59 studies and found an overall advantage for interleaving of g = 0.42, but the average hides very different results in different domains.[5]

- **Paintings and similar visual categories:** g = 0.67. Large. This is the Kornell & Bjork territory: many categories that look alike and must be told apart.
- **Mathematics problem types:** g = 0.34. Moderate, and the classroom studies above show it holding up over months.[2][3]
- **Expository text:** no significant effect. Reading passages on different topics in a mixed order did not help.
- **Word lists:** g = −0.39. Blocking won. Mixing vocabulary from unrelated sets made learning worse.

The pattern follows the mechanism. Interleaving helps when the items are **confusable**, so that discrimination is the thing to be learned, and when each item requires a **choice of method**. A vocabulary pair has neither property: "gato means cat" does not need to be discriminated from "libro means book", and there is no method to select. Nothing is gained from mixing, and the switching costs attention that could have gone into the pairs.[5] Prose is similar: understanding a passage about volcanoes is not improved by having just read one about tariffs.

So the decision rule for any subject is a pair of questions. *Could I confuse these things with each other?* And *does using them require choosing which one applies?* Two yeses: interleave. Two noes: block, and use spacing and retrieval instead, which work regardless.

:::callout Why Dunlosky rated it only "moderate"
Dunlosky, Rawson, Marsh, Nathan & Willingham (2013) put interleaved practice in the moderate-utility tier, below retrieval practice and spacing.[7] The reason was not weak effects; it was scope. Their rating depended on how well a technique generalised across learners, materials, and kinds of test, and in 2013 interleaving had been tested mainly on maths and category learning, largely in the lab, with evidence they described as having only just begun to be explored systematically. Since then, the 2015 classroom study and the 2020 preregistered RCT have filled in much of the classroom gap for maths.[2][3] But Brunmair & Richter's boundary conditions confirm the other half of the caution: it is not a technique that helps everything.[5] Read the rating as "strong where it applies, with a narrower range than the top two", which is exactly how to use it.
:::

## What people get wrong

**"Practise one thing until it's perfect, then move on."** This is the standard advice and it describes blocked practice exactly. It feels right because perfection arrives quickly: you get to 89% in a session. The 20% a week later is invisible until the exam. The fix is not to abandon focused practice entirely; when you are first learning a method you may need a few problems in a row to get the steps down. But move to mixing much sooner than feels comfortable, and treat the drop in accuracy as the signal that discrimination is being trained, not that something went wrong.[1][6]

**"Interleaving is always better."** It is not, and a learner who mixes vocabulary lists or reading passages on this principle will lose ground. The word-list result is negative, not merely null.[5] Interleaving is a tool for confusable categories and method selection. For everything else, spacing (lesson 4) and retrieval (lesson 3) are the tools, and they are stronger and more general.

**"Interleaving is the same as spacing."** They travel together, because shuffling A, B and C problems puts gaps between the A problems, and that is part of why interleaving works. But they are different levers. Spacing is about time between sessions on the same material; interleaving is about mixing types within a session. You can space without interleaving (a vocabulary list reviewed at growing gaps) and that is the right design for vocabulary.

## Practice

:::exercise Build a shuffled set
Pick a subject you are actually studying and choose two or three things in it that you could confuse with each other and that require choosing which applies. Examples: three verb tenses, three chart types and when each is appropriate, three related legal tests, three volume or area formulae, three chord progressions, three doctrines that answer the same question differently.

1. Write ten short problems, three or four for each, where the problem does not say which type it is. "Translate: I had been waiting for an hour" is good; "Past perfect continuous: translate..." is not.
2. Shuffle them. Really shuffle: write them on separate lines, number them, and use a random order, or cut them up.
3. Do the set now, without looking at notes. For each item, first write which type it is, then solve it.
4. Check. Count how many you got wrong at the *choosing* step versus the *executing* step. The first number tells you what interleaving is teaching you.
5. Apply the boundary test: could these have been confused? Did each require a choice? If either answer was no, you have built a set that would have been better blocked and spaced. Rebuild it with items that pass.

Keep the set. In lesson 8 it goes into your weekly plan.
:::

:::

## Connections

This lesson is lesson 1 seen from a new angle. Blocked practice is the fluency illusion built into a worksheet: it raises retrieval strength, produces confident performance, and leaves storage and discrimination untouched. The painters study showed the illusion surviving direct evidence, which is why you cannot rely on how a method feels.

It also sits on top of lessons 3 and 4. Interleaved problems are retrieved, not recognised, because each one makes you pull the right method from a full set. And mixing spreads the repetitions of each type out across the session, which is spacing at small scale. The three techniques stack.

Lesson 6 adds a complication: very early in learning a method, blocked practice and worked examples reduce load in a way a novice needs. Move to mixing as soon as you can execute each method at all. Lesson 8 shows how to arrange a week so that mixing happens by default.

- **Brown, Roediger & McDaniel, *Make It Stick* (2014), chapter 3, "Mix Up Your Practice"** — the readable account of interleaving and variation, with the studies above told as stories, with the practical advice for students and teachers.
- **Rohrer, Dedrick, Hartwig & Cheung (2020), *Journal of Educational Psychology* 112, 40–52** — the preregistered RCT. Worth reading for the method as much as the result; it is a model of how to test a classroom intervention properly.
