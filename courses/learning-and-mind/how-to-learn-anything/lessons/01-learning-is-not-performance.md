You already know the feeling. You go over your notes the night before, everything looks familiar, you'd bet money you know it. Then, a week later, someone asks you a question about it and there's nothing there. Most people conclude they have a bad memory. They don't. They have a normal memory and a bad instrument for measuring it: the feeling of knowing.

This lesson gives you a better instrument. It rests on one distinction that everything else in this course depends on: **what you can do right now is a poor guide to what you will be able to do next month.** Once you can see that gap in your own habits, most popular study advice stops looking like a matter of taste and starts looking like a matter of evidence.

## The core idea: two strengths, not one

Robert Bjork and Elizabeth Bjork, who have studied memory at UCLA for decades, describe every memory as having two separate strengths (Bjork & Bjork, 2011).[1]

**Storage strength** is how deeply a memory is entrenched: how well it is woven into what you already know. On their account it does not decay; once something is well learned, it stays learned in this sense.

**Retrieval strength** is how accessible the memory is right now. It rises with recent use and falls with time and with competition from other memories.

The two can come apart in both directions. A phone number you dialled every day as a child has high storage strength and, today, low retrieval strength: you can't produce it, but if someone showed it to you once you'd have it back in seconds. A phone number you read off a screen thirty seconds ago has the opposite profile: high retrieval strength, almost no storage strength, gone by tomorrow.

Here is the problem. Every test you give yourself in the moment, every "yes, I know this" as you look over a page, measures retrieval strength. What you actually want for an exam next month, a conversation next year, or a skill for life is storage strength. And you have no direct sense of storage strength at all. You infer it from how easy things feel now.

:::callout Learning versus performance
Nicholas Soderstrom and Robert Bjork reviewed a century of research on this gap and concluded that performance during practice is an unreliable index of learning, and that some study conditions move the two in opposite directions: they make you look better today and remember less later (Soderstrom & Bjork, 2015).[2] Keep that sentence in mind for every study session you ever run.
:::

## The mechanism: why familiarity gets read as knowledge

When you reread a chapter, the second pass is easier than the first. The sentences flow, nothing surprises you, your eyes move faster. That ease is called **fluency**, and it is a real signal: the text is more familiar than it was. But fluency is produced by recognition. The words on the page trigger the memory; you never had to reconstruct the memory yourself.

Reconstructing a memory from nothing, with no page in front of you, is what builds storage strength. Recognising it while looking at it does not. Rereading, then, raises retrieval strength and fluency while leaving storage strength almost untouched. And because fluency is the only signal you get, the chapter feels learned.

This is not a personal failing. Asher Koriat and Robert Bjork showed that when people judge how well they will remember something, they lean on cues that are available at the time of judging, and those cues systematically overstate future memory; they called one version of this "foresight bias" (Koriat & Bjork, 2005).[3] A review by Bjork, John Dunlosky and Nate Kornell summarises the pattern across dozens of studies: judgements of learning track fluency and current performance, so they are wrong in a predictable direction (Bjork, Dunlosky & Kornell, 2013).[4] In one line of studies they describe, 90% of students remembered more after spaced study, yet 72% still said massed study had worked better for them.[4]

The bias has consequences beyond feeling good. Dunlosky and Katherine Rawson found that students whose self-judgements were overconfident stopped practising earlier and retained less, because they believed they were done before they were (Dunlosky & Rawson, 2012).[4] The illusion doesn't just flatter you; it tells you when to stop.

## Worked example 1: a prediction game

Before you read the results below, commit to a prediction. Write it down or say it out loud; it matters that you can't quietly revise it afterwards.

Henry Roediger and Jeffrey Karpicke ran a study at Washington University that has become the founding experiment of modern research on testing (Roediger & Karpicke, 2006).[5] Students read short prose passages. Then they did one of two things:

- **Restudy:** read the passage again.
- **Test:** close the passage and write down everything they could recall, with no feedback.

Later, everyone took a final recall test. Some took it five minutes after the study session, some two days later, some a week later.

**Your prediction:** which group scores higher at five minutes? Which scores higher at one week? And by roughly how much?

Most people predict the restudy group wins at both delays, because they saw the material twice and the test group saw it once. Now the results.

**At five minutes,** restudy won: 81% recalled, against 75% for the test group.[5] So far the intuition holds. Reading twice does produce a better score right now, and if you'd asked either group how well they knew the passage, the rereaders would have had every reason to feel more confident.

**At two days,** the order had flipped: the test group recalled 68%, the restudy group 54%.[5] That is a large effect (d = 0.95, in the units researchers use, where 0.8 is usually called large).

**At one week,** the test group recalled 56% and the restudy group 42%.[5] Restudy had lost nearly half of what it had at five minutes; the test group had lost a quarter. The one-week effect size was d = 0.83.

Read those numbers as storage and retrieval strength. Rereading pushed retrieval strength up fast, which is why it won at five minutes. But it did little for storage strength, so by a week most of what it built had drained away. Writing the passage from memory was harder and scored worse in the moment. It was also the only condition that built anything durable.

Roediger and Karpicke's second experiment sharpened this. One group studied a passage four times (study–study–study–study). Another studied it once and then recalled it three times (study–test–test–test). At one week the repeated testers recalled 61%; the repeated studiers recalled 40%.[5] And the repeated studiers were *more confident* they would remember it. The condition that felt best was the one that worked worst, by 21 percentage points.

So: at five minutes, bet on rereading. At a week, bet on retrieval. If you have to guess which one an exam, a job, or your future self will resemble, it is not the five-minute one.

## Worked example 2: the illusion survives the evidence

You might hope that seeing your own results would fix the illusion. If people were shown that method B beat method A on their own test, surely they'd switch to B. This is the wrinkle.

Nate Kornell and Robert Bjork asked people to learn the painting styles of twelve artists, six paintings each (Kornell & Bjork, 2008).[6] The task was to look at a painting they had never seen and say which of the twelve artists painted it: not memorising the paintings, but learning to recognise a style.

For some artists, the six paintings were shown one after another in a block. For others, the paintings were shuffled in among other artists' work, so a painting by one artist was followed by a painting by a different one. (Lesson 5 covers why that shuffling helps; for now the point is what the learners believed about it.)

The shuffled condition won. Participants were better at identifying new paintings by artists whose work had been mixed in with others'. Then, after the test, participants were asked which condition had helped them more. Of 72 who expressed a preference, 64 said the blocked method had worked better.[6] They had just seen their own scores say otherwise.

Why? Because blocked study *feels* better while you do it. Seeing six paintings by the same artist in a row makes the style feel obvious; you get fluent quickly. Seeing one by that artist, then one by a second, then one by a third is confusing, and confusion feels like not learning. The feeling is about retrieval strength and ease in the moment. The test result is about what stuck. When the two disagreed, 89% of people believed the feeling.

This is the important lesson of the wrinkle. You will not be able to feel your way out of the fluency illusion, and you won't reliably reason your way out of it either, even with your own results in your hand. The only defence is to decide in advance to trust delayed tests over feelings, and to build your study around things that measure storage strength: recalling from nothing, after a gap.

## What people get wrong

**"If it feels easy and fluent, I've learned it."** Ease measures retrieval strength now. Bjork and Bjork put it directly: learners mistake current retrieval strength for storage strength (Bjork & Bjork, 2011).[1] The painters study shows the feeling doesn't correct itself when the data comes in. Treat ease during study as information about the present, not a promise about the future.

**"Rereading and highlighting are studying."** They are the most common study methods, which is part of why the illusion is so widespread. When Karpicke, Andrew Butler and Roediger surveyed 177 undergraduates at a selective university, 84% listed rereading among their strategies and 55% ranked it first; only 11% mentioned self-testing (Karpicke, Butler & Roediger, 2009).[7] Dunlosky and colleagues, in the most thorough review of study techniques to date, rated rereading and highlighting as low utility, and noted that highlighting can actually hurt performance on questions that require inference, presumably because it pulls attention to isolated phrases rather than to how ideas connect (Dunlosky et al., 2013).[8] The two techniques they rated highest, practice testing and spreading study out over time, are the subject of lessons 3 and 4. Rereading does have a role: use it for what you *could not* retrieve, after you have tried.

**"Cramming works."** It does, for tomorrow. Roediger and Karpicke's five-minute result is a real result: if the test is in an hour, rereading will beat self-testing.[5] The mistake is not in cramming; it is in expecting cramming to have bought anything beyond the exam. If you want to pass Friday's quiz and never think about the material again, cram. If you want to still have it in a month, you are asking for storage strength, and cramming does not sell it. The lesson isn't "never cram"; it's "know which strength you are buying".

:::callout A difficulty is only useful if you can meet it
Retrieval was harder than rereading in Roediger and Karpicke's study, and that difficulty was the point. Bjork and Bjork call conditions like this "desirable difficulties". But they are explicit that a difficulty is desirable only if the learner can overcome it (Bjork & Bjork, 2011).[1] Trying to recall a passage you never understood in the first place is not desirable; it's just failure. Later lessons show which difficulties pay and which are simply hard.
:::

## Practice

:::exercise Do it now: audit your own habits
1. Write down the three things you most often do when you "study" or "practise". Be honest and concrete: "reread my notes", "watch the video again", "do the end-of-chapter problems with the solutions open", "explain it to my flatmate".
2. For each one, decide: does it mainly raise **retrieval strength** (it makes the material feel accessible right now), **storage strength** (it forces you to reconstruct the material from nothing, after a gap), or both? The test is simple: could you do this activity with the source material closed? If not, it is almost certainly a retrieval-strength activity.
3. Circle the one habit you would need to change first if you wanted next month's memory rather than tonight's confidence.

Now close this page, or scroll to the top so you can't see the text, and answer these five questions on paper without looking back. Don't skip this; it is the first retrieval attempt in the course, and by the argument above it is doing more for you than a fourth reading would.

1. Define storage strength and retrieval strength in one sentence each.
2. In Roediger and Karpicke's experiment, which condition won at five minutes and which won at one week? Give the four percentages if you can.
3. Why does rereading raise fluency without raising storage strength?
4. In the painters study, how many of the 72 participants with a preference chose the method that worked worse?
5. Give one example from your own life of a memory with high storage strength and low retrieval strength.

Then scroll back and check. Mark what you missed. Those items, and only those, are what you should reread.
:::

## Connections

Everything else in this course is a way of building storage strength on purpose, and of not being fooled by retrieval strength in the meantime.

- **Lesson 2** explains the machinery: why working memory is small, why experts and novices see different things in the same page, and why "understanding" a lecture in the room is another version of today's illusion.
- **Lesson 3** is the fix for the problem you just met. Retrieval practice is the strongest tool in the literature, and the five-minute-versus-one-week result is its founding evidence.
- **Lesson 4** explains why the *gap* matters, and why forgetting a little between sessions is the price of remembering a lot.
- **Lesson 5** returns to Kornell and Bjork's painters to explain what the shuffling actually does.

The reason this lesson comes first is that it changes what "did that study session work?" means. From here on, the answer is never how it felt. It is what you can do, cold, later.
