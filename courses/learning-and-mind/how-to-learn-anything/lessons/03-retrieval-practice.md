You've done this. The night before an exam, you reread the chapter, maybe twice. It goes smoothly; by the second pass the sentences feel familiar and you can almost see where each idea sits on the page. Then in the exam you reach for it and there's nothing there. Meanwhile the person next to you, who spent the evening "just doing practice questions" and complained that it felt awful, is writing steadily.

Lesson 1 explained the first half of that: familiarity was being read as knowledge. This lesson is the fix, and it's the best-supported fix in the whole literature on learning. It's also the one most people already own and use wrongly.

## The core idea

Pulling information out of memory strengthens it more than putting it in again. The act of retrieving something you've partly learned, from a question, a blank page, or a flashcard, does more for later memory than another exposure to the same material. Psychologists call this the *testing effect*, and the technique built on it *retrieval practice*.

How confident should you be in it? When Dunlosky, Rawson, Marsh, Nathan and Willingham (2013) reviewed ten common study techniques for the Association for Psychological Science, they rated each against how well it held up across learners, materials, and kinds of test. Only two came out "high utility". Practice testing was one; distributed practice, which is lesson 4, was the other. Rereading, the technique 84% of students in one elite-university sample listed and 55% ranked first, was rated low. [1]

The founding modern study is worth knowing in numbers, because it tells you exactly when retrieval wins and when it looks like it loses. Roediger and Karpicke (2006) had students read short prose passages, then either restudy a passage or take a free-recall test on it (write down everything you remember, no feedback). After five minutes, the restudy group was ahead, 81% to 75%. After two days, the tested group led, 68% to 54%. After a week, 56% to 42%. In a second experiment, students who studied a passage once and then took three recall tests remembered 61% at one week, against 40% for students who studied it four times. The four-time studiers were also more confident. [2]

Read that again with lesson 1 in mind. Restudying boosted performance today and did almost nothing for storage. Testing cost a little today and paid at a week. The students' confidence tracked the today number.

## Does it hold outside the lab?

For a long time the honest answer was "mostly lab studies with word lists and short passages". That is no longer the case.

Yang, Luo, Vadillo, Yu and Shanks (2021) meta-analysed 222 classroom studies covering 48,478 students and found an overall effect of g = 0.499 for classroom quizzing on later performance, moderated by whether feedback was given, whether the practice test matched the format of the final one, and how often the quizzing was repeated. [3] Agarwal, Nunes and Blunt (2021) reviewed 50 classroom experiments (49 effect sizes, 5,374 students) from real courses and found 57% of the effects medium or large. [4] One caveat they make themselves: only 6% of those experiments came from outside Western, industrialised countries, so the classroom evidence is broad in age and subject and narrow in geography.

:::callout One number to verify
Adesope, Trevisan and Sundararajan (2017) reported a meta-analytic effect of g = 0.61 for practice testing against all comparison conditions, with multiple-choice practice tests at 0.70 and short-answer at 0.48. That headline figure reached this course via a secondary source and has not yet been checked against the paper, so treat it as provisional until the fact-check pass confirms it. [5]
:::

## Why it works

Memory is not a filing cabinet you put things into and take things out of. When you recall something, you reconstruct it from cues, and the reconstruction changes the memory. Each successful retrieval lays down more routes to the same information: the question you were asked, the context you were in, the half-formed idea you used as a stepping stone. Next time, any of those routes will get you there.

Rereading doesn't exercise a route. The page reconstructs the idea for you and your memory only has to recognise it. Recognition is a much weaker act than recall, and recognition is what rereading trains. This is why lesson 2's "memory is the residue of thought" matters here: when you reread, what you think about is the sentence in front of you. When you retrieve, what you think about is the idea itself, its connections, and the gap where a piece is missing.

That gap is doing work. Retrieval is effortful in exactly the way lesson 1 called a desirable difficulty: it feels worse than rereading because your memory is actually being made to do something, and the feeling of difficulty is the signature of storage strength being built rather than retrieval strength being topped up.

### Getting it wrong helps, if you find out

A fear stops many people testing themselves early: "If I try to recall it and get it wrong, I'll learn the wrong answer." The evidence runs the other way, on one condition: you find out. A retrieval attempt that fails and is then corrected still strengthens later recall; the attempt is not wasted and the error does not stick. Feedback is the condition. In Yang and colleagues' classroom meta-analysis, whether feedback was given was one of the moderators of the effect. [3] The failed attempt opens a gap; the feedback fills it; the memory that results is stronger than one that never had the gap. So test before you feel ready, and always check.

### How many times, and when

One correct recall isn't much. Rawson and Dunlosky (2011) ran 533 students through schedules of retrieval to find out how much was enough. Their prescription, which they call *successive relearning*: in the first session, keep testing each item until you've recalled it correctly three times; then, in three later spaced sessions, bring each item back to one correct recall. [6] Lesson 4 is about the spacing of those later sessions. For now, hold on to the shape: retrieve to a criterion, then come back.

## Worked example: retrieval versus the "deeper" technique

Here is the study that answers the objection you're probably forming: "fine for vocabulary, but I need to *understand* my subject, not memorise it."

Karpicke and Blunt (2011) took a 276-word science text and gave undergraduates one of four ways to learn it, twenty students in each. One group studied it once. One studied it repeatedly. One group did *elaborative concept mapping*: drawing the text's ideas as a diagram of nodes and links, the kind of technique study-skills guides call "deep processing". The last group did retrieval practice: study the text, put it away, write down everything you can recall, study it again, recall again. [7]

Before you look at the result, do what they asked the students to do. Predict which group will do best on a test a week later.

The students predicted concept mapping would win and retrieval would do worst. The test a week later asked both verbatim questions (facts stated in the text) and inference questions (things you'd have to work out by connecting the facts). The retrieval group scored 0.67. The concept-mapping group scored 0.45. That's an effect size of d = 1.50, one of the largest you will see in this field. And retrieval won on the inference questions, not just the verbatim ones.

Then the wrinkle inside the wrinkle. When the final test was itself a concept map, so that students had to draw the diagram, the retrieval group *still* did better. The group that had practised drawing concept maps was beaten at drawing concept maps by the group that had practised recall.

Their second experiment made it personal. 120 students each learned two texts, one by concept mapping and one by retrieval, so every student was their own control. 84% did better on the retrieval text. Asked which method had worked better, 75% said concept mapping. [7]

Walk through why. The concept-mapping students had the text in front of them the whole time. Every node they drew was a recognition act: find the idea on the page, copy it into the diagram. The retrieval students had the text taken away and had to reconstruct not just the facts but the structure that connected them, because free recall of a passage is impossible without some structure to hang it on. The inference questions rewarded the structure. The concept-map test rewarded it too. The technique that *looked* structural was doing less structural work than the one that looked like rote.

Note what this does and doesn't establish. It doesn't say diagrams are useless; a concept map drawn from memory *is* retrieval practice. It says that the thing doing the work is the reconstruction, and any technique that lets the source do the reconstruction for you is weaker than it feels.

## A harder example: testing to check versus testing to learn

Most students already self-test. That's the trap.

Kornell and Bjork (2007) surveyed 472 UCLA students about their study habits. Among those who quizzed themselves, only 18% said they did it because testing teaches more than restudying. About 70% said they did it to find out how well they had learned the material. [8] Karpicke, Butler and Roediger (2009) asked 177 undergraduates to list their strategies: 84% listed rereading, and 11% listed self-testing at all. [9]

Why does the reason matter, if the behaviour is the same? Because it isn't the same. Watch what the two kinds of student do in the minute after the quiz.

The *checker* looks at the score. If it's high, she stops: the thermometer says done. If it's low, she goes back to the chapter and rereads it, all of it, from the start. Either way the test was a measurement, and the learning was supposed to happen somewhere else, in the rereading. She has just used the strongest technique available as a gauge for the weakest one.

The *learner* barely looks at the score. He looks at which questions he missed. Those go on a list. He rereads the sections behind those questions and nothing else. The next day he retests the list. Questions he gets right come off it; questions he misses stay. He is using the test to steer the studying and then testing again, which is the three-correct-recalls shape from Rawson and Dunlosky, run informally.

The checker's routine has retrieval in it. The learner's routine is *made* of retrieval. In Roediger and Karpicke's second experiment, the gap between a routine built on restudy and one built on retrieval was 40% versus 61% at a week, and the restudiers were the more confident.

There's a second-order cost to checking. A quiz taken right after rereading measures retrieval strength at its peak, which is the least informative moment (lesson 1 again). A checker who scores 90% the night after studying is measuring fluency and calling it knowledge. A learner who tests two days later, scores 60%, and fixes the 40% has better information and a better memory.

## What people get wrong

**"Testing is for measuring, not for learning."** This is the checker's belief, held by about 70% of the students who self-test, and it's the reason most self-testing underdelivers. A test with feedback is a learning event, and one of the two strongest we have. Treat every quiz on this site, and every question you write for yourself, as study, not as an exam.

**"Flashcards are for kids and vocabulary."** Flashcards are a delivery mechanism for retrieval; the content on them can be anything you can phrase as a prompt. "State the three conditions for X" and "Given this case, what would a court decide and why?" are both flashcards. Karpicke and Blunt's inference results show retrieval builds exactly the kind of connected understanding people think it can't. The limit isn't the format but the question: a card that only asks you to recognise a term is weak, and a card that asks you to produce, explain, or apply is strong.

**"I'll get it wrong and learn the wrong thing."** Only if you never find out. A wrong attempt that gets corrected is still a retrieval attempt, and the correction is what stays. The rule is not "don't guess" but "always check".

## Practice

:::exercise Write the test first, from memory
Take a blank page. Without opening lesson 1 or lesson 2, write ten questions that a fair examiner would ask about them: five on learning versus performance and the fluency illusion, five on working memory, chunking, and cognitive load. Make at least four of them application questions ("A student does X. What happens at a week, and why?") rather than definitions.

Then, still without looking, answer all ten.

Now open the lessons and mark yourself. Every question you got wrong, or couldn't answer, or answered vaguely, goes on a list with the section it came from. Reread only those sections. Tomorrow, retest the list. Do not reread anything that isn't on the list.

Notice two things while you do this. First, writing the questions was itself retrieval, and probably the hardest part. Second, the questions you couldn't write are the ideas you don't yet have a route to; those are the ones to keep.
:::

:::exercise Set up one retrieval routine
Pick a subject you are actually studying right now, not this course. Decide, in writing, on the routine you will use for the next two weeks:

- **After each study session**: close everything and free-recall on a blank page for five minutes. Write what you remember, in whatever order it comes, including diagrams. Then open your notes and mark the gaps.
- **Two days later**: turn the gaps into questions and answer them from memory. Questions you get right three times across sessions come off the list; the rest stay.
- **Before rereading anything**: ask what you'd get wrong if tested on it now. If you don't know, test first.

Write down where the questions will live (a notebook, a card deck, an app) and when the two-day retest happens. A routine you haven't scheduled isn't a routine.
:::

## Connections

Lesson 1 gave you the diagnosis: performance now is a poor guide to storage, and your sense of knowing tracks fluency. Retrieval practice is the direct treatment. It builds storage strength while feeling worse than rereading, which is precisely why students in every study above preferred the method that worked less.

Lesson 2 explained why reconstruction is the point: memory is the residue of thought, and retrieval makes you think about the idea rather than the page.

Lesson 4 answers the question this lesson leaves open: *when* to come back. Rawson and Dunlosky's three relearning sessions have to be spaced, and the size of the gap depends on how long you need the memory to last. Lesson 5 will add that the questions you retrieve should be mixed, not blocked. Lesson 6 turns retrieval on understanding hard material through self-explanation, which is retrieval of the "why". The course project asks you to write a self-test on day one and take it on day fourteen; you now know why the test has to be written first.
