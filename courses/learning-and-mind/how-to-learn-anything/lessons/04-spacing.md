You reviewed something yesterday and it felt solid. You'll review it again tomorrow. That plan feels responsible, and it is one of the least efficient ways to spend study time that the research has measured.

The question this lesson answers is not *whether* to review (you should) but *when*. Come back too soon and the review is easy, pleasant, and nearly worthless. Come back at the right moment and the same ten minutes does several times the work. There is a rule of thumb for finding that moment, it comes from a study with over a thousand participants, and by the end of this lesson you'll have used it on something you actually need to remember.

## The core idea

Take a fixed amount of study time, say one hour on a set of material. Spend it in one block and you have *massed* practice. Split it into two or more sessions with a gap between them and you have *spaced* (or distributed) practice. At a delayed test, spaced wins, and it wins at the same total time.

This is one of the oldest findings in experimental psychology and one of the best replicated. Cepeda, Pashler, Vul, Wixted and Rohrer (2006) reviewed 184 articles containing 317 experiments and 839 separate assessments of the effect in verbal recall tasks. Spacing reliably beat massing, and the review found something more useful than the headline: the best gap is not a fixed number. It grows with how long you need to remember the material.[1] Dunlosky and colleagues (2013), rating ten study techniques for their monograph, put distributed practice in the top tier alongside practice testing, the only two techniques to earn a "high utility" rating.[2]

### How big should the gap be?

The gap-sizing numbers come from Cepeda, Vul, Rohrer, Wixted and Pashler (2008). They ran more than 1,350 participants through a study with gaps between two learning sessions of up to 3.5 months and final tests up to a year later. Plotting the best gap against the test delay produced what they called a "temporal ridgeline": the optimal gap rises as the horizon lengthens, but not in proportion.[3]

- If you need the material in **one week**, the best gap was about **20–40%** of that: one to three days.
- If you need it in **one year**, the best gap fell to about **5–10%** of the delay: roughly three to five weeks.
- As a working rule across the range: **a gap of roughly 10–20% of the time until you need it.**

Two honest caveats. The ridgeline study used two study sessions and a single test; real study involves many reviews, and the ratio is a guide for the *first* gap rather than a formula for all of them. And nearly all of this evidence is from verbal material: word pairs, facts, vocabulary, trivia. It is a reasonable bet that the pattern holds for other kinds of learning, but the precise percentages have not been mapped for, say, a motor skill or a maths procedure.

### Spacing plus retrieval

The spacing effect was originally studied with restudy: read the material, wait, read it again. But you already know from lesson 3 that retrieval beats rereading. Combine the two and you get spaced retrieval practice, which Latimier, Peyre and Ramus (2021) examined across 29 studies. Spaced retrieval beat massed retrieval with an effect size of g = 0.74, a large effect by the field's conventions.[4] The same review found that *expanding* schedules (gaps that grow) and *uniform* schedules (gaps that stay the same) came out roughly equal, a point we'll return to under misconceptions.

Kang (2016) is the accessible policy summary of all this; if you want one short paper that puts spacing in front of a teacher or a manager, that is the one.[5]

## The mechanism: forgetting is the price

Why should a gap help? Nothing happens during the gap except forgetting, and forgetting sounds like the enemy.

It isn't. Recall lesson 1's distinction between storage strength (how entrenched a memory is) and retrieval strength (how accessible it is right now). Immediately after study, retrieval strength is high. Review then, and the memory is simply recognised: "yes, I know this." Recognition is easy, fluent, and does almost nothing to storage strength. It is the reread-and-nod experience that feels like learning and isn't.

Wait a few days and retrieval strength drops. Now the review is different. You have to reconstruct the memory, search for it, rebuild it from cues. That reconstruction is retrieval practice, the act lesson 3 showed to be the strongest tool in the literature. So a spaced review is not "the same review, later." It is a *harder* review, and the difficulty is exactly the kind Bjork called desirable: effortful reconstruction that raises storage strength.

That is why the best gap scales with the horizon. Too short, and nothing has faded, so there is nothing to reconstruct. Too long, and too much has faded: you fail to retrieve, and have to relearn from scratch, which wastes the original session. The sweet spot is where the memory is fragile but recoverable, and how fast memories get there depends on how strong they need to end up. A one-week test needs a modest memory, so a short gap gets you to fragile-but-recoverable. A one-year test needs a memory that survives a long stretch, and the wider gaps that build it are the ones that would be too wide for the one-week case.

:::callout Why cramming feels right and works badly
Cramming maximises retrieval strength for tomorrow. Every pass is fluent, the material feels owned, and the exam the next morning may go fine. But nothing forced reconstruction, so storage strength barely moved, and a month later most of it is gone. Cramming is not stupid; it is optimising for the wrong test.
:::

## Worked example: an exam in 30 days

You have an exam in 30 days and a chapter to learn today. Apply the rule.

**Step 1: the horizon.** Thirty days from first study to the moment you need it.

**Step 2: the gap.** Ten to twenty percent of 30 days is 3 to 6 days. Thirty days sits between the one-week horizon (where 20–40% was best) and the one-year horizon (5–10%), so a figure around the middle of the rule of thumb is a sensible choice. Call it 4 days.

**Step 3: lay it on the calendar.** Every review is retrieval, not rereading: close the book, produce the material, check, then reread only what you missed.

| Day | What you do |
| --- | --- |
| 0 | Learn the chapter. Write 10–15 questions on it before you close the book (you'll use these on every review). |
| 4 | First review: answer your questions from memory. Expect to miss a third. Relearn the misses. |
| 8 | Second review. Fewer misses. |
| 12 | Third review. |
| 16 | Fourth review. |
| 20 | Fifth review. |
| 24 | Sixth review. |
| 28 | Final review, two days before the exam. |
| 30 | Exam. |

Seven reviews of maybe fifteen minutes each. Compare the alternative most people run: nothing for 25 days, then five hours in the last two. The total time is similar. The spaced version has forced you to reconstruct the material seven times; the crammed version forces it once, on the day, when it is too late to fix what you can't recall.

**A variation.** If you'd rather expand the gaps (3, 7, 13, 20, 27), you can. Latimier's review says you'll do about as well. What you should not do is compress the front end into daily reviews "to make sure it's in." Daily reviews of a chapter you learned yesterday are massing in disguise.

**When you have many chapters.** The same rule applies to each, and the reviews interleave naturally: by week three you are reviewing chapter 1 for the fourth time on the same day you review chapter 4 for the first. That is the schedule a spaced-repetition app builds for you; here you have built it by hand and know why it looks the way it does.

## A harder example: a language for life

Now suppose the goal is not an exam but a language you want to keep for the rest of your life. The horizon is not 30 days; it is decades. The rule of thumb still applies (5–10% of a long horizon means gaps measured in months), but two findings from Harry Bahrick's long-term studies change what "review" means at this scale.

**The 9-year study.** Bahrick, Bahrick, Bahrick and Bahrick (1993) tracked foreign-language vocabulary relearning over nine years and compared schedules directly. Thirteen relearning sessions spaced 56 days apart produced the same long-term retention as 26 sessions spaced 14 days apart.[6] Read that again slowly: half as many sessions, each four times further apart, and the same result. The wider schedule took about two years to complete instead of one, but it bought the same memory for half the effort. At a decades-long horizon, the wide gaps that would be reckless for a 30-day exam are the efficient choice.

**Permastore.** Bahrick (1984) tested 733 people on Spanish they had learned at school, some of them fifty years earlier. Retention fell steeply for the first 3–5 years after study stopped, then flattened and held for the next 25–30 years.[7] He called the stable remainder "permastore." What matters for planning is the shape: the loss is front-loaded. What you still have five years out, you will probably have at thirty-five.

Together they give a plan that looks nothing like the exam plan:

1. **Years 1–2: build with wide gaps.** After initial learning, schedule relearning sessions at gaps of one to two months, not one to two weeks. Each session is retrieval: produce the vocabulary and structures from memory, relearn the misses. Bahrick's 56-day schedule is a sensible template.
2. **Years 1–5: don't stop.** This is the steep part of the decay curve. Material that gets relearned on a spaced schedule during these years is the material that reaches permastore. Material that doesn't, mostly won't.
3. **After year 5: maintain lightly.** What has survived is stable. Occasional use will keep it; you no longer need the deliberate schedule for material that has already made it through.

The wrinkle, then, is that the exam learner and the lifelong learner are using the same rule, and getting opposite-looking advice: review every four days, or review every eight weeks. Neither is "the right gap." The horizon sets the gap.

:::callout What "I've forgotten it all" usually means
Adults who say they've lost their school French usually haven't lost what reached permastore; they've lost retrieval strength for it. Bahrick's curves are why. If you learned it properly, and especially if you relearned it over the first few years, more is there than it feels like.
:::

## What people get wrong

**"Cramming is efficient."** It is efficient at one thing: passing a test within a day of studying. Lesson 1 gave you the numbers from Roediger and Karpicke (2006). At five minutes, restudying beat testing, 81% to 75%. At one week, testing won, 56% to 42%.[8] You can now see that result as a spacing result as well as a retrieval result: the group that did worse at a week had done a massed, fluent, recognition-based session and paid for it later. If you need the material past tomorrow, cramming costs more than it saves, because you will have to learn it again.

**"I forgot it, so the studying was wasted."** This is the mistake that makes people abandon spacing. You review after four days, miss a third of the items, and conclude the first session failed. It didn't. The forgetting is what made this review count. A review where you miss nothing has taught you very little; a review where you miss a third and reconstruct it has done the work. The measure of a session is not how it felt on the day but what survives to the delayed test, which is the lesson-1 principle again in a new setting. Keep going.

**"The gaps have to expand or it doesn't work."** Expanding schedules (1 day, 3 days, 7, 14, 30) are the default in most apps and are often described as essential. Latimier, Peyre and Ramus (2021) found expanding and uniform schedules roughly equal.[4] Expanding schedules are convenient, because they let you review new material often and old material rarely, and the site's Review page uses one for that reason. But if a fixed gap suits your calendar, use it. The effect comes from the presence of gaps and from retrieving at each review, not from the shape of the sequence.

**"Spacing means spreading out the reading."** Spacing a technique that doesn't work (rereading, highlighting) produces spaced not-working. Latimier's g = 0.74 is for spaced *retrieval*. Every review in your schedule begins with the book closed.

## Practice

:::exercise Do it now: one thing you must know in three months
Pick something specific you need to know in about 90 days: a set of terms for a course, the arguments of a book you're reading for a group, verb conjugations, the sections of a standard you'll be examined on.

1. Write the date you need it.
2. Apply the rule: 10–20% of 90 days is 9 to 18 days. Choose a first gap.
3. Write the review dates, uniform or expanding, up to a final review two or three days before the deadline. Five to eight reviews is typical.
4. Write down, now, the ten questions you will answer from memory at each review.
5. Put the first review date somewhere you will actually see it.

Checkable output: a dated list of reviews and a question set. If you have neither, you have a plan to make a plan, which is not the same thing.
:::

:::exercise Do it now: read the site's schedule
Open the Review page on this site and look at how it schedules the quiz questions you have already passed. The scheme is a version of the SM-2 algorithm many flashcard apps use. A question you get right comes back after 1 day, then 3 days, then the previous interval multiplied by an "ease" factor that starts at 2.5 and creeps up with each success (so roughly 8 days, then 22, then two months). A question you miss goes back to a 1-day interval and its ease drops.

Answer from this lesson: why do the gaps lengthen? Why does a miss reset the gap? Which of Cepeda's numbers does the 1–3–8–22 sequence resemble, and for what horizon? (Note also that this is an expanding schedule, and that Latimier's evidence says a uniform one would do about as well. The site expands because it's convenient, not because it's necessary.)
:::
