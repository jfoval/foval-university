Pick a paragraph you found impossible recently. A clause in a lease. A page of a statistics textbook. A block of code someone else wrote. A verse in a commentary that assumed you knew three Greek words. You read it, you reached the end, and you had nothing. Then someone who knows the field glanced at it and said "oh, that's just saying X".

That person does not have a better brain than you in any way that matters. They have the same tiny working memory. What they have that you don't is a set of pre-built bundles in long-term memory, and this lesson is about those bundles: what they are, why they make experts see differently, and how to build them on purpose. It also explains the most common way instruction and study go wrong: overloading the tiny memory before anything reaches the big one.

## Two memories, one bottleneck

The standard model in cognitive psychology, and the one cognitive load theory is built on, has two stores.[1]

**Working memory** is where you think. It holds what you are attending to right now, and it is small and brief: new information sits there for seconds unless you keep rehearsing it, and there are only a handful of slots.

**Long-term memory** is where everything you know lives, and for practical purposes it has no capacity limit. Nobody has ever run out.

Everything you learn has to pass through the small store to reach the large one. That is the bottleneck, and most of what goes wrong in studying goes wrong there.

How small is small? George Miller's 1956 paper put the limit at "seven, plus or minus two".[2] That number is famous and, for learning, misleading. Nelson Cowan reviewed the evidence in 2001 and showed that when you stop people rehearsing or regrouping items, the limit is about four.[3] Four is what you get when you are meeting material for the first time and have no tricks available. Miller's seven is what you get when people have already found ways to group.

But four *what*? Not words. Not facts. Chunks.

## What a chunk is

A chunk is anything your long-term memory already treats as a single unit. The letters F, B and I are three chunks to a child learning to read and one chunk to you. The phrase "compound interest" is two words and, if you took the finance course, one idea. A working-memory slot holds one chunk, and a chunk can be as big as your knowledge lets it be.

This is why the same four slots make novices and experts see different things. The classic demonstration is chess. William Chase and Herbert Simon, in 1973, showed players a position from a real game for a few seconds and asked them to rebuild it from memory.[4] Masters rebuilt far more of the board than beginners. Then came the part that matters: when the pieces were scattered on the board at random, the masters' advantage largely vanished. They did not have better memories. They had thousands of stored patterns (a castled king, a fianchettoed bishop, a pawn chain), so a real position was a handful of chunks to them and a random one was as many separate pieces as it was to anyone else. Herbert Simon later estimated a master's stock of chunks at roughly 50,000, a figure Gobet and Simon revisited in 1998.[5]

So expertise, at the level of memory, is having bigger chunks. Not more slots. Bigger contents per slot.

:::callout If you've done "Learning How to Learn"
Barbara Oakley's Coursera course uses "chunking" for the whole process of practising a skill until it runs smoothly. That is a fine everyday meaning, but it is looser than the one used here. Chase and Simon's chunk is a specific thing: a pattern stored in long-term memory that working memory can treat as one item. Keep the narrow meaning in mind for this lesson; it is what the cognitive load rules below depend on.[6]
:::

## Memory is the residue of thought

If chunks are built in long-term memory, how do things get there? Not by exposure. Daniel Willingham's summary of decades of memory research is one sentence: "memory is the residue of thought".[7] You remember what you thought about, and roughly in proportion to how you thought about it.

The consequence is uncomfortable. You can read a page while thinking about the sound of the words, or that you're on page 40 of 300, or lunch, and you will remember those things. The meaning leaves no trace because you never processed the meaning. This is why the previous lesson's rereading habit fails: rereading can be done while thinking about almost nothing, so it deposits almost nothing, while feeling fluent the whole time.

It also gives you a design rule for study. Whatever you want to remember, arrange to *think* about it, in the form you want it back. If you want to remember why something is true, spend your thinking on the why. If you want to remember how to do it, spend your thinking on doing it.

## Cognitive load: two kinds, one to cut

John Sweller's cognitive load theory turns these limits into rules for instruction and study.[1] The load on working memory during learning comes from two sources.

**Intrinsic load** comes from the material itself, specifically from what Sweller calls *element interactivity*: how many elements you have to hold at once because they only make sense together. Learning ten Spanish nouns is low interactivity; each word can be learned alone. Learning to conjugate a verb in a sentence is high interactivity; person, tense, ending and word order all interact and you cannot understand one without the others. Intrinsic load is not the enemy. It is the content. But it can be *managed*: by building up the sub-parts first so they become chunks, and by sequencing so that only a few new interacting elements arrive at once.

**Extraneous load** comes from how the material is presented, and it is pure waste. A diagram on one page and its labels on another, so you shuttle between them. A paragraph that explains a term three sentences after using it. Decorative animation, background music, a lecturer's tangent. All of it occupies slots that aren't then available for the content. Sweller's experiments on what he calls the split-attention and redundancy effects are the evidence: integrate the parts a learner has to combine, and remove what they don't need, and learning improves without changing what is taught.[1]

There used to be a third category, *germane* load, for the effort of actually building chunks. In 2010 Sweller redefined it as the share of working memory you devote to intrinsic load, rather than a separate kind, which simplifies the rule: **cut extraneous load so that the whole of your four slots goes to the intrinsic load of the thing you're learning.**[1]

## The mechanism: why prior knowledge does the work

Put the three ideas together and a mechanism appears.

Material that overflows working memory cannot be assembled into a chunk, because assembling a chunk requires holding all its parts together at once. So if a paragraph contains twelve interacting new elements, no amount of rereading will store it. There is no moment when the whole thing is in mind.

What rescues you is what you already know. If eight of those twelve elements are already chunks, the paragraph needs four slots, it fits, and now it can be built into a new, larger chunk that next week will take one slot. This is the engine of expertise, and it runs on prior knowledge. Willingham draws two conclusions from it that beginners resist.[7]

First, *factual knowledge precedes skill*. You cannot "think critically" about a text or a case or a dataset whose elements are all new to you, because critical thinking needs working memory and your working memory is full of just holding the elements. Skills look like they replace facts; in practice they run on them.

Second, *abstractions are understood through what you already know*. A new idea gets in by attaching to an old one. This is why good explanations reach for analogies and concrete cases, and why an explanation that is perfectly precise but connects to nothing you own tends to bounce off.

So when something feels impossible, the problem is usually not that it is hard in the abstract but that you are missing the chunks it assumes. Find and build those; don't stare harder.

## Worked example: one clause, two readers

Here is a sentence from a commercial contract. Read it once at normal speed.

> Notwithstanding the foregoing, the indemnifying party shall have no obligation under this Section to the extent the claim arises from the indemnified party's gross negligence or wilful misconduct.

Now count what someone meeting this for the first time has to hold in working memory. "Notwithstanding the foregoing": an unfamiliar phrase, so its two words plus a guess at what it does. "Indemnifying party" and "indemnified party": two near-identical terms that must be kept apart, each needing its own definition looked up and held. "No obligation under this Section": which Section, what obligation? "To the extent": a proportional qualifier that changes the whole meaning. "Gross negligence" and "wilful misconduct": two legal standards, each a chunk you don't have yet. That is at least ten interacting elements. Working memory holds four. The sentence does not fit, so it cannot be chunked, so you reach the full stop with nothing.

The lawyer reads the same sentence as three chunks. **Carve-out** ("notwithstanding the foregoing... to the extent"): this sentence is an exception to the rule above it, sized to the fault. **Indemnity direction** (indemnifying versus indemnified): a pattern she has seen hundreds of times, so which is which is automatic. **Fault standard** (gross negligence or wilful misconduct): the standard high bar, as opposed to ordinary negligence, and she knows immediately that it is the version that favours the indemnifying party. Three chunks, one slot to spare, and she has read it in the time it took you to find "notwithstanding".

Same sentence, same four slots. Her advantage is entirely in long-term memory. And notice what that says about learning to read contracts: not by reading more of them faster, but by building the three chunks (carve-out, indemnity direction, fault standards) one at a time, until a sentence like this fits.

Swap the domain and the structure is identical. A Python one-liner with a list comprehension, a lambda and a slice is nine elements to a beginner and three to a working programmer. A verse of Romans is a wall of clauses to a first reader and one argument with a hinge to someone who knows Paul's long conditional sentences.

## A harder example: the lecture that made sense

Now the wrinkle, because the mechanism has a trap in it.

You sit in a lecture on, say, how interest rates affect exchange rates. It makes complete sense. The lecturer builds it step by step, every step follows, you nod. The next evening you sit down with the problem set and cannot begin. You are baffled and a little alarmed: you *understood* it.

What happened is that in the lecture, the lecturer's working memory was doing the assembling. She held the chunks and put them together in the right order, out loud. Yours only had to follow one step at a time, which four slots can do. Following felt like understanding, and in the moment it was a kind of understanding. But following does not build a chunk. Alone, you have to hold all the steps together yourself to see how they connect, and you have twelve elements and four slots. The chain that was assembled for you cannot be reassembled by you.

This is the previous lesson's fluency illusion, seen from the inside of the machine. Retrieval strength was high in the room because the cues were all present; storage strength was never built because you never did the assembling.

It also explains something about notes. Notes that copy the slides don't help much, because copying needs almost no thought about the meaning (residue of thought again) and records the surface rather than the links. Notes that *reconstruct*, in which you close the slides and write, in your own words, why step three follows from step two, do help, because writing that sentence requires holding two and three together, which is the act of chunking. That is reasoning from the mechanism rather than from a single study, and the note-taking literature is messier than the slogans suggest, but the direction matches everything else here: the thinking you do is the memory you get.

## What people get wrong

**"Smart people have bigger working memories."** People do differ in working memory capacity, and the differences are real, but they are a matter of a slot or so, and you cannot train the raw number very far. Chunk size, by contrast, differs between novice and expert by orders of magnitude and is entirely trainable. When someone reads a page effortlessly that defeats you, the overwhelmingly likely reason is their chunks, not their hardware. The lever you can pull is knowledge.

**"I can multitask."** Working memory is the thing you are trying to multitask with, and it has four slots. A review by May and Elder (2018) of media multitasking, meaning phones, laptops and messaging during study or class, found consistently negative associations with recall, comprehension and grades.[8] The word "consistently" is doing work there: across many studies, the relationship does not flip. Every notification that reaches working memory arrives in a slot that was holding a piece of the chunk you were building, and the chunk collapses.

**"Novices think like experts, just slower."** This appears in the misconceptions table of the consensus document written with Willingham for teacher-training programmes.[9] It is the one most likely to hurt you, because it suggests you should imitate what experts do. Experts skim, skip steps, and think in abstractions, because they have chunks for the steps and the abstractions attach to their knowledge. A novice who skims, skips and reaches for the abstraction first is running expert habits on novice memory, and overflows. Willingham is blunt: novices cannot shortcut to expert thinking; the chunks come first.[7]

**"We only use 10% of our brains."** The same table lists this one.[9] It matters here because it implies spare capacity waiting somewhere. There isn't. The constraint on learning is not unused brain; it is the four-slot bottleneck between two memories, and the only way through it is chunks.

## Practice

:::exercise Do it now
Take one page, or one screen, of something you are actually learning right now. Not this lesson; something from another course or your own work.

1. **List the chunks.** Go through the page and write down every element it assumes you can treat as a single thing: every term, formula, step, name, or move. Don't summarise; itemise.
2. **Mark each one** as *own* (you could explain it to someone without looking), *half* (you recognise it but couldn't explain it), or *new*.
3. **Count the new and half ones in the hardest paragraph.** If it is more than four, you now know why that paragraph felt impossible, and you know what to do: build those chunks separately, one at a time, before rereading the paragraph. Pick the two most-used and write a one-sentence explanation of each from memory.
4. **Cut extraneous load.** Find one explanation on the page that confused you. Rewrite it so that every term is defined before it is used, the diagram (if any) has its labels on it, and anything decorative is gone. Compare it with the original.

Keep the chunk list. Lesson 3 will turn it into a self-test.
:::

## Connections

The previous lesson established that what you can do today is a poor guide to what you'll retain, and that fluency fools you. This lesson gives the machinery underneath: fluency is working memory following along, and retention is chunks in long-term memory, and they are built by different activities.

Lesson 3 follows directly. If memory is the residue of thought, the question is which kind of thinking leaves the most residue, and the answer, which has the strongest evidence in the whole field, is reconstructing from memory rather than recognising on the page.

Lesson 6 is where cognitive load theory pays off in method. Worked examples work for novices precisely because they hand you the chunks instead of making you find them with a full working memory, and they stop working once you have the chunks, which is the expertise-reversal effect.

And from here on you have a diagnostic for any dense page: if a paragraph won't go in, count its chunks before you blame yourself.
