Abstract words feel safe and important. They are also forgettable. Readers understand and remember specifics.

## Abstract versus concrete

> **Abstract:** We are committed to improving the customer experience through enhanced service delivery.
> **Concrete:** We will answer every support email within four hours.

The first sentence could appear in any company's report. The second makes a promise you could check.

## The test

Could a reader picture it? Could they verify it? "Significant growth" fails. "Sales up 40% since March" passes. "Improve communication" fails. "A five-minute stand-up every morning" passes.

## Numbers, names, examples

-   **Numbers:** "many users" → "about 300 users a day"
-   **Names:** "a team member" → "Priya" (when appropriate)
-   **Examples:** After a general claim, give one instance. "The interface confuses new users. Yesterday a tester spent three minutes looking for the save button."

## Strong verbs

Verbs carry the action. Weak verbs (is, has, does, makes, gets) plus abstract nouns produce fog. Swap in a verb that shows what happened.

"The new process has a positive impact on turnaround" → "The new process halves turnaround."

## Jargon

Technical terms are fine when your reader shares them. They fail when used to sound impressive or to avoid committing. "Leverage synergies going forward" means nothing. If you can't say it plainly, you may not know what you mean yet.

:::exercise Rewrite
Make each concrete: "The project faced some challenges." "Our product offers superior performance." "Employees will receive training."
:::
