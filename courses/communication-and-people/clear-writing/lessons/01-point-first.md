Readers are busy. They decide in the first sentence whether to keep reading. So the first sentence should tell them the one thing you most need them to know.

## Before and after

> **Before:** Following our discussion last week and having reviewed the figures from Q2 as well as the projections that finance circulated, and taking into account the feedback from the regional teams, I wanted to reach out regarding the launch date.
> **After:** We should move the launch from May to July. Here's why.

The first version makes the reader wade through five clauses of context before learning anything. The second gives the decision and promises the reasons. The context can come after, for readers who want it.

## The upside-down pyramid

Journalists write the most important information first, then supporting detail, then background. A reader who stops after one paragraph still gets the essentials. Use the same shape for emails, reports, and memos:

1.  **The point:** your conclusion, request, or news.
2.  **The reasons:** two or three, briefly.
3.  **The details:** evidence, background, caveats.

## Write the last sentence first

If you don't know what your point is, you can't lead with it. Before drafting, finish this sentence: "The one thing I want the reader to know or do is \_\_\_\_\_\_." That's your opening line, or close to it.

## Subject lines and titles

A subject line is a point in miniature. "Update" says nothing. "Launch moving to July: need your sign-off by Friday" tells the reader what it's about and what's expected.

:::callout But isn't that abrupt?
Leading with the point is not rude. Making someone read 80 words to find out what you want is. You can be warm and direct at the same time.
:::

:::exercise Rewrite
Take the last email you sent that was longer than five sentences. Rewrite it so the first sentence contains the request or conclusion. Notice how much of the rest you can cut.
:::
