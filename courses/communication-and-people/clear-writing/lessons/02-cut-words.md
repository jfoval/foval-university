Most first drafts are 30% longer than they need to be. The extra words aren't wrong; they're just idle. Every idle word makes the reader work a little harder for nothing.

## The usual suspects

| Padded | Plain |
| --- | --- |
| in order to | to |
| at this point in time | now |
| due to the fact that | because |
| in the event that | if |
| a large number of | many |
| it is important to note that | (delete) |
| I think that / I believe that | (usually delete) |
| very, really, quite, basically | (usually delete) |

## Nominalisations: verbs frozen into nouns

"We conducted an analysis of" is "we analysed". "Made a decision" is "decided". "Provide assistance to" is "help". Turning verbs into nouns adds words and drains energy. Find the buried verb and let it act.

## Passive voice, used by default

"The report was written by the team" is passive. "The team wrote the report" is active: shorter, and it tells you who did it. Passive isn't forbidden; sometimes the actor is unknown or unimportant ("The window was broken overnight"). But when passive is your default, sentences bloat and responsibility hides.

## Before and after

> **Before (52 words):** It is important to note that at this point in time we are not in a position to make a determination regarding the proposal due to the fact that a number of the stakeholders have not yet provided their feedback in relation to the key issues that were raised.
> **After (17 words):** We can't decide on the proposal yet because several stakeholders haven't responded to the issues raised.

## The 20% rule

After drafting, cut 20% of the words without losing meaning. It's almost always possible. It's often possible twice.

:::exercise Rewrite
"In light of the fact that there has been a significant increase in the number of customer complaints, it has been decided that a review will be undertaken by the support team." Cut it to under 15 words.
:::
